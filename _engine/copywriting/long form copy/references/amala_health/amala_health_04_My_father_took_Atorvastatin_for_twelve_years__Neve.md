My mother said she always knew where to find him. "Follow the sawdust."

When I was growing up, he'd let me sit on a stool in the corner and watch. He'd narrate what he was doing. "See this grain? It tells you which direction to cut. You go against it, the wood fights you. You go with it, and it opens up." He had thick, strong hands. Steady. The kind of hands that made you feel safe when they held yours.

He was 60 when his doctor first prescribed Atorvastatin.

Total cholesterol of 243. LDL of 161. His doctor — Dr. Engel, a man my father trusted completely — told him the numbers needed to come down or he was heading for a cardiac event.

"Take this every night. One pill. Your numbers will come down and you'll be protected."

My father was a rule-follower. Served in the Army. Paid his taxes early. Went to church every Sunday. When a doctor told him to take a pill, he took the pill.

The numbers came down. Beautifully. Within four months, his LDL dropped to 98. Dr. Engel was thrilled. "Whatever we're doing is working, Frank. Keep it up."

Keep it up.

I watched my father's craft — the thing that defined him, the thing his hands were MADE for — get smaller. Simpler. Less precise. Month by month.

And his hands were just the beginning.

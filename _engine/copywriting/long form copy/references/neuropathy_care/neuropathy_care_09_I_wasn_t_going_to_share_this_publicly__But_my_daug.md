I wasn't going to share this publicly. But my daughter made me promise. She said if my story could stop even one woman from going through what I went through, I owed it to her to tell it.

Five months ago, my four-year-old granddaughter asked me to dance with her in the living room.

She had her little princess dress on. She was twirling. She grabbed both my hands and said, "Dance with me, Nana."

And I had to pull away. Because if she tugged me off-center — even a little — I knew my legs wouldn't catch me. We'd both go down. And at my age, going down on a hardwood floor with a child in your arms isn't something you recover from quickly.

I made up an excuse. Told her Nana's knees were hurting. She twirled away and found her mother instead.

I sat in my chair and watched my daughter do what I couldn't. And I felt something worse than pain. I felt old. Not my age — older. Like the woman I used to be had already left and nobody told me.

That's what neuropathy took from me. Not just the feeling in my feet. My ability to stand steady. To walk a straight line. To move through my own home without calculating every step like I was crossing a river on loose stones.

It didn't happen all at once.

Three and a half years ago, I noticed my feet felt strange when I got out of bed. Not painful — just muffled. Like standing on a folded towel instead of the floor.

I'd been a nurse for 38 years. Medical-surgical. I knew what peripheral neuropathy was before I knew I had it. I'd seen it in hundreds of patients. Told them exactly what to watch for.

But it's different when it's your own feet.

I mentioned it to my primary care doctor at my next visit. She looked at my chart — Type 2 diabetes for twelve years — and said, "We should keep an eye on that."

So I kept an eye on it. The way nurses do. Observing. Noting. Telling myself I'd act when it crossed a certain line.

The problem with neuropathy is that the line keeps moving.

The muffled feeling turned into real numbness. First my toes. Then the balls of my feet. Then my arches. Slowly, steadily, like someone wrapping my feet in gauze I couldn't peel off.

And with the numbness came something I should have recognized sooner.

My balance started failing.

I'd be walking through the kitchen and drift into the counter. I'd brush the wall with my shoulder in the hallway. I'd clip the doorframe turning a corner.

My daughter visited one weekend and watched me walk to the kitchen. She called the next morning: "Mom, are you doing okay? You were bumping into things yesterday."

"I'm fine," I told her. "The house is just cluttered."

The house wasn't cluttered. My feet had stopped telling my brain where the floor was.

I went back to my doctor. This time she ordered everything — nerve conduction test, bloodwork, A1C, EMG. The results confirmed what I already knew: diabetic peripheral neuropathy, progressing.

Her first move? Gabapentin. 300 milligrams, three times a day.

I knew gabapentin. I'd administered it thousands of times. I knew the side effect profile by heart. But when your own doctor prescribes it for your own body, you take it. Because you want to believe it'll be different for you.

The first two weeks, the strange sensations in my feet dulled slightly. Maybe 20 percent.

But by week three, the nurse in me started seeing what the patient in me didn't want to admit.

I couldn't find words mid-sentence. I gained 12 pounds in seven weeks without changing a single meal. And the drowsiness — I spent 38 years working twelve-hour shifts and doubles. I have never been a woman who can't stay awake. But on gabapentin, I was nodding off at 2 in the afternoon. My granddaughter would talk to me and I'd realize I'd missed everything she said.

I knew these side effects. I'd documented them on patient charts hundreds of times. I'd told patients "these should improve with time."

They didn't improve. They got worse.

I told my doctor. She increased the dose. 600 milligrams. Then 900. Then she added Cymbalta.

The Cymbalta made me nauseated for three weeks. And it affected my blood sugar control — a medication prescribed for a complication of diabetes was making my diabetes harder to manage.

I was taking medication for a problem caused by high blood sugar that was making my blood sugar worse.

After 38 years of nursing, I know what bad medicine looks like. This was bad medicine. But I didn't have a better option. So I kept taking the pills. And through all of it — the dose increases, the Cymbalta, the side effects — my balance never improved. Not once. Because gabapentin blocks pain signals. It doesn't restore stability.

Over the next two years, I tried everything I came across. And I came across a lot — because when you're a retired nurse, everyone sends you things.

Compression socks. Three brands. Nothing. Alpha-lipoic acid. Four months. Nothing. A TENS unit every night for two months. Nothing. Capsaicin cream that burned my skin raw. Special orthotic insoles — $195 — that the podiatrist said would improve my proprioception. They didn't. A grounding mat from a Facebook ad. $89. Pseudoscience dressed up in medical language. I knew better. I bought it anyway. That's how desperate I was.

I kept a detailed log. Nurse habits die hard. Product name. Date started. Cost. Outcome at 30, 60, and 90 days.

$3,100 in two years. Twelve different products. Not a single one made me steadier on my feet. Not one made the floor feel solid again. Because none of them were designed to restore balance. They were designed to mask pain. And pain wasn't what was making me fall.

And the whole time I was tracking failures, the neuropathy was progressing.

The numbness climbed past my ankles. My feet became strangers — attached to my body but no longer reporting to my brain. The proprioceptive feedback that tells you where you are in space, whether you're leaning, whether the ground is level — gone. And without it, every step became a calculation.

I started planning routes through my own house.

I stopped going to the grocery store alone — because I couldn't walk the aisles without leaning on the cart like it was a walker.

I stopped volunteering at the food pantry. Stopped taking my granddaughter to the park. Stopped walking the dog. Stopped everything that required me to trust my own legs.

My world shrank to the distance between something I could hold onto and something else I could hold onto.

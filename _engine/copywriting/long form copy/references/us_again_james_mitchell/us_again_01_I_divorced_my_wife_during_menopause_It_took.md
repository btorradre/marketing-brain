I divorced my wife during menopause. It took me 2 years to realize it was the biggest mistake of my life.

I want to be clear about something before I tell you this story.

I was not a bad husband.

I worked. I provided. I showed up. For 21 years I did what a man is supposed to do. I was not out drinking. I was not chasing other women. I came home every night to a wife who, somewhere around year 18, stopped being the woman I married.

No warmth. No intimacy. Mood swings I couldn't predict and couldn't survive. Picking fights over nothing. Pulling away every time I got close. I would come home after a long day and the house felt like a place she tolerated me in. Not a home. A place.

I told myself I had been patient long enough.

I told myself a man can only take so much.

I filed for divorce on a Tuesday morning in March. My wife cried. My kids looked at me like I had burned the house down. I told myself they would understand eventually.

They didn't. And neither did I. Not yet.

The first few months after the divorce were honestly not bad.

I am not going to pretend otherwise.

The apartment was mine. The evenings were quiet in a way that felt like relief after years of walking on eggshells. No mood swings to navigate. No coldness to decode. I slept without tension for the first time in years. I told myself this was what I had needed. Space. Peace. A life that was finally mine again.

I started going out more. Friends I hadn't seen in years. Weekends with no obligations attached to them. For a man who had spent three years feeling like a stranger in his own home it felt like coming up for air.

That is when I met Karen.

She was attractive. Great smile. Easy to be around. No history, no baggage, no mood swings. She laughed at my jokes and looked at me like I was worth looking at. After three years of feeling invisible in my own marriage I am not going to pretend that didn't feel good. Because it did.

For a while it felt like I had made the right call.

She moved in after about seven months. That is when things started to shift.

Slowly at first. Small things you brush off because everything is still new and you don't want to be the man who complains. But they accumulated.

I would come home after a ten hour day and there was nothing on the stove. Not occasionally. Consistently. The kitchen in a state that would have embarrassed a college student. Dishes from two days ago. Laundry sitting in the machine because she hadn't gotten around to it. The house I was paying $1,800 a month for looked like nobody lived there with any intention.

She wanted restaurants on weekends. Trips she expected me to fund. Nice things I was supposed to provide while she contributed nothing to the actual running of the home we shared.

I said something about it once. She looked at me like I was being unreasonable.

And here is the thing I did not expect.

In those moments — standing in my own kitchen ordering pizza at 9pm after a full day of work — I started thinking about my ex wife.

Not because I missed the arguments. Not because I had forgotten what the last three years of our marriage felt like. But because I started remembering the other 18 years. The ones before everything changed.

Coming home to a hot meal on the table. Clothes washed and folded without anyone having to ask. A house that felt like someone cared about it. A woman who knew how I took my coffee and made sure there was always enough of it. Small things. The kind of things you stop noticing when they are always there and cannot stop thinking about when they are gone.

I pushed the thoughts away. Told myself Karen and I were still new.

But they kept coming back. Especially on pickup days.

We had two kids. Which meant my ex wife never fully left my life.

Sundays I would pull up to the house and she would bring them out. For the first year we kept it cold and functional. The kind of exchange that gets the job done and nothing more.

But somewhere around month 14 I started noticing something.

She looked different. Lighter. Like something heavy had been lifted off her. She moved differently. Talked differently. She laughed at something our son said one Sunday afternoon and I recognized that laugh immediately. I hadn't heard it in years. I had actually forgotten it was part of her.

I asked my son casually on the drive home how mom was doing.

He said she was good. Really good actually. Back to her old self. Like the last few years hadn't even happened.

I drove the rest of the way home in silence.

Back to her old self.

I started paying closer attention at the pickups. The way she had the kids organized. The way the house looked through the open door — warm, clean, dinner already on the stove. The way she spoke to me. Calm. No edge. No coldness. After a few weeks she started making small conversation at the door. Easy. Natural.

She was back. The woman I had married was fully back.

And I was going home to an apartment I hated and a woman who couldn't find the washing machine.

That is when I started doing what I should have done three years earlier.

Research.

I wanted to understand what had actually happened to my marriage. Not the version I had told myself — that she had changed, that we had grown apart, that I had simply run out of patience. The real version.

I went deep. Medical articles. Forums. Facebook groups full of men going through exactly what I had gone through. Men describing their wives pulling away, the coldness, the mood swings, the feeling of living with a stranger. Men who were exactly where I had been two years ago.

And I found my answers.

Menopause. What it actually does to a woman — and to a marriage. Not the hot flashes version people joke about. The real version. The hormonal shifts that affect mood, emotional availability, intimacy, everything. The withdrawal that feels like rejection but has nothing to do with how she feels about you. The irritability that has a biological cause she cannot fully control. The woman who used to reach for your hand suddenly feeling lost inside her own body and unable to explain why.

And the part that hit me hardest.

The average duration is three to five years.

Two to three years.

I had been married for 21. I had survived job losses and sick parents and hard winters and lean months when we ate pasta four nights a week. I had made it through all of it side by side with this woman.

And then I quit. With two years left on the clock.

In those Facebook groups I kept seeing the same book recommended. Men saying it had changed how they understood what their wives were going through. Men saying it had saved their marriages. The book was called UsAgain by a man named James Mitchell.

I bought it and read it in two days.

And sitting there with that book I had the clearest and most painful thought I have ever had in my life.

I could have saved my marriage.

Not by becoming a different man. Not by accepting things that were genuinely wrong. But by understanding what was actually happening to my wife and having the tools to navigate it instead of making a permanent decision in the middle of something temporary.

I thought about reaching out to her. I actually considered picking up the phone and asking if there was any conversation left to have.

Then my daughter mentioned in passing that her mom had been seeing someone. Had been for a few months. The kids liked him.

I put my phone down.

The door I had been thinking about walking back through had already closed.

A few months later I was at my friend David's place. We had known each other since our twenties. He was on his second beer telling me about how done he was with his wife. The distance. The mood swings. Feeling like a roommate in his own house. Thinking seriously about his options.

He sounded exactly like me. Three years ago. The week before I filed.

I told him to put his beer down and listen.

I told him everything I just told you. The divorce. Karen. The pickups. What I found when I finally went looking for answers. All of it.

Then I told him about the book.

UsAgain is not a book that tells you to simply understand more and give more while she figures herself out. It is written for the man living inside it — confused, frustrated, running out of patience, not understanding why the woman he built a life with has seemingly disappeared. It explains what is actually happening to her. The mechanism. The timeline. What comes back and when. And how to hold your marriage together during the years in between without losing yourself in the process.

David read it. Six months later he told me things were genuinely better. That understanding alone had changed how he showed up. His wife had noticed. They were finding their way back to each other.

He still has his house. He still comes home to a warm meal. He still wakes up next to the woman he chose.

I wake up alone.

If you are reading this and you recognise anything I have described — the distance, the coldness, the mood swings, the feeling that the woman you married has gone somewhere you cannot reach her — think it over. A hundred times if you need to. Because the decision you are considering is permanent and what she is going through almost certainly is not.

I really recommend you read UsAgain. It will give you everything you need to know before making a decision you cannot take back. I wish I had found it before I made mine.

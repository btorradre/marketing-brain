Told my doctor my headaches start in my neck. He said "take Advil." Spoiler: that didn't fix anything.

It didn't fix anything because Advil doesn't reach the thing that's actually causing the headache. But I didn't know that yet. It took me almost two years, a lot of wasted money, and one very specific explanation from a physical therapist to finally understand what was happening.

And once I did the headaches stopped being a mystery.

But let me start at the beginning.

The headaches didn't show up the way I expected headaches to show up.

There was no sudden pounding. No sensitivity to light. No classic migraine aura.

It always started in the same place.

The back of my neck.

I'd feel the tension building there first. A slow tightening at the base of my skull like someone was gradually turning a dial.

Then the pressure would start creeping upward.

Over the back of my head. Around the sides. Settling behind my eyes like something heavy was pressing inward.

By the time it fully arrived the whole top half of my head felt like it was in a vice.

Not sharp. Not pulsing.

Just this relentless squeezing pressure that sat there for hours.

The first few times it happened I treated it like a normal headache.

Two Advil. Glass of water. Wait.

Sometimes it faded slightly. Sometimes it didn't. But even when the edge came off the tightness in my neck was still there underneath.

Like the headache had dimmed but the source was still running.

I didn't think much of it.

Headaches happen. Stress, dehydration, bad sleep. Any of those can trigger one.

But then the pattern started.

Every few days the same sequence.

Neck tightens. Tension climbs. Pressure settles behind my eyes.

Then every other day.

Then almost daily.

I started tracking it in my phone. Just a note with the date and time. After two weeks I looked at the list and felt a chill.

Eleven headaches in fourteen days.

Every single one started in my neck.

That's when it stopped feeling like "just headaches."

Because these weren't random.

They had a pattern. A starting point. A route they followed every single time. Up the back of the neck, over the skull, behind the eyes.

And no matter what I took. Advil, Tylenol, Excedrin. The pills only blunted the pain at the end of the chain.

They never touched the beginning.

The worst ones came in the late afternoon.

By 2 or 3 PM after sitting at my desk all day the base of my neck would be so tight it felt like a clenched fist.

And I could feel the headache forming in real time.

It would start as pressure. Then heaviness. Then this slow suffocating squeeze that made it hard to concentrate on anything.

I'd sit in meetings trying to focus while the inside of my head felt like it was being compressed.

I'd drive home gripping the steering wheel with my jaw clenched because the pressure behind my eyes made everything feel slightly too bright.

I'd get through dinner on autopilot then spend the rest of the evening on the couch with my hand pressed against the back of my neck waiting for it to ease up enough that I could think clearly again.

Some nights it followed me to bed.

I'd lie there with that heavy full skull pressure and think how is it possible that my head still hurts? I took something four hours ago.

Because the pills wore off.

But the neck tension that caused the headache was still right there.

I brought it up with my doctor.

I remember being very specific. I said "These headaches don't start in my head. They start in my neck. The tension builds there first and then it turns into a headache."

He nodded. Wrote something down.

"Tension headaches," he said. "Very common. Take Advil at the first sign. If that doesn't help we can try a prescription."

I asked about the neck.

"Probably muscular. Try stretching. Maybe see a chiropractor."

That was it.

I left the appointment holding a pamphlet about tension headaches that said essentially the same thing. Manage the pain when it arrives.

Nothing about why my neck was generating the pain in the first place.

Nothing about what was causing the tension that triggered every single headache.

Just take something when it hurts.

So I started trying to manage it myself.

Stretching every morning. Neck rolls at my desk. Heat on my shoulders during the afternoon when I could feel the tension starting to build.

I bought a cervical pillow for sleeping thinking maybe the headaches were starting overnight.

I tried magnesium supplements. Someone told me they help with muscle tension.

I booked a massage and for about two days afterward the headaches actually stopped. My neck was loose. My head was clear. I felt like a completely different person.

Then on day three the tension crept back. And by day four the headache was right there again squeezing behind my eyes like it had never left.

I tried the massage again the following week. Same result. Two good days then everything rebuilt.

I even went to a chiropractor. The adjustment gave me an afternoon of relief. That light open feeling in your neck where the pressure suddenly drops. But by the next morning the muscles had tightened right back around the adjustment and the headache cycle started over.

Between the massage appointments, the chiropractor visits, the supplements, and the pillow I'd spent more than I wanted to admit chasing something that kept coming back.

And the Advil was still in my purse. Every single day.

And that was starting to scare me too.

Because I'd started reading about what happens when you take NSAIDs like Advil too often. Stomach lining damage. Kidney strain. And the one that really got me. Something called medication overuse headache.

Basically if you take painkillers for headaches more than fifteen days a month the medication itself can start causing more headaches.

I counted. I was taking Advil at least four or five days a week.

So I was possibly making the very problem I was trying to treat even worse. And damaging my stomach in the process.

That was the moment I realized I couldn't keep doing this.

The moment I realized something deeper was going on was at my son's school play.

He had a speaking part. He'd been rehearsing for weeks. I'd promised I'd be in the front row.

I was there. Front row. Smiling.

But by the middle of the second act the headache was so heavy that I could barely focus on the stage.

The pressure behind my eyes was throbbing. My neck was rigid. I was sitting there with my jaw clenched trying to look like I was enjoying it while the inside of my head felt like it was being slowly crushed.

Afterward my son ran up to me.

"Did you see my part Mom?"

"Of course I did sweetheart. You were amazing."

But the truth is I barely remembered it. The headache had taken most of it from me.

I cried in the car on the way home. Not because of the pain.

Because my own body had stolen a moment I couldn't get back.

That's what chronic headaches do. They don't just hurt. They take things from you.

A few nights later I was up late. Another headache, couldn't sleep. And I started reading.

Not about headaches this time.

About the neck.

I found forums full of women my age describing the exact same pattern.

One woman wrote

"Neck tension leads to headaches."

Another

"I feel like I'm constantly stretching my neck but nothing really fixes it."

And one that stopped me mid scroll

"Some days it feels like my neck is carrying all the stress in my life."

I kept reading. And eventually I found something that changed how I understood everything.

A physical therapist's explanation of cervicogenic headaches. Headaches that originate in the cervical spine not the brain.

He explained that the occipital nerves run from the top of the cervical spine up through the scalp. When the muscles at the base of the skull become chronically tight or when the upper cervical joints compress those nerves get irritated. And irritated occipital nerves generate pain signals that travel up and over the skull settling behind the eyes, across the forehead, and around the temples.

The pain follows the nerve path. That's why it always starts in the same place and travels the same route.

It's not a headache that happens to come with neck tension.

It's a headache caused by neck tension.

The neck is the engine. The headache is the exhaust.

And that's why Advil never worked for more than a few hours. It was dulling the pain signal at the end of the chain while the compression at the top of my spine kept generating new ones.

Worse. Taking it every day was potentially creating its own cycle of rebound headaches on top of the originals.

He also explained something that suddenly connected everything to my age.

During perimenopause and menopause declining estrogen increases muscle stiffness and inflammation. The muscles in the neck and shoulders literally become harder to relax. Meanwhile years of forward head posture from screens and phones compress the cervical spine further.

So the muscles are tighter because of hormones. The spine is more compressed because of posture. And the combination creates exactly the kind of chronic cervical tension that generates headaches. Not occasionally but daily.

That explained why the headaches started in my late 40s. Why they got progressively worse. Why nothing I did to treat the headache ever fixed the neck.

And it explained why everything I'd tried only worked partially.

The Advil dulled the pain signal but didn't touch the compression creating it. The stretching loosened the muscles temporarily but the tightness rebuilt within hours. The massage released the tension beautifully but without addressing the spinal compression the muscles clenched right back. The chiropractor adjusted the spine but without relaxing the muscles first they pulled everything back into compression by the next day.

Each approach addressed one link in the chain.

But the chain had four links. Muscle tension, spinal compression, poor circulation, and inflammation. To actually break the cycle something needed to address all of them at the same time.

That's when I read about a multi therapy protocol used in physical therapy clinics across Scandinavia.

Instead of treating one part of the problem at a time they combined everything into a single session.

Heat to relax muscle tissue and reduce inflammation.

Vibration massage to release deep tension in the muscles surrounding the cervical spine.

Traction to gently decompress the vertebrae and relieve pressure on the nerves generating the headache signals.

Proper support to hold the neck in a neutral position while everything releases.

All working together. So the muscles relax enough for the traction to open the compressed joints while the heat and vibration help blood flow flush out the inflammation.

Clinics using this combined approach reported significantly better results for cervicogenic headaches than any single treatment alone.

I read that and thought this is the first thing I've found that treats where my headaches actually start.

A few days later buried in a forum thread about cervical tension someone mentioned a device built around that exact combination.

A small curved pillow that applied heat, vibration massage, and gentle cervical traction simultaneously.

It was called the Callixe traction therapy pillow.

I almost scrolled past.

After two years of chasing solutions my expectations were nearly zero.

But the mechanism matched the research. And for the first time a product wasn't promising to treat my headache. It was designed to treat the neck tension causing the headache.

That distinction mattered.

So I ordered one.

When it arrived it was simpler than I expected.

A small curved pillow with a single button. No straps. No complicated setup. No twenty page manual.

The fabric was soft. A dense velvety cushion material that felt more like a therapy clinic prop than a consumer gadget.

I set it on my couch that same afternoon.

I should be honest about the timing.

I used it for the first time during one of the headaches.

Not a full blown crusher but I could feel it building. That familiar tightness at the base of my skull, the pressure just starting to creep upward.

I lay back on the pillow thinking let's see if this actually does anything before it gets worse.

The warmth came first.

Slow. Gradual. Spreading into the muscles at the base of my skull. The exact spot where every headache began.

Not the blunt heat of a heating pad. Something more targeted. Like it was designed to reach the specific muscles that were generating the problem.

Then the vibration.

A deep steady pulse that settled into the tight bands along the back of my neck. Not aggressive. Not buzzy. Just this low rhythmic pressure that seemed to convince the muscles to let go instead of forcing them.

And then the stretch.

A slow gentle traction like the pillow was gradually cradling my cervical spine open creating space between the vertebrae that had been compressed all day.

You know that feeling right after a good chiropractic adjustment where the pressure behind your eyes suddenly dissolves like a valve being released in your skull? It was that feeling. Except it happened slowly and gently while I was lying on my couch. No twist. No crack. No bracing for impact.

I stayed on it for about fifteen minutes.

And somewhere around minute ten I noticed something.

The headache had stopped building.

The tension at the base of my skull, the starting point, the source, had softened. And without the tension feeding it the pressure in my head had quietly eased.

Not from a pill.

From the neck.

When I sat up my head felt clear for the first time that day.

Not medicated clear. Not dulled by Advil clear.

Actually clear. Like the compression that had been squeezing pain up into my skull had been physically released.

I sat there for a minute thinking that's what it feels like when you treat the right thing.

I used it again the next evening. Then every evening that week.

And the headache pattern. The thing that had run my life for nearly two years. It started breaking apart.

Not all at once.

But the headaches started arriving later in the day. Then less frequently. Then some days not at all.

By the end of the second week I had gone three consecutive days without a headache.

I couldn't remember the last time that had happened.

The real shift happened around week three.

I was sitting at my desk in the late afternoon. The exact time of day when the headache used to arrive like clockwork.

I noticed the tension starting to build at the base of my neck.

And then I noticed something else.

It stopped.

The tension built to a point and didn't climb.

It didn't crawl up my skull. Didn't settle behind my eyes. Didn't turn into the squeezing pressure I'd grown to expect every single afternoon.

It just sat there at a low level in my neck and went no further.

For the first time in almost two years the chain broke before the headache formed.

I sat there staring at my screen almost afraid to trust it.

But the headache never came.

My husband noticed the change before I was ready to talk about it.

We were making dinner together and he said "When's the last time you had one of your headaches?"

I had to think about it.

"Maybe six days ago?"

He raised his eyebrows. "That used to be every day."

He was right. And I hadn't even realized how much had changed until he said it out loud.

I wasn't reaching for Advil every afternoon anymore. The bottle that used to live in my purse was still in the medicine cabinet where I'd left it weeks ago.

My stomach felt better too. That low grade queasiness I hadn't even realized was a side effect had quietly disappeared.

I wasn't pressing my hand against the back of my neck in meetings. I wasn't losing evenings to that heavy full skull pressure that made me want to close my eyes and shut everything out.

The headaches hadn't completely disappeared. But they'd gone from daily to occasional. From crushing to mild. From something that controlled my schedule to something I barely thought about.

The moment I knew this was real came at my daughter's volleyball game a few weeks later.

I was in the bleachers. It was loud. Bright gym lights. The kind of environment that used to guarantee a headache by halftime.

But I sat through the entire game.

Cheering. Clapping. Actually watching.

And when my daughter looked up at me after a big play I was fully there.

Not squinting through pain. Not counting the minutes until I could leave.

Just there.

The way a mom should be.

I thought about my son's school play. The one the headache had stolen from me months earlier.

And I realized something had actually changed.

Not just the pain.

The life around the pain.

# VSL structure, pacing and voice: findings and implemented skill changes

September 9, 2026. Sources: the user's new Motilli H1/H2/H3 script; the supplied extraction prompt and Alicia Darling/IM8 summary; `_engine/copywriting/copywriting docs/nuora vsl breakdown.pdf`. The new Motilli script is preserved as the user's preferred reference, not rewritten into the earlier draft.

## Main finding

The repeatable pattern is a sequence of motivated questions: **this is happening to me → why is it happening? → why didn't the obvious approach solve it? → what would address that gap? → which option meets those requirements? → what changed in ordinary life? → where can I find it?**

The customer-discovery VSL puts that argument inside a woman's account of learning something. The narrator's reactions carry the transitions. Merely arranging “hook, mechanism, ingredients, CTA” does not reproduce the effect.

There is a recognizable family of structures, not one universal order. These two examples share a customer-discovery spine, and Motilli was intentionally developed from Nuora. IM8's supplied opening is a time-bound product-use promise, with a different authority cue. Its short summary cannot establish its complete order, timing or spoken voice. The skill should select an archetype and consistently execute it, rather than force all VSLs into a single chronology.

## B. Three recurring decisions a novice writer could miss

### 1. Reopen curiosity after the viewer recognizes the stakes

Both examples make the woman describe an ordinary private frustration before renewing the invitation to understand it. Nuora moves from clothing shame into a promise of the explanation. Motilli moves from looser jeans but continued discomfort into “what I was actually missing.” This renewed promise has a new emotional basis; it is not just another hook pasted into the middle.

Implementation: log the first promise, the added personal consequence, the second question and its payoff. Don't automatically cut the second invitation as repetitive. Do cut repeated invitations that add no new question or delay an answer already promised.

### 2. Make the woman ask the next question

In Motilli, the UMP leads to “And I thought okay,” which reinterprets fiber/MiraLAX; then she asks what helps; then the explanation creates selection criteria. Nuora's narrator asks how to stop the claimed problem, then asks about the remaining symptom after the first ingredient explanation. These responses let the writer teach without making the narrator suddenly sound like a lecturer.

Implementation: each beat gets an incoming question, one new understanding or feeling, and an outgoing question/action. Test every boundary with “Because she now understands X, she asks/does Y.” The DM/group sequence is one delivery vehicle, not a mandatory invented story.

### 3. Turn objections and outcomes into events

Nuora places its claimed processing distinction inside a practical obstacle: the raw ingredient would be inconvenient. Motilli presents DIY assembly as something the other woman tried. Both include skepticism or delayed change, then small everyday results, then a return to clothes/meals and a reason to share.

Implementation: express a relevant objection through the narrator's decision where the account supports it. Give the outcome a callback to the opening inconvenience. Do not automatically invent a first-week failure, special ratio, failed DIY experiment, shortage or recovery timeline to reproduce the pattern.

## Language, voice and tonality

The strongest sentence in the supplied Motilli opening is: “My jeans were getting looser but I was still picking outfits around how my stomach felt.” It holds the entire contradiction in one recognizable image. “My confidence was undermined despite progress” would communicate less while sounding more written.

The speech feels conversational because:

1. **Each sentence responds.** “Because I was dropping weight” explains the confusion; “And that's the bit…” interprets it; “So let me tell you…” offers the next piece. The conjunctions have jobs.
2. **The narrator owns her understanding.** “I thought,” “I asked” and “what I learned” place the lesson inside her experience. She does not become an omniscient scientific narrator for forty seconds.
3. **Ordinary objects carry emotion.** Jeans, dinner, coffee and the morning routine make consequences imaginable without naming five emotions.
4. **The rhythm is uneven in a useful way.** Longer connected thoughts do the explaining. Short lines react or name the product. Uniform punchy fragments would make it feel more like an ad.
5. **Understatement makes room for recognition.** “That sounds like nothing but I hadn't had that in months” lets the viewer supply the emotional weight.
6. **Objections arrive when the listener would have them.** Learning the ingredients creates the question of assembling them herself. Learning the product creates the question of whether it changed daily life.

“Sounding like she picked up her phone” is an effect of coherent thought, familiar language and credible narrator knowledge. Adding random “honestly,” “like,” stumbles or slang will not create it. Natural social-feed language also does not require hiding a commercial relationship or fabricating an independent customer.

The current skill already had good sentence-level advice. Its gap was operational: it did not require a full beat allocation before drafting, identify this particular story spine clearly enough, or test the narrator's reaction at every transition. More generic instructions to “be conversational” would not fix that.

## C. Reusable three-minute allocation for this archetype

This is a **proposed design**, not a measured average and not a rewrite of the user's longer reference. Use it only when a new brief asks for a three-minute version. Reserve 3 seconds for the hold and about 9 seconds for pauses: at 155 wpm, roughly **434 spoken words** remain. The slot budgets below total 434 words; actual copy must still be read and aligned.

| Beat | Time | Word budget | What to say / draft-line function | Proof placement |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Hook | 0:00–0:08 / 8s | 20 | Recognizable problem + missing explanation: “Let me tell you what I finally understood.” | Genuine visual payoff if available |
| Agitation | 0:08–0:18 / 10s | 25 | One ordinary moment disrupted by the problem | Supplied experience |
| Twist | 0:18–0:25 / 7s | 17 | The contradiction: progress in one area, same discomfort in another | Concrete lived detail |
| Renewed curiosity | 0:25–0:30 / 5s | 12 | “That was the part I hadn't understood.” | No new proof required |
| Discovery | 0:30–0:38 / 8s | 20 | How the explanation reached her | Actual source/account |
| UMP | 0:38–0:58 / 20s | 49 | Cause → physical consequence → familiar feeling | Evidence for that precise mechanism |
| Prior solutions | 0:58–1:08 / 10s | 25 | “That's when I understood what my earlier approach was doing.” | Accurate functional comparison |
| Solution question + criteria | 1:08–1:20 / 12s | 29 | “So what would actually address that part?” | Supported requirement; collapse extra message logistics |
| UMS, three components | 1:20–1:50 / 30s | 73 | Roughly 10s each: ingredient → supported action → relevant consequence | Evidence matched separately to each job |
| Selection/formulation distinction | 1:50–2:00 / 10s | 25 | Why the relevant form/combination affected her choice | Actual format/comparison evidence; omit unsupported exclusivity |
| Product | 2:00–2:06 / 6s | 14 | “That's when I found [product].” | Confirmed fit with stated criteria |
| Routine | 2:06–2:12 / 6s | 14 | Simple directions and a relevant format preference | Current directions |
| Experience and emotional callback | 2:12–2:44 / 32s | 79 | One supplied change returning to the opening activity | Real experience with honest scope |
| Guarantee + CTA | 2:44–2:57 / 13s | 32 | Actual terms and one invitation to click | Verified offer; optional true availability detail must fit here |
| End hold | 2:57–3:00 / 3s | 0 | Stable destination/CTA | No new spoken claim |

The beat allocations total **180 seconds** and the spoken budgets total **434 words**. At 155 wpm that is approximately 168 seconds of speech, plus 9 seconds for pauses and a 3-second hold. These are planning allocations; final recorded delivery must be aligned and measured. A slower voice requires fewer words, not automatic retiming.

## IM8 follow-up: second structural reference

The supplied IM8 summary was studied separately after the user's follow-up. A local search found other IM8 ad metadata, but no full transcript matching this exact four-week/four-phase example. Those unrelated ads were not substituted.

The reusable lesson is product-led system explanation: a concrete daily action and outcome window open curiosity; four named component categories present a complete-system rationale; one sachet makes the routine appear simple; the clinical outcome claim and adoption count address different doubts; the welcome kit and guarantee supply offer value and risk reversal. These are interpretations of the supplied summary, not verified efficacy findings or measured source order.

This complements Nuora's customer-discovery story. The skill must be capable of explaining a product's component jobs and combination clearly without requiring every VSL to recreate a long forum-discovery journey.

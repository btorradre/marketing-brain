## Finding

This reference keeps the same close, direct-to-camera woman in a parked-car setting visible for the entire ad. She wears navy scrubs and a navy head covering. Her face, eye contact and hand gestures carry the hook, credibility, family anecdote, chapter changes, reassurance and persuasion. The supporting visuals are small inserts over her chest, not full-screen B-roll. A wider shot holding the branded pouch appears only at 147.375–165.583 seconds; it remains the same presenter/location.

The key editorial choice is **what to leave on the person**. The claim about a grandfather suffering for years is mostly face-only (43.5–49.9s). Energy and brain-fog benefits are face-only (125.83–130.5s). Harsh growing conditions are face-only (165.58–172.54s), despite their being visually describable. This is evidence against automatically covering every noun or every problem line. The presenter can supply the human experience and authority while inserts explain otherwise invisible events.

37 supporting visual events occupy approximately 99.92 seconds, or 45.2% of this source, counting ingredient stills, illustrative science, shopping scrolls, product inserts and CTA graphics. This is an observation of this ad, not a target B-roll ratio. Original source files for individual insets are unavailable, so photographed, generated and found provenance cannot be determined from appearance alone.

## Reusable skill implications — interpretation, not a performance conclusion

1. **Choose the carrier before choosing clips.** A direct-to-camera authority/testimonial can remain visible while B-roll works as a small semantic annotation. Full-screen cutaways are an independent decision.
2. **Human distress belongs to human coverage when lived experience is the job.** In this specific reference, the presenter supplies that coverage; it does not establish a rule that agitation requires a separate stock actor. For a pain-led concept, a phone-style person doing the relevant frustrated action can serve that job. Do not insert that rule mechanically into this educator format.
3. **Use science to show a specific causal verb.** Barrier dissolves, fluid enters, shell opens, object shrinks, nutrients move. Specify before-state, action and after-state. A generic glowing organ is weaker when the sentence describes a distinct process.
4. **Match image scale to the idea.** Ingredient macro for ingredient identity; tissue close-up for barrier action; microscopic-looking view for particles/organisms; whole digestive map for location; everyday toilet for an observable sign; palm/softgels for usage; screen recording for shopping comparison.
5. **Return to the face at resets and belief statements.** Time chapter, reassurance, testimony and final ask can need unobstructed expression more than another explanatory image.
6. **Let the visual state resolve before replacing it.** The shield sequence lasts nearly five seconds because something visibly changes. Most ingredient stills need less time. Time to comprehend the action, rather than a fixed two-second cut schedule, should drive duration.
7. **Pair the alternative with the asserted consequence.** The shopping → intact shield pattern makes the objection causal rather than a random competitor screenshot. Any factual comparative claim still requires independent substantiation.
8. **Use tangible product behavior near action.** Two capsules in palm and a pouch identify what the buyer actually receives/does after a long invisible-mechanism explanation.
9. **Carry over the semantics, not the repetition.** This ad returns frequently to torso/gut imagery and ingredient dishes. The user's current variety requirement is stricter: preserve the distinction between mechanism ideas and vary scientific views/actions rather than copying this repetition.
10. **Do not confuse illustrative science with evidence.** The source alleges parasite clearance, antimicrobial effects and a precise 72-hour transformation. This visual audit verifies what is depicted and when, not whether those health claims are valid. Likewise, a scrub-clad person in a car does not verify professional credentials; microscopy-style visuals do not authenticate organisms; a testing statement without a displayed report is still a spoken claim.

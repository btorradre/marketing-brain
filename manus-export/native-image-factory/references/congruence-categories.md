# Image Congruence Categories

Every native image ad falls into one of seven congruence patterns, derived from analysis of 500+ winning native image ads across brands running 50-900+ image variants per piece of copy. The category determines HOW the image locks to the copy.

## 1. Story Prop Images

The image shows a physical object that appears in the first 1-3 lines of the copy; the object is the bridge. A leather jacket on a thrift store rack, a hand holding an open wallet behind a steering wheel, a prescription bottle with the specific drug name visible.

**Congruence mechanic:** the prop must appear in the first 1-3 lines of the copy — if it shows up in paragraph four, congruence breaks.

**Use when:** the hook opens with a concrete, photographable object.

## 2. Scene Recreation Images

The image IS a scene from the story, presented as if it were a real photograph taken in the moment. A couple on a porch shot from a doorbell camera with a timestamp; firefighters rushing a stretcher through an ER entrance; a three-panel before/crisis/aftermath sequence.

**Congruence mechanic:** the scene must match the emotional peak of the hook, not a random scene from the story.

**Use when:** the copy opens with a dramatic, narrative moment.

## 3. Narrator Identity Images

The image shows WHO the narrator is; their credential loads instantly, before the reader processes a word. A woman in scrubs in a clinical hallway; a man in a white coat behind a pharmacy counter.

**Congruence mechanic:** the narrator's visible identity must match how the copy introduces them — a mismatch (image shows a nurse, copy introduces a pharmacist) flags as inconsistent.

**Use when:** the copy's persuasive power depends on who is speaking.

## 4. Mechanism Visualization Images

REAL medical photography showing what's happening inside the body, not educational illustration. Arterial cross-sections, annotated X-rays, progressive-deterioration imaging, microscopy, visible symptom close-ups.

**Critical distinction:** if it looks like it came from a medical facility or a doctor's screen, it works; if it looks like someone created it to explain something (a drawing, a diagram, a napkin sketch), it's banned.

**Use when:** the angle involves a mechanism reframe ("it's not your cholesterol, it's oxidized LDL"). This creates "is that what's inside me?" curiosity and primes the reader to receive the mechanism education in the copy.

## 5. Narrator's World Images

The image shows the narrator's life context, not the problem or the product. A softball team photo, two men at a backyard barbecue, a young couple with their newborn — exactly what people post on Facebook.

**Congruence mechanic:** the image must belong to the narrator's world as established in the copy — it doesn't need to match the hook directly, just the narrator's identity and life context.

**Use when:** the copy's hook doesn't have a strong visual prop or crisis moment, but the narrator's world IS the hook.

## 6. Symptom/Condition Images

Close-ups of visible symptoms/conditions that stop the scroll through recognition or shock: cholesterol deposits on eyelids, jaundiced hands beside normal ones, swollen joints, skin conditions.

**Congruence mechanic:** the symptom shown must be one the avatar actually experiences or fears — the wrong symptom stops the wrong reader.

**Use when:** the avatar's problem is visible on their body.

## 7. Multi-Panel Collage Images

3-6 panels combining two or more categories above into one frame: a jacket + a handwritten note + a prescription bottle + artery imagery; a happy dinner → an ER scene → a hospital bed.

**Congruence mechanic:** every panel must correspond to something in the copy — no decorative or "dead" panels.

**Use when:** the copy has multiple distinct visual settings, characters, or narrative beats.

## Image Variant Generation Framework

The best native-image operators don't build ONE image per piece of copy — they build image categories and test systematically, running anywhere from a handful to 50-100+ image variants against a single piece of copy. The copy stays the same; the images rotate; the algorithm finds the winning pairing.

Why: the image's job is to stop the scroll; the copy's job is to convert. These are separate functions. A good artery-cross-section image can pair with multiple cholesterol-angle copy pieces because it creates the same "medical context" scroll-stop regardless of which specific narrator is telling the story.

For any piece of copy, generate images from at least three of the seven congruence categories. Not every copy piece supports all seven, but every copy piece supports at least three.

| Category | Question it answers | Works best when |
|----------|---------------------|------------------|
| Story Prop | "Is this a real person sharing something?" | Hook opens with a concrete, photographable object |
| Narrator Identity | "Can I trust who's talking?" | Copy depends on authority/credentials |
| Mechanism Visualization | "What's actually happening to me?" | Copy involves a mechanism reframe |
| Narrator's World | "Is this person like me?" | Hook is about life context, not a specific object |
| Scene Recreation | "What happened?" | Copy opens on a dramatic moment or crisis |
| Symptom/Condition | "That's what I see on my own body" | Avatar has visible, recognizable symptoms |
| Multi-Panel Collage | "Tell me the whole story at once" | Copy has multiple settings, characters, or narrative beats |

The winner isn't predictable — generate the variants and let the data decide.

## The Visual Elements Library

Building blocks that populate the categories above — usable standalone, as collage panels, or as components within one frame.

- **The Prescription Bottle** (Story Prop / Problem-Aware) — the avatar's current medication, photographed domestically, with the exact drug name/dosage from the copy visible. The single most powerful element for any health ad where the avatar distrusts their medication. Variations: hand holding the bottle in a kitchen; bottle on a nightstand by reading glasses; bottle on a car dashboard; bottle on a pharmacy counter; a pill organizer open on a bathroom vanity.
- **The Narrator's Setting** (Narrator's World) — the physical environment that identifies who is talking, empty of the narrator, creating "who is this person?" curiosity. Variations: an old-school barbershop; an independent pharmacy with wooden shelves; a woodworking shop with sawdust; an Uber dashboard at night; a kitchen table at 3 AM with a laptop; a thrift store rack; a church interior; an exam room from the patient's POV.
- **The Person** (Narrator Identity) — someone who looks like they belong in the reader's Facebook friends list, not a model. Real skin, real age, real imperfections, photographed in their actual environment, never performing emotion — caught candid, or taking a selfie the slightly-off-angle way a real person does.
- **The Clinical Mechanism** (Mechanism Visualization) — real medical photography (arterial cross-sections, annotated X-rays, endoscopy, imaging scans, lab results), arranged as single hero images or grids (3x3 is common). Photography of a real medical phenomenon, never illustration or diagram.
- **The Hospital Witness** (Scene Recreation) — someone in a hospital bed, monitors, IV lines, gown, food tray, shot from a visitor's phone-at-bed-height perspective — the "update from the hospital" photo, documentation rather than drama.
- **The Consequence** (Scene Recreation) — what happened, shown without melodrama: a person collapsed on a floor, an ambulance outside a suburban house, a grave with flowers, a wheelchair from behind, an empty workshop with untouched tools.
- **The Domestic Object** (Story Prop) — an everyday item anchoring the reader in the avatar's life: a bathroom scale, a blood pressure monitor, a heating pad on a couch, reading glasses beside a pill bottle, compression socks over a chair.
- **The Failed Attempts Graveyard** (Solution-Aware) — a bathroom counter, medicine cabinet, or kitchen counter crowded with supplements/medications/remedies that didn't work, some half-used, some expired. For readers who've already tried things.
- **The Couple/Family Anchor** (Narrator's World) — a candid photo of people together (an older couple, a parent with adult children, a care-visit moment) — warm, unposed, slightly imperfect, like an anniversary or hospital-visit photo.

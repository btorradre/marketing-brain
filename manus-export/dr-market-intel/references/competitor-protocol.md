# Competitor protocol — find the gap, never the copy

The gap between what the market wants and what category advertising currently offers is where the best-performing creative lives. This protocol locates that gap precisely enough to brief from.

## Operating rules

1. **Study the angle, not the execution.** Format, editing, and production quality are irrelevant here. The core message is the only thing that matters. A beautifully shot ad running one week is worth less than an ugly one running six months.
2. **Running time is the signal.** An ad live 90+ days is a proven winner and gets treated as evidence. Record the first-seen date on every ad, and note when the source only shows a range.
3. **Category patterns reveal category gaps.** When every competitor says the same thing, the first brand to say something different owns that position by default.
4. **Never recommend copying.** The output is differentiation, not replication.
5. **Map to awareness levels.** Most category advertising clusters at one or two levels, and that clustering shows exactly where the opening is.

## Pulling real ads

Work from live data, not category memory. Use whatever competitive ad-intelligence tools are available: an ad library, a brand-tracking platform, or manual review of competitors' live ad accounts and social pages. Useful angles of attack:

- Find the advertisers active in the category.
- Pull each one's currently running ads, and flag which ones are visibly scaling.
- Pull transcripts for any video ad worth a real teardown, and watch it scene by scene rather than working from the transcript alone — production quality and cuts often hide or reveal the actual angle.
- Check what changed recently on a brand you're already tracking (new hook, new format, new offer).
- Pull a competitor's email program if it's available — offers and sequencing show up there before they show up in paid social.

If the tool has a credit or quota limit, check it before running a large sweep across many advertisers.

Record every ad's identifier and first-seen date. An analysis that cannot be re-verified in the source library is not intelligence.

## Output sections

### Section 1 — Category angle map

For each competitor ad:

- Competitor name and ad identifier
- **Core angle**: the single idea the ad is built around, one sentence
- **Awareness level**
- **Hook type**: problem naming / failed solution / transformation / social proof / direct offer
- **Creative maturity**: fresh angle, or repeated for years?
- **Running time** and first-seen date

### Section 2 — Category patterns

- What angle does every competitor share?
- What awareness level is the entire category clustering at?
- What messaging has gone invisible through repetition?
- What customer desire is the category consistently failing to address? Answer this against real voice-of-customer research, not against intuition. A gap customers don't care about is not a gap.

### Section 3 — Gap analysis

3 to 5 clear gaps nobody is running. For each:

- What the gap is
- Why it exists (usually: the category can't make the claim, hasn't noticed the desire, or finds the angle unglamorous)
- The brief direction it points to
- One example hook that would own it

Each gap must survive the swap test before it's listed. A gap only this brand can fill is a position. A gap anyone could fill is a trend.

### Section 4 — Differentiation strategy

The single most defensible creative position for this brand:

- The position in one sentence
- The awareness level to target first
- The hook that signals the position immediately
- Why it's hard for competitors to replicate quickly

"Hard to replicate" means grounded in something structural: the brand's actual physical product facts, its price-to-quality reality, its founder, its manufacturing truth. A position built only on a phrase gets copied in three weeks.

## The gate, restated

Everything in this document is input. None of it is copy.

The output of this protocol may never become an ad that mentions, implies, or price-compares against another brand. Translate every gap into a positive claim built from the brand's own physical facts before it reaches an angle bank or a brief, and flag anything that can't survive that translation as a competitor-comparison risk.

The one carve-out is a format that IS a comparison by design (a ranked roundup, "I tested five products"). Inside it: no specific competitor is denigrated by name, every competitor is treated as genuinely excellent and given a trade-off rather than a defect, and no competitor price is quoted unless it was verified on that brand's own site.

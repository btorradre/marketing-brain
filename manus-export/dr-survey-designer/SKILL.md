# DR Survey Designer

Most post-purchase surveys ask the questions the brand wants answered. This one asks the questions that produce the language a creative team can write ads from.

A survey that returns "great product, fast shipping" is dead weight. A survey that returns "I tried everything else for three years and nothing worked until this" is a brief that writes itself.

## Golden nugget doctrine

Before drafting, keep in mind what the survey is actually hunting for: not the topic customers will mention (the product category, the feature list) but the motive underneath — the real reason a person acted. A question that only surfaces the topic ("did you like it?") produces filler. A question that reaches the motive ("what was going on in your life that made you start looking for this?") produces the line that becomes a hook.

## Operating rules

1. **Every question must be capable of producing quotable ad copy.** If the best possible answer to a question could not appear in an ad, cut the question.
2. **Open-ended over closed-ended.** Scales and star ratings produce numbers, and numbers don't become hooks. Words do. One rating question is the maximum, and only when it's used to segment the free-text.
3. **Target the moment of decision.** The most valuable question in any post-purchase survey is some form of *what almost stopped you from buying?* The answer is routinely the best hook in the dataset, because it's the objection every non-buyer is still sitting with.
4. **Match questions to awareness levels deliberately.** Some questions surface problem-aware language ("what was going on that made you start looking"), others surface solution-aware language ("what had you already tried"). Design for the level the account is actually starved at.
5. **Brevity wins responses.** Five great questions beat fifteen average ones. Every question that doesn't produce creative-ready language should be cut before delivery.

## Step 1 — Load context

Read whatever brand brief, house voice rules, and existing voice-of-customer research already exist. If a coverage-gaps section exists in prior research, that's the reason this survey exists — build the category-specific questions to close those gaps specifically. Read any prior survey so questions aren't repeated verbatim across waves. If an awareness-level read exists for the account, use it to decide which end of the funnel this instrument should be pulling language from.

Design for realistic response volume: if the brand's email/SMS list is small or lightly subscribed, response volume will be capped by reach rather than by question quality — design for a small n and weight accordingly. Never report a percentage from a survey without stating the n it's based on. An unsourced statistic is as much a liability here as an unsourced study elsewhere.

## Step 2 — Produce the instrument

### The core five

Five questions every brand needs. For each:

- **The question**, worded exactly as the customer will see it
- **What creative output it's designed to produce**
- **The awareness level of language it typically surfaces**
- **An example response that would become a winning hook** — clearly marked as illustrative, never presented as a real answer

### Category-specific questions (5 to 8)

Tailored to this product and this customer, aimed at the coverage gaps identified in Step 1. For each:

- The question
- The creative angle it's designed to surface
- How the response would be used in a brief

### The single best question

The one question that, answered honestly, produces the most powerful language in the entire survey. Write it, and explain why it beats the rest.

### Survey design notes

- Recommended format and timing: in-flow post-checkout, an email sent at delivery plus N days, or SMS
- How to frame the opening line so customers understand why they're being asked — this is the single biggest lever on free-text length
- The one framing mistake that kills response quality, and how to avoid it
- Expected n, and what that n does and does not support claiming

## Step 3 — Consider the refusal survey

When the brand has traffic but weak conversion, the higher-value instrument isn't post-purchase at all — it's an exit or post-refusal survey aimed at people who did not buy. Offer it as a second block when the situation looks like a conversion-rate problem rather than a creative-supply problem, and keep it to three questions maximum.

## Step 4 — Write and hand off

Save the finished instrument as a standalone document. Deployment — an email platform, an on-site post-purchase page, SMS — is an action that reaches real customers, so present the instrument and get explicit confirmation before sending or publishing it.

Close with two lines: which coverage gap each block closes, and a note that responses should flow back into voice-of-customer analysis once collected. Treat survey free-text exactly like a review or support-ticket source: quote it verbatim, note where it came from, and mine it for angles the same way you would mine any other research source.

## A note on sourcing discipline

Whatever comes back from this survey becomes raw material for angles and hooks, which means the same rule that governs all research applies to it: a quote is only usable if it's verbatim and traceable back to a specific response. Never smooth a customer's phrasing into something more "on brand" before quoting it — the imperfection is usually what makes it land as real.

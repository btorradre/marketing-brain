# DR Market Intel

Competitive and winning-ad intelligence. Two modes: **competitor mode** maps what the category is running, finds the white space nobody owns, and produces a differentiation position; **winner mode** reverse-engineers one of the brand's own winning ads into transferable principles and an iteration roadmap before it dies. Use when someone says "what are competitors running," "find the gap," "white space," "analyse this competitor," "why is this ad working," "document this winner," "what do we test next off this ad," "find new information for this product," "we need a new mechanism," "the market has seen everything," or hands over competitor ads or a top-performing asset. The output is always intelligence and brief direction — never competitor-comparison copy.

## Golden Nugget Doctrine

Before any verdict is produced, name the **golden nugget**: the single most emotionally loaded deep frame in the research — the real motive that makes buyers act — never the surface theme. State it in one sentence before drafting. When analyzing a reference ad or competitor set, state the nugget it's built on and whether it leads with it.

## Pick the mode

| Situation | Mode |
|---|---|
| Studying other brands, hunting white space, entering a category | **Competitor mode** |
| One of the brand's own ads is working and the learning needs capturing | **Winner mode** |
| The market is sophisticated and the account needs fresh, credible news to sell with | **New-information mode** |
| The product needs a "reason why" that restores hope in a burned-out category | **New-mechanism mode** |
| Both (a category sweep plus the brand's own top performer) | Run competitor first, then winner — the category map tells you whether the winner is winning because of the angle or in spite of it |

## How to use this

### Step 1 — Load brand context
Read the brand's existing research, house voice/brand rules, and any prior swipe files or research before starting. Read the voice-of-customer research in particular — a category gap that no customer has ever expressed a desire for is not an opportunity. Check what has already been analyzed so the same ad isn't torn down twice.

### Step 2 — Run the chosen protocol

**Competitor protocol** (find the gap, never the copy):

Operating rules:
1. **Study the angle, not the execution.** Format, editing, and production quality are irrelevant. The core message is the only thing that matters. A beautifully shot ad running one week is worth less than an ugly one running six months.
2. **Running time is the signal.** An ad live 90+ days is a proven winner — treat it as evidence. Record the first-seen date on every ad.
3. **Category patterns reveal category gaps.** When every competitor says the same thing, the first brand to say something different owns that position by default.
4. **Never recommend copying.** The output is differentiation, not replication.
5. **Map to awareness levels.** Most category advertising clusters at one or two levels, and that clustering shows exactly where the opening is.

Pull real ads via whatever competitive ad-intelligence tool is available (an ads library, a competitor-tracking platform, or manual browsing of competitors' live ad accounts and social pages). Record every ad's identifier/URL and first-seen date — an analysis that cannot be re-verified is not intelligence. For a video ad worth a real teardown, watch it scene by scene rather than working from a transcript alone.

Output sections:
- **Section 1 — Category angle map.** Per competitor ad: competitor name and identifier, core angle (one sentence), awareness level, hook type (problem naming / failed solution / transformation / social proof / direct offer), creative maturity (fresh vs. repeated for years), running time and first-seen date.
- **Section 2 — Category patterns.** What angle does every competitor share? What awareness level is the entire category clustering at? What messaging has gone invisible through repetition? What customer desire is the category consistently failing to address — answered against actual customer research, not intuition.
- **Section 3 — Gap analysis.** 3-5 clear gaps nobody is running. For each: what the gap is, why it exists (usually the category can't make the claim, hasn't noticed the desire, or finds the angle unglamorous), the brief direction it points to, and one example hook that would own it. Each gap must survive the swap test before it's listed — a gap only the brand can fill is a position; a gap anyone could fill is a trend.
- **Section 4 — Differentiation strategy.** The single most defensible creative position: the position in one sentence, the awareness level to target first, the hook that signals the position immediately, and why it's hard for competitors to replicate quickly. "Hard to replicate" means grounded in something structural — the brand's own physical product facts, its price-to-quality reality, its founder, its manufacturing truth. A position built only on a phrase gets copied in three weeks.

**Winner protocol** (make the learning outlive the ad):

Run this while the ad is still spending, not after it has died — fatigue signals are easier to name while you can still see them arriving.

Operating rules:
1. **Diagnose, don't describe.** Explain why each element works psychologically and strategically.
2. **Separate hook from angle.** The hook is the opening move; the angle is the entire idea the ad is built around. Conflating them is why iterations fail — teams rewrite the hook and think they tested a new angle.
3. **Map to one awareness level** and explain why it works there and not elsewhere.
4. **Every finding ends transferable.** "This works because [mechanism]. This means future briefs should [specific action]." A finding with no instruction is trivia.
5. **Name what could kill it.** The signs this ad is starting to die, and what to test when it does.

Gather first: the creative itself (watch it beat by beat), performance data (spend, CPA, ROAS, hook rate, hold rate, CTR), account-level frequency and delivery trend, and the angle this ad came from if it's on record. Never estimate a performance number — if the data isn't there, say so.

Output sections:
- **Section 1 — Structural diagnosis.** Hook (what it is, who it speaks to, why it stops the scroll), angle (the single core idea, one sentence), golden nugget (the deep frame it's built on, and whether the ad leads with it or buries it), awareness level and why, format (why this format serves this angle), opening loop (what question the hook creates, and when the ad answers it).
- **Section 2 — Psychological mechanics.** Which human desire or fear does this speak to at its core? The emotional journey from hook to CTA, beat by beat. What objections it handles, and how. What trust signals it uses. What makes it feel native rather than like an ad.
- **Section 3 — Language analysis.** Which specific phrases are doing the most work? Is there customer language present? Quote it and trace it to its source in the research if possible — an ad whose best line came from a real review is the strongest possible argument for funding more voice-of-customer mining. The single most powerful line, and why.
- **Section 4 — Transferable framework.** Minimum five principles, each completing: *"This works because [mechanism]. This means future briefs should [specific action]."* Write these as instructions, not observations.
- **Section 5 — Iteration roadmap.** Same hook, new format (which to test next). Same format, new hook (3 alternatives for the same angle). Same angle, new awareness level (what this ad looks like one step higher in the funnel). Fatigue signals (hook rate falling with CTR holding = the opening is worn out and the body still works, change the first three seconds only; CPM falling alongside flat spend = delivery is decaying, not the creative). The first iteration to test, named specifically.

### Step 3 — New-information and new-mechanism modes
Run these when the awareness audit shows the market has seen every standard promise — Stage 3 sophistication needs a unique mechanism, Stage 4 needs new discoveries, Stage 5 needs repositioning.

**New-information mode.** Hunt genuinely new, credible material from the last 12-24 months that competitors haven't weaponized: peer-reviewed studies, regulatory changes, emerging use cases, demographic shifts. Deliver each finding as: source with publication and date → why it's NEW (the sophistication gap) → how to use it ethically (educate, never fear-monger) → 3 curiosity-piquing hook samples.

**New-mechanism mode.** Identify 3-5 mechanisms in the actual product — delivery method, uncommon ingredient or process, contrarian approach, improved combination — that can be positioned as "the reason why" it works when everything else failed. The #1 job of a mechanism is to give the buyer NEW HOPE.

Hard rails on both modes:
- Every study, N, %, and expert must be real and verifiable before it ships. A finding you cannot source is not a finding.
- Mechanism is an ingredient inside a hook, never its own layer — these modes feed existing angles with new hooks, or reveal a new problem worth its own angle; they don't create "mechanism angles."
- Mechanism claims must be true of the actual product. No invented processes, no unverifiable process stories.
- Findings go into the market-gap record under their own heading, each with source and date, and are subject to the same translation rule below before briefing.

### Step 4 — Translate before handing off
This is the step that keeps competitor intelligence from leaking into competitor copy, and it's the step most likely to be skipped. Every gap and every finding leaves this process as a **positive claim built from the brand's own facts**, never as a comparison.

| Raw finding | Ships as |
|---|---|
| "Competitors all hide that the material frays" | The brand's own weave, hardware, and construction facts, stated plainly |
| "Nobody in the category shows the interior" | An ad built on the brand's own lining, pockets, and what actually fits |
| "Everyone is at product-aware, nobody is problem-aware" | A top-of-funnel brief at the starved level |

Anything that can't survive the translation still gets recorded, flagged as a competitor-comparison risk. It stays as intelligence and never becomes a brief.

### Step 5 — Write and hand off
Save competitor-mode output as a market-gaps document; save winner-mode output per asset, and update the source angle record with the asset ID and verdict.

Self-audit before presenting: every ad cited with an identifier and a date, every gap tested against customer research and the swap test, no finding that reads as comparison copy, no invented running time or performance number.

Close with two lines: the single most defensible position found, and the recommended next step — usually turning gaps into tagged angle records, or a full funnel-strategy rebuild if the finding is structural rather than about any one angle.

## Rules & standards

### The Law 6 gate, restated
Everything produced by this process is input. None of it is copy. Competitor intelligence may never become an ad that mentions, implies, or price-compares against another brand. Translate every gap into a positive claim built from the brand's own facts before it's used, and flag anything that can't survive that translation.

The one carve-out is a format that IS a comparison (a ranked roundup, "I tested five products"). Inside it: no brand-specific product should be denigrated by name, every competitor is treated as genuinely excellent and given a trade-off rather than a defect, and no competitor price is quoted unless verified on that brand's own site.

### House laws referenced throughout
- **No fabricated citations.** Every study, N, %, and doctor must be real and verifiable.
- **The swap test.** If a competitor's product could drop into the claim and it still reads perfectly, rewrite onto the brand's own facts.
- **The six-month test.** No angle whose relevance dies with a season or moment.
- **Voice-tell check.** No em dashes, no "not X, it's Y," no parallel repetition stacks, on any line that ships as copy.

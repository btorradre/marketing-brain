# SHARED BODY

Let me back up.

My name is Denise. I'm 54. I live in Kettering, Ohio, with my husband Gary and a fat orange cat named Perry who owns the good armchair. I spent thirty-one years as a court reporter, sitting in the corner of courtrooms writing down every word people said, exactly the way they said it. You learn two things in that chair. People lie in long sentences. And the truth usually comes out short.

Here is the shortest true thing I know.

Five years ago, on a Saturday morning in June, my big brother Danny went out to mow his backyard and didn't come back in. His wife Beth found him because the mower was still running, sitting in one spot, chewing a hole in the lawn. Heart attack. He was 54 years old and 104 days.

My phone rang at 11:40 that morning. I saw Beth's name on the screen, and I want to be careful about this part, because people say they knew, and it sounds like something you tell yourself afterward. But I stood in my kitchen with my hand flat on the counter for a full second before I answered. And what I thought, God forgive me, was: there it is. Like a date I'd been waiting on had finally shown up on the docket.

You need to know who my brother was, because it matters to everything that comes after.

Danny was five years older than me, and he was the disciplined one. He quit drinking at 40, cold, on a Tuesday, and never touched it again. He ran the Turkey Trot every Thanksgiving in a costume. He did oatmeal before oatmeal was a personality. He had a resting heart rate nurses complimented and a gym bag in his truck that actually got used.

And the word cholesterol followed him around anyway, the same way it had followed our father around before him. It came home from every checkup like a report card grade that never improved. His doctor knew his ran high, and watching it was the whole plan, because look at the guy. He mowed his own lawn at 54. He ran 10Ks. Hold onto that. It's the whole story.

You learn things about your family at funerals. What I learned at Danny's is that we had done this before. Many times.

After the service, the great aunts ran the casserole operation out of Beth's kitchen, the way the women in my family always have. I stood in the doorway with a paper cup of coffee and listened to them talk in the shorthand of women who have buried a lot of people they grew up with. Our father, at 58, when I was in my twenties. His father at 56, before I was born. A cousin at 61.

And then one of them said it, over a tuna casserole, in exactly the tone you'd use to comment on the rain.

"Well. It runs in the family."

Nobody looked up. Nobody asked what "it" was. The foil kept coming off the dishes. That's what did it to me, I think. Not the sentence. The foil. The absolute routine of it.

I didn't have to ask what "it" was either. It was the same word that followed Danny home from every checkup. Cholesterol. Our father had it. His father had it. In my family that word works like a surname. Everybody gets it handed down, and nobody talks about it, and every few years there are casseroles.

One of the men from Danny's Saturday running group stood by the coffee urn and said nobody could have seen it coming. I was standing close enough to hear it, doing arithmetic in my head. Our father at 58. His father at 56. Ten years of somebody watching Danny's number. Everybody had seen it coming. Seeing it coming is my family's religion. We just call it weather.

Two weeks after the funeral, I sat at my kitchen table with a wall calendar and a red pen, and I did a piece of arithmetic I have never said out loud to another living person until now. I worked out the exact day I would become older than my big brother ever got to be. I wrote it on a piece of notebook paper, folded it twice, and put it in my jewelry box, under his watch, the one Beth gave me after the funeral.

For five years I have known exactly where that day sits on the calendar. It has moved toward me the whole time, like a court date.

I want to show you what living with that math actually looks like, because high cholesterol plus a family like mine doesn't feel like a health problem. It feels like a countdown. And it doesn't look like fear from the outside. It looks like a normal life with three or four small locked rooms in it.

Two months after the funeral, a man named Doug sat at our dining room table selling us life insurance. He slid an illustration across the table with three columns on it, and I pointed at the biggest number on the page. Doug looked at my paperwork, then at me, and said, "Most folks your age take about half this." Gary laughed and said, "She likes to be prepared." I smiled and let that be the reason. Prepared. The real word was certain, and it was not available for purchase.

The next spring, our beagle Champ died. For a month afterward, Gary kept leaving the Humane Society page open on the computer. Puppies. He'd angle the screen toward me at breakfast. He finally said, "The house is too quiet, Deni." I said a puppy was too much work at our age, and he let it go, and he was sad about it in that quiet way he has. Here is what I never told him. A dog is a fifteen-year promise. I did not believe I was in a position to make one.

And Italy. Lord, Italy. Gary has been bringing home Rome printouts for twenty years. Hotel pictures, folded open on the counter. "April's the time," he'd say. "Before the crowds." I used to at least look at them. For the last five years I haven't even pretended. Flights were high. The dollar was weak. Something wrong with April, five Aprils in a row, because some quiet bookkeeper in the back of my head had ruled that the trip fell after the end of my lease.

My daughter thinks I'm bad with calendars. My husband thinks I'm a homebody. I have let them think it, because the true sentence was too strange to say out loud: I have not fully believed I'd be here for any of it. Not since the mower.

And here's the part I've never told anybody. The word found me too. Both inheritances did. The math, and the number.

I keep every lab printout I've ever had in a manila folder in my desk, filed by date, like exhibits. I was 43 the first time my cholesterol came back flagged. Two forty-two. My doctor said the thing they say. "A little high. Let's keep an eye on it."

So I kept an eye on it, the way my family has kept an eye on it for three generations. Which is to say, I watched it happen.

At 43 I cut the butter. At 45 I started the oatmeal, every morning since, and I don't even taste it anymore; it's a ritual, like leaving flowers at a grave. At 47 I went about as close to vegan as a woman married to Gary can go. Eighteen months. Tofu, lentils, a cookbook with a smiling bean on the cover. It dropped nine points, and I want you to sit with that math the way I had to. Eighteen months of discipline. Nine points. Then it came back up anyway, like water finding its level. At 49, the year we buried Danny, I added the walking. Five miles most mornings, on the same before-work loop he used to run. At 51, the fish oil, and a cabinet shelf that started to look like a pharmacy aisle.

This March, at 54: two hundred and eighty-nine. The highest number of my life, at the end of eleven years of the cleanest living of my life.

Meanwhile Gary eats pizza. Gary had a bratwurst at the Reds game in July, and his number is 178. He thinks that's funny. I have laughed along for eleven years, and every time, something in my chest files it away as evidence of a rigged trial.

And at every single draw, for over a decade, I have done the same thing in the chair. The tech ties the band, the needle goes in, and I hold my breath while the tube fills. As if the blood could hear me. As if being very quiet and very good could change what's already written in it.

In March, my doctor, who is a decent man, looked at eleven years of my folder and asked, very kindly, whether I was being completely honest about my diet.

I write down what people say for a living, so I can give you my answer word for word: "I have eaten oatmeal every morning for eleven years. If I were lying to you, I would have invented better numbers."

He laughed. He apologized. Then he handed me a printed sheet called Heart Healthy Eating, with a clip art heart holding a fork. I have been handed that exact sheet four times in eleven years. I was holding a piece of paper telling me to start doing what I'd been doing since my daughter was in middle school.

Then he said it. "Numbers like yours are genetic. There's honestly not much diet can do. I think it's time we talked about a statin."

And while he typed: "It runs in the family."

From him it sounded like a diagnosis. From the great aunts it sounds like weather. From inside my chest it has sounded, for five years now, like a sentence. Three words that have buried three men I loved, and in three generations, nobody has ever once finished the thought.

That same week I drove to the bakery on Far Hills Avenue and ordered a small round cake. White frosting, no name, no message. The girl at the counter had questions I didn't have good answers to. I paid cash, drove home, and hid it in the garage refrigerator behind the water softener salt, like contraband.

Now I need to tell you about the phone call, because everything that's happened since started there.

My aunt Marie is 79. She's my father's older sister, the last one left of that generation, the one who has outlived everybody she grew up with. She calls me every year around my birthday. This year, when I turned 54, the call went differently.

She asked how I was. I said fine. Then came a long pause, the kind I spent thirty-one years listening for, the pause right before somebody stops performing.

"Denise. Can I ask you something I've never asked you?"

"Sure."

"Have you done the math?"

I sat down at my kitchen table. I hadn't said a word to anyone. Five years of nobody knowing, and my father's sister found it on the first try.

"How did you know?"

"Because I did it too," she said. "After we buried your dad. Bought myself a cake the year I passed him and everything. Couldn't tell a soul what it was for."

I asked what she did on her day. The day she became older than her little brother ever got to be.

She was quiet a second. "I drove out to the cemetery with a slice of it on a paper plate. Set it on his headstone, sat in the grass in my good slacks, and told him I was sorry. And then I told him I intended to be greedy about the rest."

Greedy about the rest. Marie has been greedy about the rest for twenty years. She grows tomatoes she gives away by the grocery bag. She walks every morning with her neighbor Dot, who is younger and slower. She has sat in a folding chair at every graduation and christening this family has had since, third row, waving like a lunatic.

Then she asked me a question that knocked the air out of me.

"Do you know what Danny's cholesterol was?"

I said high. That's all I'd ever been told. High, like Dad's. High, like mine.

"Two eighty-three," Marie said. "Beth told me at the funeral. And do you know what mine was, the year I bought my cake? Two eighty-one."

I didn't say anything for a long moment. Because the printout in my folder, the one from March, says two hundred and eighty-nine.

Three of us. Same blood. Same number, give or take a few points. Danny never found out what his actually meant. Marie found out in time. And I was sitting at a kitchen table with a secret cake in the garage refrigerator, finding out right now.

Marie's has been steady for two decades. She's 79.

I asked her what she did.

"First thing I did," she said, "was get angry at the right thing. Took me most of my life to figure out what the right thing was."

Then she told me about Ray, the pharmacist who filled every prescription in her town for forty years. A year after they buried my father, Marie stood at his counter and asked him, half joking, whether it was the eggs that were killing her family.

Ray looked at her over the register and asked one question back.

"Marie, who told you cholesterol comes from food?"

She said she stood there with her mouth open. A lifetime of funerals deep, and nobody had ever asked her that.

I've spent months inside that question now, and I've come to believe it's the most important question nobody ever asked my brother.

Most of the cholesterol in your blood never touched your plate. The large majority of it, most estimates put it around 75 to 80 percent, is made by your own liver. Your body manufactures it on purpose, around the clock, whether you had the oatmeal or the bratwurst. Food contributes the small remainder.

Now add the family part. If high cholesterol runs in your family the way it runs in mine, your liver isn't just making cholesterol. It's following instructions that were written before you were born. It over-produces, like a factory with a broken thermostat. And the cleanup crew that's supposed to pull the extra back out of your blood can't keep up. Production runs hot. Clearance runs cold. The number climbs no matter what you do at the table, because the table was never where the number was coming from.

The night after Marie's call, I did what the courtroom trained me to do. I pulled the transcript. The actual research, printed out at my kitchen table, with a highlighter, my reading glasses, and eleven years of exhibits stacked beside me.

Around one in the morning I found a forum thread. A woman had written, and I'm quoting her exactly because I wrote it down: "I am like the strictest person on diet along with gym and cardio." Then she'd posted her numbers, and her numbers looked like mine. Hundreds of replies. Marathon runners with numbers like mine. Vegans with numbers like mine. A woman who described holding her breath at every blood draw, which is when I put the highlighter down, because I had believed for over a decade that I was the only person on earth who did that.

And then eleven years reorganized themselves in front of me, like a witness finally telling the story in order.

The oatmeal works in the gut, on the little slice of cholesterol you absorb from food. Eleven years of aiming at the slice. The fish oil works on a different number entirely, one that was never even my problem. The vegan months, the five-mile mornings: genuinely good for my heart, and nearly powerless against a liver that was over-producing by design. Nine points for eighteen months of tofu, because I was negotiating with the 20 percent while the 80 percent ran hot on instructions Danny and I inherited from the same place.

I didn't fail for eleven years. I was sent to the wrong war. And so was he. That's the part that put me on the floor. Danny didn't fail either. The running, the oatmeal, the quitting drinking cold on a Tuesday, all of it aimed at the slice, because the slice was all anyone ever told him about. If your folder looks like mine, your croissants were never the problem, and neither was your willpower. The game was rigged before either of us ate our first bowl of oatmeal.

I cried at my kitchen table when that landed. Relief, the strangest kind, because for the first time in five years, the number made sense, and it had nothing to do with anything I'd done wrong. And grief, the ordinary kind, because my brother died doing everything right in the wrong war, and nobody ever told him there was another front.

So the question stopped being "how do I eat cleaner than I already eat." The question became: what actually works at the production line?

That's when I found red yeast rice. Fermented rice, used in China for centuries, studied for decades, and it kept coming up as one of the only natural compounds that works at the source, in the liver, on the production side. The only battlefield a family number respects.

Then I read a sentence that nearly ended the whole thing, and I want to be honest about it, because you'll read the same sentence too.

Red yeast rice is molecularly similar to the medication my doctor wanted to prescribe. Chemically close. Same territory.

I closed the laptop. I sat there thinking about the stories, the ones you've probably heard too, the ones that were half the reason I'd been stalling on that prescription. The legs that ache going up stairs. The fog. The people who say they stopped feeling like themselves.

It took two more nights of reading, and one more call to Marie, to understand what that similarity actually means.

The similarity is the reason it works. A family number lives at the liver's production line. Anything that actually moves it, natural or prescribed, has to work there, because there is nowhere else to work. That's the whole reason a plant compound can touch a number that eleven years of oatmeal couldn't. If it worked somewhere gentler, it would work like the oatmeal did. Which is to say, barely.

But the miserable stories cluster around something specific, and it's been documented for years. That same production line in your liver makes something else: CoQ10, the fuel molecule your muscles and your brain lean on all day. Turn the line down without putting CoQ10 back, and that's where the aching legs and the fog come from. The line was making two things, and only one of them was cholesterol. The research that got the good results paired the two together for exactly that reason. Turn down the line, restock the fuel.

Most bottles don't do that. That was the first thing I learned shopping. The second thing was worse.

A lot of red yeast rice, especially the cheap bottles, carries a contaminant called citrinin. It's a byproduct of sloppy fermentation, and it's been linked in research to kidney damage. It shouldn't be in anything you swallow, and with most brands you cannot find out whether it is, because nobody tested, or nobody published the testing. I stood in a store in Centerville holding two bottles, same ingredient on both labels, and realized I had no way on earth to know what else was in either one. I put them both back.

And the third thing: dose. The research that showed real movement used right around 2,400 milligrams. The average bottle on the shelf contains 600 and calls itself the same product. A quarter of what the studies used, sold under the same name. That's how a compound that works gets a reputation for not working.

So I wrote my criteria on the back of a lab printout, like a verdict form, before I let myself look at another product. The research dose, 2,400 milligrams. Tested citrinin free, stated in writing. And CoQ10 in the same serving, 200 milligrams, so the line being turned down keeps making the fuel my body runs on.

Most products checked one line. A few checked two. Underdosed capsules with no CoQ10. CoQ10 bottles with no red yeast rice. Page after page that said nothing at all about citrinin, which I learned to read as an answer.

Lunessa was the one that checked all three. A sugar free raspberry gummy: 2,400 milligrams of red yeast rice and 200 milligrams of CoQ10 per serving, third party tested, citrinin free, and they say so in writing. I checked their numbers against my checklist twice, because checking twice is the entire personality of a court reporter.

They also had a 90 day money back guarantee, which mattered for one specific reason: 90 days is roughly one lab cycle. Either the folder would show something or it wouldn't. Either way I'd finally know, and the only thing I was risking was the waiting. I've been waiting for five years.

Two gummies a day. I'm going to tell you the truth about the timeline, because I promised myself I'd tell this straight.

Week one, nothing. Week two, nothing. I'd stand at the counter some mornings with the bottle in my hand thinking, this is a raspberry gummy, Denise, and your brother is dead, and what exactly do you think you're doing. Week four, still nothing I could have put in the folder. I told Marie I wasn't sure anything was happening.

She said something I've thought about every day since.

"You won't feel it," she said. "You'll read it."

Twelve weeks after the first gummy, I sat in the lab chair for my recheck. Same lab. Same chair. Band on, needle in, tube filling.

And I want to tell you what I did, because I didn't plan it, and I didn't notice until I was walking to the car.

I watched the tube fill. Breathing.

The results posted two days later. I opened them in the Kroger parking lot because I couldn't wait to get home.

Two hundred and forty-one. Down forty-eight points.

Still not a number a doctor frames on the wall. I want to be straight about that too. But it moved. In twelve weeks it moved more than eleven years of oatmeal and tofu and five-mile mornings ever moved it, and in the one direction my family's numbers have never gone on their own. Not in three generations. Not once.

At the follow-up, my doctor read the panel, scrolled up and read March again, and looked at me over his glasses.

"Well," he said. "Something's different."

I took out the folder and the checklist and told him everything, the way I've just told you. He listened, which I'll give him credit for. He asked to photograph my checklist with his phone. He didn't reach for the prescription pad. He said keep doing what you're doing, come back in six months.

Six months. I've been on three-month rechecks since my forties. Nobody has ever given me six months. I drove home with the windows down like a teenager.

But the number was never really the point. The number was the bars on the window.

I booked Rome. April, ten days. Twenty years of printouts, booked on a random Tuesday, actual money on actual dates that sit well past the line on my calendar, and my hands were steady the whole time. Gary read the confirmation email twice and looked at me like I'd announced I was running for mayor. There is nothing wrong with April.

And last month my daughter Kate, who is 26, asked me a question from the couch. Too casually. Without looking up from her phone.

"Mom, how old was Uncle Danny again?"

"Fifty-four," I said. "Why?"

"No reason."

No reason. Twenty-six years old, and already starting the count. I know that tone. I invented that tone.

So I sat down next to her and told her everything I've just told you. The phone call in the kitchen. The mower. The piece of notebook paper in my jewelry box under his watch. The dog we never got. The 80 percent. And the blank cake in the garage refrigerator, which is how my family found out what it was for a little earlier than I'd planned.

She cried. I didn't, until she said, "So I don't have to do the counting thing."

No. She doesn't have to do the counting thing.

She can have Danny's laugh, which she already has, and the stubborn hair, and every ridiculous Turkey Trot costume photo in the album. She is not inheriting the countdown. That stops with me.

You can keep doing the math the way I've done it for five years. Quietly. Alone. Holding your breath while the tube fills, buying the big policy, skipping the dog, finding something wrong with April.

Or you can find out what I found out at my kitchen table at one in the morning: the number came off a production line. Danny's came off the same line. So did our father's. And for the first time in three generations, somebody in this family can actually do something at the source.

# CLOSE A — for Hooks 1 and 2 (pre-crossing)

The day is almost here. I used to believe it would feel like trespassing, like standing somewhere I was never issued a ticket to be. I don't believe that anymore. Marie has had twenty years of birthdays her brother never got, and she spends them planting tomatoes and being greedy about the rest. Every year past the line is the only inheritance Danny would have wanted me to take.

So there will be candles on a blank cake. Fifty-four of them, and one extra, for the first of the years he never got. Gary will be there. Kate will be there. Beth is coming. Marie is driving down with tomatoes.

I'm going to eat cake.

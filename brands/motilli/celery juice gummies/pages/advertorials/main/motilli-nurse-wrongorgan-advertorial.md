## The Pause I've Learned to Watch For

When I take a patient's vitals, I ask the same list of questions I've asked for twenty-three years. Sleep. Appetite. Pain. And then: "Any digestive issues?"

Most people answer without thinking.

But for the past two years there's a specific pause I've learned to watch for. The patient looks at the floor. Then at me. Then decides how much of the truth she's willing to say out loud.

It's almost always a woman in her fifties. She's almost always on Ozempic, Wegovy, or Mounjaro. The weight is coming off — the bloodwork looks better than it has in a decade. And she's about to tell me something she hasn't told her husband.

The one I can't stop thinking about is a 58-year-old woman I'll call Karen. Eleven days without a bowel movement. Bloating so severe she couldn't button her pants by dinner. Sulfur burps that smelled like rotten eggs — bad enough that her grandson commented on her breath at his baseball game, in front of the whole bleacher section.

She'd given herself an enema in a gas station bathroom on a road trip because the pain was a ten out of ten and she couldn't make it to her daughter's house.

She kept a spreadsheet on her phone tracking the last time she'd gone.

And then she said the sentence I have now heard, almost word for word, from dozens of women:

**"But I've lost 31 pounds. So I just deal with it."**

---

## What the Doctor Said — and Why It Was Never Going to Work

The doctor spent nine minutes with her. I know, because I checked the chart.

He told her constipation is a common side effect. Asked about her water intake. Suggested more fiber and a daily Miralax.

She told him she'd been taking Miralax every day for three months.

He paused, typed something, and offered two options: try Linzess, or consider lowering her dose.

Karen's face went white. *Lower my dose? I've lost thirty-one pounds.*

I want to be clear about something, because it matters: I don't blame that doctor. He was doing exactly what he was trained to do. Water, fiber, an osmotic laxative, a prescription if those fail. That's the protocol. I handed out the same advice for years.

The problem isn't the doctors. The problem is that the protocol was written for a colon that's backed up.

And that is not what's happening inside a GLP-1 patient.

## The Problem Isn't Where You Think It Is

Here's what your medication is actually doing — and why I need you to understand it before you spend another dollar at the pharmacy.

GLP-1 medications slow your stomach down. On purpose. It's not a glitch — it's half of how they work. Food stays in your stomach longer, you feel full longer, the food noise goes quiet, the weight comes off. Nobody argues with the results, and nothing on this page asks you to change your dose. The medication is doing its job.

But food that sits in a slowed stomach does three things.

It ferments. Bacteria break down food that should have moved on hours ago, and that fermentation produces hydrogen sulfide — the same gas that makes rotten eggs smell. That gas rises. Those are your sulfur burps.

It dries out and compacts. That's the cement feeling under your sternum — dinner still sitting there at breakfast.

And almost nothing arrives downstream on schedule. By the time anything reaches your colon — if it reaches your colon — it's already hard and slow. Your colon isn't blocked. Most of the time it's nearly **empty**.

Think back to the highway picture if you saw my story: the slowdown is at the on-ramp, not the off-ramp. The pileup is at the top of your system.

Every product in your medicine cabinet is aimed at the bottom.

## What Actually Works at the Top of the System

After Karen's appointment — after I walked her out and watched her leave with a Linzess suggestion and nothing else — I stopped handing out advice I'd never questioned and spent three nights on PubMed finding out why it never worked.

Three things kept surfacing in the research. Not one. Three. And they only make sense together.

### 1. Motility — wake the stomach up

Your stomach pushes food on a signal from the vagus nerve. GLP-1 medications turn that signal down — that's the design. **Apigenin**, a compound concentrated in celery, kept appearing in motility research as a way to gently support that exact signal. Not override your medication. Not force anything. Turning the volume back up on a nerve the medication turned down — while the appetite effect stays intact.

### 2. Gas neutralization — end the sulfur chemistry

Even once food is moving, weeks of slow digestion mean bacteria have been producing hydrogen sulfide faster than your body can clear it. **Chlorophyllin** — a stabilized, concentrated form of chlorophyll — binds directly to hydrogen sulfide and neutralizes it before it rises as gas. It doesn't mask the smell. It disarms the molecule that makes it.

### 3. Downstream flow — soften the backlog without adding to it

Everything already sitting in the line is dry and compacted. The last thing it needs is bulk. A specific type of **low-bulk, gel-forming soluble prebiotic fiber** draws moisture into what's stuck and softens it — without adding volume to the gridlock. The exact opposite of the fiber on the pharmacy shelf.

Motility. Gas neutralization. Downstream flow.

Address all three at the same time, or nothing changes. Wake the stomach without handling the gas, and you're still chewing gum and dodging close conversations. Kill the gas without moving the stomach, and you're still counting days on a spreadsheet.

## Why You Can't Build This From the Supplement Aisle

I tried. Here's what I found standing in that aisle — and what you'll find too.

**Celery products?** The apigenin in celery juice is a trace — you'd need gallons a day to approach what the studies used. The celery-seed capsules run one or two percent, buried in the fine print. The right word on the wrong product.

**"Motility" blends?** Almost all ginger — a different lever entirely — and not one of them touches the gas.

**Chlorophyll drops?** The pretty green ones all over your feed are sold for glowy skin and "detox." They're unstable, they degrade on the shelf, and they're dosed for photos, not physiology. The form that actually binds hydrogen sulfide is chlorophyllin — stabilized and specific — and it's almost never what's in the dropper.

**Fiber?** The entire shelf is bulk — psyllium, wheat powders, the stuff every doctor recites. Behind a stomach that isn't moving, bulk packs the line tighter: it makes the exact thing you're treating worse. And the inulin "prebiotics" ferment, which makes *more* gas.

Every product has one of the three ingredients, in a counterfeit form. Not one has all three, the way the research used them.

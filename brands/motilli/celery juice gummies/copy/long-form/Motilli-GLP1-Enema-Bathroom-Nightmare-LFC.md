On Thanksgiving, while my family carved the turkey downstairs, I was locked in my daughter's upstairs bathroom giving myself an enema.

I could hear them laughing through the floor.

I ran the exhaust fan so nobody would hear anything. I'd smuggled the box in under a sweater, in a tote bag, like contraband. I am 61 years old. I taught fourth grade for thirty-one years. That is the single most humiliating sentence I have ever written.

When I came downstairs forty minutes later, my daughter asked if I was all right. I said I'd been lying down with a headache.

Add it to the pile of small lies this thing had me telling.

I need to be clear about something first: Ozempic gave me my numbers back. My A1C had been creeping for a decade and my doctor was saying words like "insulin" in a voice I didn't like. Six months in, I was down 29 pounds and my labs looked better than they had at fifty.

I would start it again tomorrow. This is not a story about quitting.

This is a story about what nobody warned me it would do to my gut. And how the fix was aimed at the wrong end of me for almost a year.

It crept up the way these things do. Two days became four. Four became six. The bloating arrived every afternoon like a shift worker. That hard knot under my ribs — I used to press on it during television, absently, the way you worry a sore tooth.

I did what a careful, responsible retired teacher does. I escalated methodically.

Prune juice. Then Miralax, one capful, then two. Then Colace. Then senna tea, which gave me cramps at three in the morning and nothing to show for them. Then magnesium citrate, the bottled kind, which works the way a fire alarm works — once, violently, at maximum volume, with no dignity attached.

Then the enemas.

I stopped going to water aerobics because I couldn't be forty minutes from a bathroom I trusted. Do you understand what I mean by a bathroom you trust? If you do, this letter is for you.

The Monday after Thanksgiving, I went back to the pharmacy for another box.

I'd started timing these trips. Mid-morning on a weekday, when the young cashier with the earbuds works the front register — the one who scans things without seeing them. Box turned label-down on the counter. Card already out. A whole quiet system for buying my shame in under ninety seconds.

The young cashier wasn't there. Dane was.

Dane has been my pharmacist for eleven years. He filled my blood pressure pills through my husband's bypass. He knows my grandchildren's names. He picked up the box, scanned it, stopped — and I watched him look at the screen, then at me, and do the math he's too good at his job not to do.

"Ruth," he said, very quietly. "This is your third box this month."

I stood there between the counter and the reading-glasses display with my face going hot. I heard myself start a sentence — "They're for—" — and there was no end to it. Nowhere for it to go.

Eleven years this man has known me, and I was about to cry over a box of enemas at 10:40 on a Monday morning.

Dane didn't make me finish. He waved the tech over to cover his register, lifted the counter gate, and said, "Come stand over here with me a second." The little consultation window at the end, where they do the flu shots. Semi-private. A chair he didn't make me take, which I was grateful for.

He just stood with me.

"You started the Ozempic in the spring," he said. Not a question — he fills it in himself. "And I'd bet my license the bathroom trouble started a month or two after."

I nodded. It was all I had.

"Okay. Can I tell you what's actually happening? Because you're the fourth woman this month standing where you're standing, and not one of you has had anybody explain it."

I nodded again.

"That medication slows your stomach down. On purpose. There's a clinical name for it — delayed gastric emptying — and it's part of the design, not a glitch. Food goes in and sits. Hours. Sometimes days."

He let that sit.

"Those burps — the ones that taste like rotten eggs?"

I stared at him. I have never said one word to another human being about the burps.

"That's fermenting, Ruth. That's the sulfur. And while it sits, it dries out and packs down hard. That's the brick you're feeling under your ribs."

He picked the box back up. Turned it over in his hand.

"Now here's the part that matters. Your colon — the place this works, the place all of it works —" he tipped the box toward the shelf behind me, at the Miralax and the senna and the fiber, my entire year of purchases standing in a row "— your colon is mostly empty. The pileup is at the top. Not the bottom."

He nodded at the shelf.

"Miralax pulls water into the colon. Nothing down there to soften. Fiber adds bulk — bulk behind a stomach that isn't moving just packs the line tighter. Senna squeezes the colon. You can squeeze an empty pipe all day. Nothing comes. And these" — the box — "rinse the last three feet of a problem that starts way up at the top. Every one of them, Ruth. Wrong end."

I looked at that shelf. A year of my life at retail price. I felt it land in the order these things actually land. Stupid first. Then robbed.

He must have watched it cross my face, because his voice changed. This next part I will be grateful for as long as I live.

"Hey. Ruth. Look at me."

"You did everything right. Every product on that shelf is exactly what the labels say to buy and exactly what half the doctors say to try. You are not broken, and you haven't been careless. You've been aimed at the wrong end. That's it. That's the whole thing. Nobody told you."

I did cry then. Not much — a grandmother's ration of it, standing at the flu-shot window. Dane handed me a tissue from behind the counter and had the decency to study his shoes while I used it.

A year of hiding this. From my family. From my doctor, mostly. From myself, if I'm honest. And it took a man at a cash register eleven minutes to hand me back my dignity.

"So what do I take?" I asked, when I could. "What actually fixes it?"

And Dane — a man whose livelihood is selling me things — said, "Nothing on my shelves. That's the honest answer. Everything in this aisle works downstream. But there's real research now on supporting the stomach directly. Motility support. It's newer than my inventory. Go home and read it."

He slid the enema box back across the counter to his own side.

"And don't buy this. Come tell me what you find instead."

I drove home. Sat at my kitchen counter at four in the afternoon — no dramatic midnight scene, just a retired teacher with a laptop and her reading glasses — and did the homework. Printed studies. Highlighter. The whole business, same as thirty-one years of lesson plans.

Everything Dane said checked out, line by line. And the reading handed me the teardown he'd been too kind to spell out: the enemas, the thing that ruined Thanksgiving, flush the one part of the system that was never the problem. Forty minutes on a bathroom floor, treating an organ that was never broken.

So what actually fixes it? That became the week's project.

Three things kept coming up.

Apigenin. A compound concentrated in celery. It supports the vagus nerve — the nerve that runs the stomach's contractions, the exact signal the medication turns down. In the motility research it shows up supporting that exact signal. Doesn't fight the medication. Just restores what it muted.

Chlorophyllin. Concentrated chlorophyll. It binds to hydrogen sulfide — the sulfur gas — and neutralizes it. The gas that had me positioning myself downwind of my own grandchildren. Disarmed at the molecule.

And a low-bulk soluble prebiotic fiber. Gel-forming. Draws moisture into what's already stuck and softens it without adding bulk. Precisely the opposite of what every well-meaning person had told me to take.

Wake the stomach. Neutralize the gas. Soften the backlog. All three together, or you're treating the wrong end again.

So I started checking ingredients the way I used to check a worksheet against the answer key. Most of what's out there gets one piece right and the rest wrong.

Celery came up first, because apigenin comes from celery, and the internet had opinions. But the celery-juice fad delivers a trace of apigenin — you'd have to drink a produce aisle to reach the dose in the studies. The celery-seed capsules admitted to one or two percent, when they admitted a number at all.

Real celery. Useless dose.

The motility formulas were built on ginger, a mild prokinetic on a different pathway that does nothing for the gas.

The chlorophyll products were the biggest surprise. Those liquid-chlorophyll drops that everyone's daughter is putting in her water are unstable, dosed for skin and "detox," and they degrade sitting on the shelf. The form that actually binds hydrogen sulfide is chlorophyllin — stabilized, specific — and it was almost nowhere.

And the fiber — the very thing everyone had been prescribing me — was the entire aisle of bulk. Psyllium. The wheat powders. Which would have packed me tighter, not looser. The chicory and inulin "prebiotics" ferment and make more gas. What I needed was the low-bulk, gel-forming soluble kind, sold alone by nobody.

Every product had one of the three pieces, and usually the wrong form of it. Trace apigenin. Chlorophyll that dies on a shelf. Bulk fiber. Not one had all three.

I was reading about apigenin concentrations — most celery supplements have only a trace, it turns out, nowhere near what the studies used — when a small company's page came up in my results. They'd built all three pieces into a gummy, made specifically for people on GLP-1 medications.

A gummy. I'm a grandmother. I have gummy vitamins in my house for visiting children. I nearly closed the laptop.

But I held the label up against my printed studies, highlighter in hand, same as I checked thirty-one years of papers.

Celery juice concentrate standardized for apigenin — standardized, an actual measured dose, not the trace in a juice glass. Chlorophyllin — the stabilized form that binds the sulfur, not the drops that fade. Low-bulk soluble prebiotic fiber — the gel kind, not the bulk everyone kept pushing on me.

All three. At the amounts the research used.

The company was called Motilli. I ordered a bottle that night.

Two gummies each morning with my coffee.

Day four: nothing, and I said so, out loud, to an empty kitchen.

Day six: the afternoon bloat came late and mild, which I refused to celebrate.

Day twelve: I went, in the morning, on my own. No capful. No tea. No box. I sat in the kitchen afterward with my coffee going cold and did a little arithmetic. It had been over a year since that had happened without assistance.

By week five it was most mornings. I went back to water aerobics the first week of February. Nobody in that pool knows why I was gone or why I came back. They just know Ruth's back.

I kept my promise. Stopped by the pharmacy in February and told Dane everything — the twelve days, the mornings, all of it. He wrote the name down on a sticky note and put it in his shirt pocket.

"Fourth woman this month," he said. "And now I'll have something to tell her."

And in December — Christmas at my daughter's — I went upstairs to that same bathroom exactly once, to wash my hands before dinner. I stood there a moment, looked at the tub edge where I'd sat crying thirteen months earlier with the fan roaring, and then I went downstairs and had pie.

Picture your own next holiday. Both versions of it exist right now. In one, you're upstairs with the fan running, counting the minutes, rehearsing the headache excuse. In the other, you're at the table the whole evening, and the upstairs bathroom is just a place to wash your hands.

The only difference between those two holidays is which end of the problem gets treated.

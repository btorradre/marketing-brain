Your stomach has a cleaning wave. It's called the migrating motor complex. It's the muscular contraction that sweeps bacteria and food debris out of your small intestine between meals. In SIBO patients, that wave is already weak. That's part of why we get overgrowth in the first place. Bacteria accumulate because the broom isn't sweeping.

GLP-1 medications work by slowing gastric emptying. That's how they reduce appetite. But slowing gastric emptying also weakens that cleaning wave even further. In a normal gut, this causes some mild side effects. Nausea. A little constipation. Nothing terrible.

In a SIBO gut, it's like turning off the only defense you have against bacterial overgrowth. You're taking a system that was already barely functioning and you're suppressing it even more.

She said most doctors don't think about this. They prescribe the GLP-1, the patient comes back eight weeks later with terrible GI symptoms, and the doctor says "that's normal, give it time." But for a SIBO patient, those aren't startup side effects. That's a full relapse. And every week you wait for it to "get better on its own" is a week the bacteria are multiplying in a stomach that can't move them out.

She told me what she now does with every SIBO patient before they start a GLP-1.

She supports gastric motility first.

Not colon motility. Not downstream. Not Miralax or Dulcolax or fiber supplements. Those work on the large intestine. The problem for SIBO patients on GLP-1s is in the stomach and small intestine. You need something that wakes up the upper GI tract. Something that supports the migrating motor complex even while the medication is trying to slow it down.

She said chlorophyll — the green pigment in plants — neutralizes sulfur compounds. It doesn't stop the gas from forming. It changes the chemistry of the gas so it doesn't smell and doesn't cause that rotten-egg belching that makes you afraid to open your mouth around other people.

And then the nutrition piece. SIBO patients already have absorption problems. We don't extract nutrients from food efficiently. Add a GLP-1 that cuts your appetite in half, and you're absorbing even less from even less food. She said the vitamins that drop fastest are A, C, K, and the B vitamins. The ones your gut lining needs to stay intact. The ones your immune system needs to keep the bacterial balance in check.

She said if she could put all of this — the prokinetic, the chlorophyll, and the key vitamins — into one thing her patients would actually take every day, it would change how SIBO patients experience GLP-1s.

I was skeptical. I have been burned by so many supplements. So many things that promised to help SIBO and made it worse. I asked her if she'd actually seen results.

By week three I was tolerating the medication better than people without SIBO were describing. I read the forums with a different kind of emotion now. Not fear. Something like relief mixed with sadness. Relief that I'd had a doctor who thought ahead. Sadness for the women who didn't.

Support your gut before you challenge it. Protect what you built before you put it at risk. You fought too hard for your stability to lose it because nobody thought to prepare you.

## AD 10B: "Your Skin Is Starving" — Skincare / Dermatologist (Converted Specialist)

**Headline:** Your dermatologist can't fix what your diet stopped building

If you're on a GLP-1 and your skin is getting dull, thin, dry, or aged-looking — it's not because you lost weight too fast.

It's because your skin is starving.

You're eating 800 calories a day. Your body is rationing nutrients. And your skin is the first thing it stops feeding.

I'm a dermatologist. I've been practicing for nineteen years. And in the last two years, I have seen a pattern walk through my door that I have never seen before in my career.

Women in their fifties and sixties coming in with skin complaints that don't match their history. Women who had good skin a year ago. Women who had reasonable moisture, decent elasticity, normal healing. Not perfect. Age-appropriate. But functional.

Now their skin is papery. Fragile. Bruising easily. Healing slowly. Their face has thinned in a way that isn't just fat loss. It looks depleted. Like the skin itself has lost its substance.

Every single one of them is on a GLP-1 medication.

The first few times I saw it, I attributed it to rapid weight loss. That's what everyone calls it. Ozempic face. The idea that losing weight quickly leaves behind loose, sagging skin because the fat that was filling it out disappeared faster than the skin could retract.

But that explanation never fully satisfied me. Because I was seeing something beyond sagging. I was seeing skin that looked malnourished. And there's a difference.

Sagging is a structural problem. The scaffolding lost its filling. Malnourished skin is a cellular problem. The cells themselves don't have what they need to turn over, repair, and rebuild.

I started running bloodwork on these patients. Not the standard panel. I was looking at specific micronutrients. Vitamin A. Vitamin C. Vitamin K. B6. Folate.

The results were remarkably consistent.

Almost every GLP-1 patient I tested was deficient in at least two of those vitamins. Many were deficient in three or four. And these weren't women who had been deficient before starting the medication. These were women who had been eating normally, getting reasonable nutrition, and maintaining their skin from the inside without even thinking about it.

Then they started a GLP-1. Their appetite dropped by half or more. Some of them were eating one small meal a day. Some were eating two meals but with portions so small they wouldn't sustain a child. They weren't doing this on purpose. The medication suppresses hunger so effectively that eating feels like a chore.

And nobody told them what would happen to their skin.

Here's what I need you to understand about your skin. It is an organ. It is the largest organ in your body. And it is constantly rebuilding itself. Every twenty-seven days, you get an entirely new outer layer of skin. Every single cell in your epidermis is replaced in less than a month.

That rebuilding requires raw materials. Specific ones.

Vitamin A controls cell turnover. It's the signal that tells old skin cells to shed and new ones to form. Without adequate vitamin A, your skin holds onto dead cells too long. It gets dull. Rough. That glow you used to have, the one that people would compliment you on, that's not some mysterious quality. That's healthy cell turnover. That's old cells leaving and new cells arriving on schedule. Cut off the vitamin A supply, and the schedule breaks down.

Vitamin C is the foundation of collagen production. Your body cannot synthesize collagen without it. Not slowly. Not partially. It cannot make collagen at all without adequate vitamin C. Collagen is what gives your skin its firmness, its bounce, its ability to resist gravity. When collagen production drops, your skin doesn't just sag. It thins. It bruises. It stops healing properly. That cut that used to close in three days now takes ten. That bruise that used to fade in a week lingers for three.

Vitamin K controls your skin's microcirculation. The tiny blood vessels that feed nutrients to the skin cells and carry waste away. When vitamin K drops, you get dark circles under your eyes. You get that translucent quality where you can see veins you never noticed before. Your skin starts looking ghostly because the circulation underneath it is failing.

B6 and folate support the cellular machinery that makes all of this happen. Without them, even if you have enough A and C and K, the cells can't use them efficiently. It's like having all the building materials delivered to a construction site but no workers to assemble them.

Now think about what a GLP-1 does to your intake of these vitamins.

You're eating a fraction of what you used to eat. The foods that are easiest to tolerate on a GLP-1 tend to be bland. Crackers. Toast. Broth. Plain rice. The nutrient-dense foods — the leafy greens, the orange vegetables, the organ meats, the berries — those are the ones that are hardest to eat when your stomach feels full after three bites and the thought of food makes you nauseous.

So you're depleting your stores. Every day, your skin needs its raw materials. Every day, you're not providing them. And your body is making a calculation. It's triaging. When nutrients are scarce, your body sends them where they're needed most urgently. Your heart gets fed first. Your brain. Your liver. Your kidneys.

Your skin is last in line.

It's the organ your body considers most expendable. And when resources are tight, it is the first one to get starved.

This is what women are experiencing when they say they look ten years older since starting their GLP-1. They're not imagining it. Their skin has literally been defunded by their own body.

And here's what breaks my heart. These women are going to their dermatologists — some of them are coming to me — and asking for topical solutions. Better moisturizers. Retinol creams. Vitamin C serums. Hyaluronic acid.

And those products help. I'm not going to tell you to stop your skincare routine. But they are treating the surface of a problem that starts at the cellular level. You can put vitamin C on your skin all day long. If your body doesn't have enough vitamin C circulating in your blood to feed the cells that are building new collagen underneath, the serum is a coat of paint on a house with no foundation.

I had to solve this for my patients. I had to find a way to get these specific vitamins into women who couldn't eat enough food to get them naturally. And it couldn't be a horse pill they'd gag on. These women are already nauseous. Asking them to swallow a handful of supplements with a full glass of water on a stomach that rebels against volume was setting them up to fail.

I started researching delivery formats. What could deliver meaningful amounts of vitamins A, C, K, B6, and folate in something small, gentle on the stomach, and easy to take daily. Something that wouldn't trigger the nausea that was already making their lives difficult.

One of my colleagues in gastroenterology had been recommending a celery juice gummy to her GLP-1 patients for a different reason — for the digestive side effects. The motility issues. The sulfur burps. When she told me what was in it, I pulled up the formulation.

It was called Motilli. And it had every single vitamin on my deficiency list.

Vitamins A, C, K, B6, and folate. In a pectin-based gummy, which meant gentle absorption, no stomach irritation. The celery juice extract and chlorophyll were in there for the gastric motility and gas issues, which was a bonus for my patients. But what I cared about was the micronutrient profile.

I was cautious. I've recommended supplements before that didn't deliver what they claimed. But this one was third-party tested and Clean Label Certified, which meant an independent lab had verified that what was on the label was actually in the product.

I started suggesting it to my GLP-1 patients who were showing skin deterioration. Two gummies a day. I told them to give it twelve weeks because that's how long it takes for a full cycle of skin cell turnover to reflect nutritional changes.

The first thing I noticed, at about four to five weeks, was the bruising. Women who had been bruising from the lightest contact reported that the bruises were fading faster. Healing was improving. That told me the vitamin K was reaching the microcirculation and the vitamin C was supporting the vascular walls.

By eight weeks, the dullness was lifting. Skin tone was more even. That dead, flat quality was being replaced by something warmer. That was the vitamin A kicking in, normalizing cell turnover, allowing fresh cells to reach the surface on schedule again.

By twelve weeks, some of my patients had firmness returning. Not dramatically. Not like they were twenty years younger. But measurably. The skin on the backs of their hands, which is where I always check, had more resilience. More bounce-back. The collagen production was recovering.

One patient told me her daughter looked at her and said, "Mom, you look like yourself again." She cried in my office. She said she'd been afraid she'd traded her face for her weight loss and there was no getting it back.

She got it back.

Not all of it. I want to be honest. These women are still aging. They're still on a medication that limits their food intake. But the active deterioration stopped. The skin stopped getting worse and started slowly getting better. And for women who had been watching themselves age in fast-forward, that reversal was everything.

I'm still careful about what I recommend. I still want my patients eating as many nutrient-dense foods as they can tolerate. I still want good skincare routines. But for the gap between what they can eat and what their skin needs, this has become something I mention in nearly every GLP-1 patient visit.

If your mirror has been telling you something is wrong since you started your medication, listen to it. Your skin is trying to communicate. It's telling you it's hungry.

And no serum, no cream, no facial is going to feed it from the outside.

Not because I don't have the recipe. I have it memorized. Every layer, every measurement, passed down from a woman who never wrote anything down. I know it by heart.

I can't be in the kitchen long enough to make it. The smell of the meat sauce makes me nauseous before I can brown the first pound of beef.

I need to tell you about last Thanksgiving because I think some of you will understand it in a way nobody in my family did.

I was the one in the kitchen by six in the morning with coffee and a plan. I made my rolls from scratch. I brined my turkey for two days. I made four pies every Thanksgiving because different people liked different kinds and I wanted every person at my table to have their favorite.

Food was how I told my family I loved them. I didn't grow up saying it out loud. My people showed love by feeding you. By remembering you liked extra gravy. By making a separate batch of mashed potatoes without garlic for the one child who complained about it in 2004 and I never forgot.

I started Wegovy in June of last year. I needed to. My doctor had been talking about my blood sugar for three years. My knees were shot. I was sixty-one and I felt eighty. I knew the medication could help me and I wanted the help.

I couldn't eat.

Not just didn't want to. Couldn't. The nausea started in week two and it never left. It wasn't the kind where you feel sick and then it passes. It was a constant low hum of revulsion toward food. Like my body had decided that everything I put in my mouth was a threat.

At the dinner table. In the car. In the middle of a conversation with your granddaughter.

"You're backed up to here."

The ER doctor said it standing at the screen, pointing at a shadow on my wife's X-ray. It started low. It went all the way up under her ribs.

He asked her how long it had been.

"Nine days," she said.

He looked at her over his glasses like she'd gotten the number wrong.

My wife's name is Cheryl. She's 55. We've been married 26 years. And I have never felt more useless than I did that night, 2 AM, sitting in a hospital chair holding her purse because she was too doubled over to hold it herself.

Let me back up.

Cheryl started Zepbound in January. Her A1C came back at 6.1. Her doctor circled it and said "pre-diabetic." She weighed 214 that morning. I know because she wrote it on the whiteboard inside the pantry door — the same board she'd written a starting number on a dozen times over twenty years. Weight Watchers, twice. The clinic diet. Nutrisystem. A rowing machine that became a coat rack.

Every one worked for a season. Then the season ended.

Zepbound was different. The food noise just — stopped. She cried at the kitchen table the week she noticed. Happy crying. By June the whiteboard said 180. Thirty-four pounds. She wore a dress she'd kept in the closet for a decade.

I'd have called it a miracle. I still would.

Then, month three, the constipation started.

Three days between bathroom trips. Then four. Then a week and I didn't even know it. Bloating so bad she looked four months pregnant by dinner. Sulfur burps — the rotten-egg kind — an hour after every meal. A hard knot under her right ribs she'd press on during TV, the way you check a bruise to see if it still hurts.

First thing I actually noticed was the heating pad. Stayed on the couch. I didn't ask why.

Then we stopped going to the diner on Fridays. She said she wasn't hungry. That same night I watched her stand at the pantry pressing her fist into her stomach like she was holding a door shut.

Then the CVS bags. Miralax. Colace. Magnesium citrate, the kind you drink.

Then the box of enemas under the sink. I found it. I didn't say anything.

One night I heard her crying in the bathroom. I stood in the hallway with my hand on the door. I didn't knock.

I've apologized for that since. She said, "What would you have done?"

Honest answer: nothing. Neither of us understood the problem yet.

By September it was five, six days at a time. Then eight. Then the night it was nine, and she woke me at 1:40 AM, gray, sweating, saying something felt like it was tearing.

So. The ER. The X-ray. Backed up to here.

They didn't send us home.

They admitted her. Around 4 AM a surgeon came into the bay — calm voice, the kind they train them to use — and explained what happens if a blockage like this doesn't clear. The bowel wall stretches. Blood flow gets pinched off. If the tissue starts to die, they go in.

"We'd remove the affected section."

The affected section. Of my wife's colon.

He was telling us that if the next twelve hours went badly, Cheryl would wake up missing part of her large intestine. Maybe a bag. "Temporarily, in most cases." Then he slid a consent form across the tray table.

"Just so we're not chasing signatures if it becomes urgent."

I signed at 4:10 AM. My hand needed the bed rail to steady it.

Then I sat in the vinyl chair and didn't close my eyes once.

It cleared. Barely. I'll spare you the details. The surgeon came back the next afternoon, looked at the new imaging, and said the sentence I still hear at odd hours.

"She keeps her colon. This time."

This time.

Three days later, Cheryl sat in her own doctor's office. The doctor who'd heard about this for months. The doctor who wrote the prescription in the first place. She told her everything — the ER, the X-ray, the consent form.

Her doctor nodded. Typed something.

"Eat more fiber. Drink more water. Try Miralax."

Cheryl had been on Miralax daily for four months. We had three fiber supplements in the pantry. My wife drinks water like it's her job.

She didn't argue. She just went quiet.

In the car she said, "That's everything I'm already doing."

I watched something leave her. Not anger. Disappointment. The kind you feel when the person who's supposed to have the answers just reads you the label off a box you already own.

A near-surgery. A consent form. And the plan was the same three things that already failed for four months.

That night I couldn't sleep. "She keeps her colon. This time" doesn't let you sleep. I sat at the kitchen table at 11:40 PM and typed into my laptop: GLP-1 constipation why laxatives don't work.

What I found made me angry in a way I still haven't gotten over.

These medications slow the stomach down. On purpose. There's a clinical name for it — delayed gastric emptying — and it's part of the design, not a glitch. Food goes in and sits. Hours. Sometimes days. While it sits, it ferments. That's the sulfur. It dries and compacts. That's the brick feeling.

And here's what nobody at that hospital said out loud: the colon — the place everyone treats — is mostly empty. The pileup is at the top of the system. Not the bottom.

Cheryl's entire cabinet was aimed at the bottom.

Miralax pulls water into the colon. Nothing was down there to soften.

Fiber adds bulk. Bulk behind a closed valve just packs the line tighter.

Stimulant laxatives squeeze the colon. You can squeeze an empty pipe all night. Nothing comes.

The enemas — the thing that had her crying at a rest stop off I-80 — rinse the last three feet of a problem that starts fifty feet upstream.

I looked at the prescriptions too. Figured that's where the real medicine had to be.

It wasn't.

The one drug that actually speeds up a slow stomach is metoclopramide. Reglan. Its own label carries a black-box warning for a permanent movement disorder. Hard pass. Everything past that — Linzess, Motegrity — works back down at the colon. The exit. Again.

Not one thing anyone prescribes touches the stomach.

So the question became: what does?

I read for three nights. Followed the citations like a trail. Three things kept surfacing, and each one taught me the same lesson twice.

Apigenin, first. A compound in celery. It supports the vagus nerve — the wiring that tells the stomach to push food through, the exact signal the medication turns down. In the studies it read like turning a volume knob back up. Not fighting the medication. Restoring what it muted.

So I went looking for it. And learned the hard way that the right ingredient in the wrong form is just a slower way to waste money.

There's a whole celery-juice craze. Mason jars every morning. But apigenin in raw celery is a trace. You'd drink a garden a day and still fall short of what the studies used. The celery-seed capsules admitted to one, maybe two percent — when they admitted a number at all.

Real celery. Useless dose.

Second thing: chlorophyllin. A concentrated form of chlorophyll that binds hydrogen sulfide — the sulfur gas — and neutralizes the molecule itself. Not covering the smell. Disarming it.

Here's the trap. Everyone's feed is full of liquid chlorophyll drops. Sold for "detox." For skin. Most of it is unstable — degrades sitting on a shelf, dosed for a glow, not for grabbing sulfur. The stabilized form, the one that actually works, was almost nowhere.

Third: a specific low-bulk, gel-forming soluble fiber. Draws moisture into what's already stuck. Softens it without adding volume to a jammed line.

The opposite of everything her doctor kept recommending. The entire fiber aisle is bulk — psyllium, wheat powders — which just packs a jam tighter. The "prebiotic" inulin blends are worse. They ferment. More gas, when gas was half the problem.

Wake the stomach. Neutralize the sulfur. Soften the backlog. All three, at once, or you're just squeezing a different section of empty pipe.

Three nights. A dozen browser tabs. Three abandoned Amazon carts.

Trace apigenin. Chlorophyll that dies on a shelf. Bulk fiber. Everything did one job — and usually the wrong version of it.

A gummy. I almost kept scrolling. Sounded like candy pretending to be medicine.

But I checked the label against my notes.

Chlorophyllin. The stabilized form. Not the drops that fade.

Low-bulk soluble prebiotic fiber. The gel kind. Not the bulk that had been making it worse.

Day eleven, she came downstairs, poured her coffee, and said, "We're okay."

That's our code. Twenty-six years old. I had to turn around and look out the window for a minute.

Week four, we went back to the diner. Friday night. She ordered the meatloaf and ate it without doing the math she'd been running silently at every meal for six months — the if-I-eat-this-at-seven-it's-still-there-Thursday arithmetic.

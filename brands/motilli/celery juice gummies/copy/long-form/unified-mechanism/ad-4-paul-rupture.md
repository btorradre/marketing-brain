My wife had been on a GLP-1 medication for ten weeks.

Last fall on a Saturday evening she developed a sharp pain in her lower abdomen.

By Sunday morning her bowel had ruptured.

—

I am writing this because what happened to my wife should not have happened to anyone, and I wish to God that someone had written this for me before it did.

If you are on a GLP-1 medication, please read every word of this.

I will not embellish anything. I will tell you exactly what happened and exactly what we learned from the man who saved her life.

—

I'm 54.

My wife Ellen is 51.

We have been married twenty-three years. Two adult kids, one in grad school, one teaching at a high school in another state.

Ellen started a GLP-1 medication ten weeks before the night this happened. Her primary care doctor had prescribed it for her A1C, which had crept into pre-diabetic territory at her annual physical.

She had also wanted to lose forty pounds for years. The medication was supposed to do both.

In the first ten weeks she lost sixteen pounds. The food noise in her head — the thing she had been trying to describe to me for two decades and had finally given up explaining — went quiet for the first time in her adult life.

She said it was like someone had turned down a radio that had been on in her head since college.

I want all of that on the record before I tell you the rest, because the medication did do what it was supposed to do.

It is what it did to her gut, silently, that I am writing about.

—

For ten weeks, she had been mildly constipated.

Going every four or five days instead of every day.

She had taken Miralax twice that week. She had mentioned to me at breakfast that her stomach felt "heavier than usual" but she had not used the word pain.

I want to say that again.

For ten weeks, no real warning. No emergency, no impaction, no ER trip, no bathroom-floor moment.

Just a slow, quiet slowdown that we both wrote off as a side effect she would adjust to.

—

The Saturday evening this happened was the Saturday before our daughter's birthday.

We were at the kitchen table eating roast chicken and roasted potatoes that I had made.

Ellen put her fork down halfway through her plate and put one hand on her lower right side.

She said: "Something is wrong."

She did not look scared yet. Just confused.

She said the pain was in her lower right abdomen, sharp, "like someone was twisting a knife slowly."

We thought appendicitis.

I drove her to the closest hospital, fifteen minutes away.

—

The first ER did a quick exam.

The doctor on shift was a man maybe ten years younger than us. He pressed on her stomach. He asked when she had last eaten. He ran basic bloodwork.

He came back two hours later and said the bloodwork was fine, the rebound test was inconclusive, it was probably "muscular or possibly an early UTI."

He prescribed her a painkiller and an antibiotic for the possible UTI.

He sent us home.

—

I do not blame him.

I have thought about him a hundred times since and I have decided that he was doing the best he could with what he was trained to look for.

What he was not trained to look for was a slowly perforating colon in a woman ten weeks into a GLP-1 medication.

Almost nobody is, yet.

—

We got home around 1 AM.

I helped Ellen into bed.

I gave her the painkiller.

She fell asleep around 2.

At 3:40 AM she woke me up by getting out of bed and trying to walk to the bathroom.

She did not make it to the door.

She vomited on the carpet.

She tried to stand back up and her knees gave out.

She was white. Whiter than I have ever seen her. Whiter than the morning we lost her father.

I called 911.

—

I want to be honest about something.

The dispatcher told me to drive her instead of waiting for the ambulance because we lived in a part of town where the response time would have been longer than the drive.

I drove her to a different hospital — a bigger one, thirty-five minutes from us, on the other side of town.

I do not remember the drive.

I remember her in the passenger seat with her head against the window. I remember her saying, twice, "I am so sorry, Paul."

I do not know what she was sorry for.

I have asked her since. She does not remember saying it.

—

We got to the second hospital at 4:02 AM.

A nurse named Carmen took one look at her, put her in a wheelchair, and ran with her to triage. I have never seen anyone run in a hospital before that morning.

The CT scan happened within the first hour.

The surgeon was on the floor within ninety minutes.

She was in surgery by 8 AM.

—

I sat in the waiting room from 8 AM until 12:47 PM, when a man named Dr. Marcus Adeyemi walked through the swinging doors and said my name out loud.

He was tall, sixty, gray hair cropped close, scrubs. The kind of presence you instinctively trust.

He sat down on the chair next to me.

He said: "Your wife is stable. The surgery went as well as it could go. We removed about a foot of her colon. We have given her a temporary stoma, which means there is a bag attached to her abdomen that will collect her stool while the part of her colon we left behind heals. In about three months we will do a second surgery to reconnect everything. She is going to be in the hospital for at least two weeks."

I did not cry in front of him.

I waited until I got back to the parking garage.

—

She was in the hospital for seventeen days.

—

I want to tell you about the conversation I had with Dr. Adeyemi on day four of her stay, because that conversation is the reason I am writing this.

—

He had asked me to come to his office in the surgical wing for a consult.

Ellen was on heavy painkillers and sleeping most of that day.

I walked over at 2 PM. His office was small, windowless, a wall of medical books and a wall-mounted screen.

He pulled up Ellen's CT image on the screen.

I had not seen it.

He pointed to a place on the image that looked like a tear in a piece of paper.

He said: "This is where her colon perforated. This is the rupture site. I want to walk you through how we get here, because most of the surgeons I trained with are not telling families about this."

I sat down.

—

He stood in front of the screen with his hand on the image.

He said: "Your wife's colon is a muscle. To do its job, that muscle has to contract and push waste through. To contract, it needs a signal. And that signal is a short-chain fatty acid called butyrate."

He looked at me.

"Your wife does not make butyrate herself. The bacteria that live in her colon make it, by fermenting the fiber she eats. They produce the butyrate, the butyrate tells the muscle to contract, and things move. That system runs quietly her whole life and she never thinks about it."

He pointed at the image.

"Now. GLP-1 medications do something to those bacteria that almost no primary care doctor is currently warning patients about."

I leaned forward.

—

He said: "They starve them."

He said it quietly, like he was angry.

"The medication slows the entire digestive transit, which is part of how it works for weight loss. But that slow transit also changes what reaches those bacteria. The food they ferment stops getting down to them. Fewer of them survive, so they make less butyrate. Less butyrate means a weaker signal, which means an even slower colon — which means even less reaches the bacteria the next day, and fewer of them survive to the day after that."

He moved his hand in a small circle in the air.

"It feeds on itself. Every week it pulls a little tighter. And the medication holds that first slowdown in place, so the loop cannot break on its own. By month two or month three, in many users, those bacterial populations are already at a fraction of what they were before the medication started."

He tapped the screen.

"When the colony collapses, the signal collapses with it. Your wife's colon lost the instruction it needed to contract. So waste built up. Pressure built up. And in most women this stays a miserable, worsening constipation for a long time. In a smaller number — and I am seeing more of them — the wall of the colon is not designed to hold that pressure for weeks on end. It thins at a weak spot. And eventually the wall gives way."

He paused.

"That is what I removed from your wife this morning. The wall that gave way. She is the far end of the same problem thousands of women on this medication are sitting in the early stages of right now."

—

I want to tell you the next sentence Dr. Adeyemi said because it is the sentence that will not leave my head.

—

He turned away from the screen and looked at me directly.

He said: "Your wife is not the first GLP-1 patient I have done this surgery on this year. She is the ninth."

The ninth.

In one calendar year.

In one surgeon's caseload.

—

I asked him why nobody had warned us.

He said: "Because the research is newer than the medication. The medication moved faster than medicine could catch up. We are seeing this happen in real time, in operating rooms, before the journals can write about it. Most primary care doctors have not seen it yet. Most patients have not heard about it yet. By the time they do, they are in the bay your wife was in last night."

He sat down across from me.

He said: "I am telling you this because she is going to come home. She is going to be on a stoma bag for three months. We are going to do the reattachment surgery in late winter. And between now and then, you are going to have to make sure she does not end up back here."

—

I asked him what to do.

I had nothing else.

I had a man in scrubs who had just removed a foot of my wife's colon and I had nothing to ask except what to do.

—

He said: "First, understand what does not need to change. The weight loss and the gut are two different systems. The medication controls her appetite up in her brain — that is the part that gave her back her life, and there is no reason to give it up. What broke is the bacterial community down in her colon. You fix that without touching the other. She keeps what the medication gave her."

He held up a finger.

Both at once, or it does not work."

He looked at me.

I did not cry there either.

There's a calendar on the back of my bathroom door. The free kind, from the pharmacy, with a different bird for every month.

For the last seven months I've kept a pen on the edge of the sink and marked an X on every day I managed to go to the bathroom.

In the blank days between the X's, I write a number. How many days it's been since the last one. So a normal week, back when I had normal weeks, would just be a row of X's. Now a week looks like one X and then 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 climbing up toward the next one.

Last month had four X's.

The longest stretch was eleven.

I'm going to tell you how that calendar started, what I finally found out, and why the page looks completely different now. Because if you're on a GLP-1 medication, odds are you've started counting too — maybe not on a calendar, maybe just in your head. And I need you to know what I wish someone had told me about a hundred days sooner.

HOW THE COUNTING STARTED

I'm fifty-two. I'd been heavy my whole adult life, and I'd tried everything you've tried, and none of it held.

When my doctor put me on a GLP-1, it was the first thing that ever worked. I don't say that lightly. The food noise — the constant arguing in my head about what I wanted and what I was allowed — it just went quiet. The weight came off. I felt, for the first time in thirty years, like a normal person walking through a normal day.

I want that on the record before anything else, because I never wanted to stop. I still don't.

The constipation started a few weeks in. Small at first. My doctor said to keep an eye on it — "track your bowel movements, let me know if it doesn't settle." That's all the calendar was at the beginning. A medical instruction. An X so I'd remember what to tell her.

It didn't settle. It got worse, and somewhere in there the calendar stopped being an instruction and turned into something else.

I started writing the day-counts. Then came the notes in the margins — what I'd eaten, what I'd taken, every variable I could think to log. I was looking for a pattern. I thought if I just tracked carefully enough, I'd find the thing — the food, the trick, the variable — that made the difference between an X and a 6.

I'd stand at that sink in the morning and study the page like it was going to tell me something.

It never did.

WHAT I TRIED, ALL OF IT LOGGED

If you saw my calendar you'd see the whole graveyard written right there in the margins.

Miralax. Twice a day for a while. Fiber gummies. Metamucil. Magnesium citrate at night. Stool softeners. Prune juice. The smooth-move tea that cramped me so badly I logged it and never touched it again. I was already drinking eighty, ninety ounces of water a day — I walked every morning — so don't tell me about water and walking. It's in the margins, logged and dated like everything else.

Some weeks looked slightly better and I'd think I'd cracked it. Then the next week the numbers climbed again and whatever I thought I'd figured out turned out to be nothing.

The bloating was constant. I looked six months pregnant by dinner. I stopped eating dinner some nights because eating made it worse. I started turning down plans I couldn't predict around. My whole life began to organize itself around a page on the back of a door.

THE APPOINTMENT WITH THE PHOTOS

I did the thing you're supposed to do. I brought it to my doctor.

Not just my word for it — evidence. I'd photographed the calendar pages, all of them, month after month, and I had them ready on my phone. I'd done exactly what she'd asked, for months, and I had the record to prove it.

I held up my phone and scrolled through seven months of it — the X's getting sparser, the numbers between them climbing.

She glanced at it for about three seconds.

"Constipation is a known side effect," she said. "Have you tried adding fiber?"

I told her fiber made the bloating worse. I told her what was in the margins.

"Sometimes it takes a while to adjust. Keep tracking. We can talk about a prescription laxative if it gets severe."

I put my phone back in my purse.

I'm not angry at her, exactly. I think she was doing what she was trained to do. But I had handed her a record of my own decline and she'd looked at it for three seconds and told me to add fiber. I drove home and I understood, in a way I hadn't before, that nobody was going to figure this out for me.

WHEN STEVE FOUND IT

My husband didn't know how bad it was.

I'd kept it on the back of the door, which we keep closed, and I'd kept the worst of it to myself the way you do when you're embarrassed and you don't want the person you love watching you fall apart over something you can't fix.

He found it on a Sunday. He was looking for the spare razors in the cabinet behind the door and he swung it shut and there it was at eye level.

He didn't say anything for a second. Then he said, "What is this?"

I told him.

He looked at the page for that month. The four X's. The eleven. Then he reached up and started flipping back through the earlier months I'd taped behind it, and I watched him watch the numbers climb backward through the year.

"How long have you been doing this?" he asked.

"Seven months."

He turned around and looked at me, and his face did something I hadn't seen before, and he said, "Seven months? You've been keeping a calendar of this for seven months and I didn't know?"

I started crying. Not because of the constipation. Because somebody had finally seen the thing I'd been carrying by myself, and the size of it showed up on his face before I could explain it away.

He sat down on the edge of the tub. "We're going to figure this out," he said. "Tonight."

WHAT I WAS ACTUALLY MEASURING

That night I did something different.

For seven months I'd been searching the same way: "how to relieve constipation," "best laxative for GLP-1," "does magnesium help." All of them relief searches — the searches of someone hunting for the next thing to write in a margin.

This time I stopped looking for relief and started looking for the reason. I searched why does the colon stop moving. I wanted to understand the machine, not patch it.

And I finally found someone explaining what was actually happening underneath the calendar.

The first thing I had to understand was that the constipation and the weight loss aren't the same problem — they aren't even in the same system. The weight loss happens up in your brain and your appetite; the medication turns down the hunger signals, and that's the dial doing all the work I was grateful for. The constipation happens somewhere else entirely, down in the colon, on completely different wiring. That mattered more than I realized at the time, because it meant the thing I never wanted to give up and the thing that was ruining my life were never actually attached to each other.

So what was happening down in that second system?

Your colon is a muscle. To move, it needs a signal telling it to squeeze — and that signal is a short-chain fatty acid called butyrate. You don't make butyrate yourself. The bacteria living in your colon make it, by feeding on what passes through. They produce the butyrate, the butyrate tells the muscle to contract, things move. That's the system. It runs quietly your whole life and you never think about it.

Then I read what a GLP-1 does to it, and I had to put the phone down.

The medication slows everything down. That's how it works on your appetite — slower digestion, you feel full longer, you eat less. But that slowdown reaches all the way to your colon. When everything slows, less material reaches those bacteria, and the colony begins to thin. Fewer of them produce less butyrate. With less butyrate, the signal weakens and the colon slows further — which means even less reaches the bacteria the next day, and fewer of them survive to the day after that.

It feeds on itself. Every week, a little tighter. And the medication holds that first slowdown in place, so the loop can't break on its own.

I sat there and I understood, finally, what my calendar actually was.

It was a scoreboard.

For seven months I'd been writing down the score of a game that was being lost somewhere I couldn't see. I kept hunting the margins for a variable — a food, a remedy, a trick — and there was no variable. There was a colony of bacteria being quietly starved down, week after week, and the X's spreading apart were just the scoreboard reporting it.

That's why nothing I tracked ever revealed a pattern. I'd been measuring the wrong thing the whole time — the symptom of a die-off I didn't even know was happening.

And every remedy in my margins, I suddenly saw why it failed:

The fiber was the cruelest one. Fiber is just food for those bacteria — and if the bacteria have been starved down, more food with no one to eat it just piles up and bloats you. That's why the gummies made it worse.

Probiotics never had a chance. They drop fresh bacteria into a gut whose whole environment has been disrupted, and nothing can survive in conditions that won't support it.

And the laxatives? They force one X onto the calendar and rebuild nothing underneath. The colony is still starved the next morning, and the next number just starts climbing again.

None of it touched the thing that had actually broken.

WHAT A REAL FIX HAD TO DO

So I knew, finally, what I was actually looking for. Two things, together.

It had to rebuild the environment those bacteria need — calm the irritated lining, fix the conditions — so the right bacteria could come back and survive. And then it had to feed them, the gentle way, so they'd multiply and start making the signal again. Not the coarse bulk that had been backing me up worse. Something soluble. Something that fed the colony without packing more mass into a system that wasn't moving.

Rebuild the place. Then feed what grows there. Both at once, or it doesn't work.

And the moment I had those two requirements, I understood why every product I'd ever logged had failed. Each one did one or the other, or neither. The fiber fed bacteria that weren't there. The probiotics added bacteria with no place to live. Nothing did both.

I read about apigenin — a compound concentrated in celery juice — that worked on exactly the first half. It calms the inflammation in the gut lining, shifts the balance back toward the bacteria that make butyrate, and helps rebuild the colon wall itself. And there was one strange detail: apigenin barely absorbs into your bloodstream. For most things that's a flaw. Here it's the whole point, because if it doesn't get absorbed away, it stays down in the colon — right where the problem is.

And I read about a fine, soluble prebiotic fiber that did the second half: fed those specific butyrate-making bacteria so they'd come back and produce the signal again, without the bulk that had bloated me for months.

I wasn't looking for a product. I was looking for those two things in one place. Steve sat with me while I looked, and almost everything had one and not the other.

Then we found a small company that had put both into one gummy. Concentrated celery juice, standardized so you actually get a real dose of apigenin instead of a trace. And the soluble prebiotic fiber. Third-party tested, with the lab results posted right on the site.

It's called Motilli.

The site didn't look like the big brands and I almost clicked away. But the formula was, line for line, the two things I'd just spent a night deciding a real fix would have to do.

Three gummies before bed with a full glass of water. Sixty in a bottle. Steve ordered it before he went to sleep.

THE PAGE STARTED TO CHANGE

The first few days, nothing. I'd been let down enough times that I almost didn't keep marking the calendar.

Then on the fifth day, an X. A real one. Not the emptied-out, cramping kind the Miralax forced. Just normal and quiet, the way it used to be.

I marked it and I stood there looking at it.

The next number didn't climb as high before the next X. Then the X's started landing closer together. Within two weeks the long climbs of 5 and 6 and 7 were gone, and the page was starting to look like a page from years ago. Mostly X's. Barely any numbers between them.

The bloating went down. I ate dinner again, and I wore a pair of jeans I'd written off two sizes ago. And I never came off my medication. The weight kept coming off, the food noise stayed quiet, and I hadn't given up a single thing it gave me. I'd just fixed what it broke on the way.

Steve started checking the calendar before I did. He'd come out of the bathroom in the morning and just say "X" with this little smile, like he was reading me a score that was finally going the right way.

WHY THE CHEAP VERSIONS WON'T DO IT

A friend of mine on the same medication tried to shortcut it with a regular probiotic and a fiber tub from the grocery store. Nothing changed for her, and I knew it wouldn't.

A standard probiotic just drops bacteria into an environment that still can't hold them. Grocery-store fiber adds bulk to a system that isn't moving — the exact thing that had bloated me worse for months. And a laxative? One mark on the calendar, nothing rebuilt underneath.

The reason Motilli worked when seven months of my margins didn't is that it does both halves at once — the celery-juice apigenin to rebuild the environment so the bacteria can survive, and the soluble fiber to feed them once they're back. Most things have one or the other. That's the whole difference.

## AD 1A: "The Purse Pharmacy" (REVISED)

My purse became a pharmacy and none of it was working

Gas-X. Tums. Charcoal capsules. Ginger root chews. Peppermint oil capsules. Three different brands of mints I rotate throughout the day.

My purse weighs four pounds more than it should. Every pocket has something in it for the burps. And none of them — not one — has ever actually stopped a sulfur burp from happening.

I know this because I keep a mental scorecard. Not on purpose. It just happens when you've been managing something every single day for eleven months. You start tracking. You start timing. You chew two Gas-X at 11:45 because lunch is at noon and maybe if you get ahead of it this time, maybe today will be different.

It's never different.

My name is Debra. I'm fifty-eight. I've been on Wegovy since April of last year and I want to be clear about something before I say anything else — I love this medication. I have lost forty-one pounds. My A1C is down. My knees don't ache when I get out of bed. My doctor is genuinely proud of me for the first time in fifteen years and that feeling alone is worth every penny of the copay.

But the sulfur burps. The bloating that makes me look five months pregnant by dinnertime. The constipation that turned into this rotating cycle of nothing for four days and then a full morning trapped in the bathroom. I won't describe it more than that. You already know.

And so I built the purse pharmacy.

It started with just Tums. Then my friend Linda said charcoal capsules were the answer. Then my daughter sent me a link to peppermint oil capsules on Amazon. Then I read a thread in a Wegovy support group about ginger root and someone else said simethicone was better and someone else said fennel tea and someone else said they take a probiotic with forty billion CFU and it cleared everything up in a week.

So I tried that too.

The thing about trying something new every two weeks is you never know what's working and what isn't. You just keep layering. You keep adding. You tell yourself the combination is helping even when you're still burping sulfur at your grandson's baseball game and praying the wind doesn't shift.

The moment I actually stopped and looked at what I was doing came at my sister-in-law's house in October. We were all there for my niece's engagement dinner. Fifteen people. Beautiful table. Her future in-laws meeting our family for the first time. I was feeling fine, which should have been my first warning because I always feel fine right before I don't.

I reached into my purse for the peppermint capsules and somehow knocked the whole bag off my chair. It hit the tile floor and just exploded open. Everything came rolling out. The Gas-X tin. The charcoal bottle. The ginger chews. The mints. A gallon-size Ziploc with five different supplements. Two of those little Pepto tablets I keep in a contact lens case so no one sees the pink.

My sister-in-law's mother picked up the charcoal capsules and read the label. She didn't say anything. She just looked at me the way people look at you when they're deciding whether to be concerned or amused and they land on concerned.

My niece said, "Aunt Deb, are you okay?"

I laughed it off. I said something about my stomach being sensitive. I shoved it all back in my purse and smiled and ate three bites of chicken piccata and spent the rest of the night trying not to move too much because movement makes it worse.

In the car on the way home I cried. Not the dramatic kind. The quiet kind where you just leak a little because you're tired and embarrassed and you can feel your stomach doing the thing it does where it sits there like a brick and you know tomorrow morning is going to be awful.

My husband didn't ask what was wrong because he already knows. He just handed me a napkin from the glove box.

That night I couldn't sleep and I did what I always do — I went back to the support groups. But this time I wasn't looking for another remedy. I was trying to understand why nothing worked. I typed it differently. Instead of "what helps with sulfur burps on Wegovy" I typed "why don't gas remedies work on GLP-1 medications."

That search changed everything for me.

I found an article from a gastroenterologist explaining something so simple I couldn't believe no one had told me. GLP-1 medications work by slowing your stomach. That's not a side effect. That's the mechanism. That's how the drug reduces your appetite — it tells your stomach muscles to stop contracting as often, so food sits there longer, so you feel full.

But when food sits too long in a slow stomach, it ferments. Bacteria break it down and produce sulfur gas. That gas comes up as burps. It also pushes down and creates bloating. And the slowdown itself is why things don't move through — that's the constipation.

Every single thing I was taking — the Gas-X, the charcoal, the peppermint oil, every one of them — was designed to work in the colon. Downstream. At the end of the process. But my problem wasn't at the end. It was at the beginning. My stomach wasn't moving.

I was standing at the end of a clogged pipe trying to unclog it from the wrong direction. For eleven months.

I sat with that for a minute. I actually said "you've got to be kidding me" out loud in my kitchen at two in the morning.

The article mentioned something called prokinetic compounds. Things that actually encourage the stomach muscles to contract again. Not laxatives, which push from below. Prokinetics, which wake up the muscles that GLP-1s are putting to sleep. The article specifically mentioned apigenin, a compound found in high concentrations in celery, as having natural prokinetic activity.

And that's how I came across something I never would have found in the supplement aisle. It's called Motilli. It's a celery juice fiber gummy. It has the apigenin from celery juice extract, which is the prokinetic piece. It has chlorophyll, which I learned is what actually neutralizes sulfur compounds — not charcoal, which just absorbs general gas in the intestine and usually the sulfur has already been released by then. And it has a micro-dose of prebiotic fiber and a handful of vitamins — A, C, K, B6, folate — that I later learned GLP-1 patients are often depleted in because when your stomach isn't processing food properly, you're not absorbing nutrients properly either.

I ordered it that night. I want to tell you it worked immediately. It didn't.

The first three days I felt nothing and thought here we go again. Day four I realized I hadn't reached for the Gas-X tin at lunch. Not because I decided not to. Because I forgot. That had never happened.

By the end of the first week the sulfur burps were still there but they were softer. Less frequent. I went from six or seven a day to maybe two. And the two that came weren't the rotten egg kind that make you cup your hand over your mouth. They were just regular burps.

Then week two hit me like a wall. I had a terrible day on Tuesday. Sulfur burps came roaring back. Bloating so bad I changed pants at lunchtime. I sat in my car in the parking lot at work and thought, of course. Of course this was too good to be true. I almost stopped taking them that night.

But Wednesday was better. And Thursday was better than Wednesday. And by Friday the burps had settled back down to where they'd been at the end of week one. My body was adjusting. It wasn't a straight line. It was a bad day inside a good trend, and the good trend held.

By week three the bloating cycle broke. I don't mean it got a little better. I mean the evening bloat that made me change into elastic waistband pants every night at six o'clock just stopped being a thing. I ate dinner at my normal table in my normal clothes and I cleaned up the kitchen without unbuttoning anything.

The constipation took the longest. It was probably five weeks before I had what I'd call a normal routine again. But it came. And it stayed.

It's been four months now. I cleaned out my purse last week. I kept one roll of regular mints because I like mints. Everything else went into the trash. The charcoal. The ginger chews. The Gas-X tin I've been refilling since last January. The contact lens case with the Pepto.

My purse weighs what a purse should weigh.

My husband noticed before I said anything. He picked up my purse off the counter to move it and he stopped and looked at it and looked at me. He said, "It's light." I said, "I cleaned it out." And he got this look on his face that I can only describe as relief. Like he'd been carrying the weight of that purse too, in his own way, for eleven months.

When my niece gets married in June I'm going to sit at that table and eat the dinner. My husband is going to be next to me. And I'm not going to have a single thing in my purse except my phone, my lipstick, and my keys. He's going to reach for my hand under the table and I'm not going to flinch or angle away because I'm scared of what my body might do. I'm just going to hold his hand and watch my niece dance with her husband and be there. Fully there. In my body. At the table. With nothing to manage and nothing to hide.

If your purse looks like mine did — if you're carrying six things that don't work because nobody told you why they can't — maybe the problem isn't that you haven't found the right remedy.

Maybe you've been looking in the wrong part of your body.

## AD 8B: "The SIBO Shield" (REVISED)

She told me to prepare my gut before the first injection

My gastroenterologist said something that changed how I think about starting Mounjaro.

She said, "The patients who do best on GLP-1s are the ones who support their gut BEFORE their first dose. Not after the side effects start."

I have SIBO. I've been terrified of starting. She told me exactly what to do first.

I need to back up. Because if you have SIBO and you're about to start a GLP-1, what I learned might save you from the worst six months of your life.

I was diagnosed with SIBO in 2021. Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth. If you know, you know. If you don't, imagine every meal feeling like it's sitting in your stomach rotting. Bloating so bad you look six months pregnant by dinner. Gas that clears a room. Cycles of constipation and diarrhea that make you afraid to leave the house without mapping every bathroom on your route.

It took me two years to get it under control. Two rounds of Rifaximin. A low-FODMAP diet that made me cry in grocery stores. A prokinetic that finally got my motility moving again. Careful, deliberate, exhausting work to get my gut to a place where I could function.

Then my doctor brought up Mounjaro for my weight.

And I wanted to say yes. I really did. I've watched friends drop thirty, forty, fifty pounds. I've seen the energy come back into their faces. I know this medication works. My A1C was creeping up. My knees hurt every morning. I needed this.

But the first thing I did was go home and read every SIBO forum I could find.

And what I read made me sick.

Woman after woman with SIBO saying the GLP-1 destroyed everything they'd built. Motility ground to a halt. Bacterial overgrowth came roaring back. Sulfur burps so bad they couldn't go to work. Constipation lasting weeks. One woman said she spent eleven days without a bowel movement and ended up in the ER.

These weren't women with sensitive stomachs. These were women who already had a motility disorder. And they were putting a medication on top of it that slows the stomach even further.

I almost didn't start. I almost threw the prescription away.

But my gastroenterologist — and I thank God for this woman every day — she didn't just say "start it and see what happens." She didn't hand me a prescription and wish me luck. She actually thought about what a GLP-1 would do to a gut that was already compromised.

She explained it to me like this.

Your stomach has a cleaning wave. It's called the migrating motor complex. It's the muscular contraction that sweeps bacteria and food debris out of your small intestine between meals. In SIBO patients, that wave is already weak. That's part of why we get overgrowth in the first place. Bacteria accumulate because the broom isn't sweeping.

I stopped her there. "So you're telling me the one thing keeping my SIBO in check is already barely working?"

She nodded.

GLP-1 medications work by slowing gastric emptying. That's how they reduce appetite. But slowing gastric emptying also weakens that cleaning wave even further.

"And I'm about to take something that slows it down more," I said.

She said, "That's why we prepare first."

In a normal gut, she explained, the slowdown causes some mild side effects. Nausea. A little constipation. Nothing terrible. In a SIBO gut, it's like turning off the only defense you have against bacterial overgrowth. You're taking a system that was already barely functioning and you're suppressing it even more.

I felt my chest tighten. I'd spent two years rebuilding something fragile and now I was being told the medication I needed could tear it down in weeks.

She must have seen it on my face because she leaned forward and said, "This is why I'm telling you now. Before you start. Not after."

She said most doctors don't think about this. They prescribe the GLP-1, the patient comes back eight weeks later with terrible GI symptoms, and the doctor says "that's normal, give it time." But for a SIBO patient, those aren't startup side effects. That's a full relapse.

"And every week you wait," she said, "is a week the bacteria are multiplying in a stomach that can't move them out."

I wanted to ask how she knew all this. How a gastroenterologist in a busy practice had figured out something the forums were full of women suffering through. She told me she'd had three SIBO patients start GLP-1s the previous year without preparation. All three relapsed. She said watching them lose what they'd fought so hard to build changed how she approached every SIBO patient after that.

She told me what she now does with every SIBO patient before they start a GLP-1.

She supports gastric motility first.

Not colon motility. Not downstream. Not Miralax or Dulcolax or fiber supplements. Those work on the large intestine. The problem for SIBO patients on GLP-1s is in the stomach and small intestine. You need something that wakes up the upper GI tract. Something that supports the migrating motor complex even while the medication is trying to slow it down.

She mentioned prokinetic compounds. Specifically, she talked about natural prokinetics because many of her patients can't tolerate prescription prokinetics long-term. She said there's a compound in celery called apigenin that acts as a natural prokinetic. It stimulates the smooth muscle contractions in the stomach wall. Not aggressively, not like a stimulant laxative. Gently. In a way that supports the cleaning wave without fighting the medication.

She also talked about what happens to the gas. Because SIBO patients already produce excess hydrogen and methane. Add a GLP-1 slowing everything down, and that gas has nowhere to go. It builds up. The sulfur burps get unbearable. The bloating becomes constant.

She said chlorophyll — the green pigment in plants — neutralizes sulfur compounds. It doesn't stop the gas from forming. It changes the chemistry of the gas so it doesn't smell and doesn't cause that rotten-egg belching that makes you afraid to open your mouth around other people.

And then the nutrition piece. SIBO patients already have absorption problems. We don't extract nutrients from food efficiently. Add a GLP-1 that cuts your appetite in half, and you're absorbing even less from even less food. She said the vitamins that drop fastest are A, C, K, and the B vitamins. The ones your gut lining needs to stay intact. The ones your immune system needs to keep the bacterial balance in check.

She said if she could put all of this — the prokinetic, the chlorophyll, and the key vitamins — into one thing her patients would actually take every day, it would change how SIBO patients experience GLP-1s.

Then she showed me what she'd been recommending.

It was a gummy. A celery juice fiber gummy called Motilli. Pectin-based, which matters because pectin is a prebiotic fiber that feeds beneficial bacteria without feeding the SIBO bacteria the way psyllium or inulin can. It had the celery juice extract with the apigenin. It had the chlorophyll. It had vitamins A, C, K, B6, and folate.

I was skeptical. I have been burned by so many supplements. So many things that promised to help SIBO and made it worse. I asked her if she'd actually seen results.

She said she'd been using it as part of her pre-GLP-1 protocol for about four months. She was having patients start it two weeks before their first injection. And the difference between those patients and the ones who started without it was significant enough that she now considers it standard preparation.

I started taking it fourteen days before my first Mounjaro injection. Two gummies a day. They tasted like green apple. They didn't upset my stomach. I didn't bloat from them. That alone was remarkable for a SIBO supplement.

Then I took my first dose.

Injection day was a Thursday. I did it in the morning, in my bathroom, the way the nurse had shown me. Then I sat on the edge of my bed and waited. I know that sounds dramatic but if you have SIBO you understand. Every new thing that enters your system is a potential threat. Every supplement, every medication, every food you haven't tested is a coin flip between okay and catastrophe.

I waited for the wave of nausea. I ate a small lunch. I waited for the sulfur burps that the forums had warned me about. I went to bed that night with my hand on my stomach like I was checking a pulse.

Friday morning I woke up and lay there for a minute, scanning. A little nauseous. Appetite was down, which is the medication working. But my stomach was moving. I could feel it. That gentle rolling sensation after I drank water that tells you the muscles are working. The cleaning wave was still sweeping.

I went to the bathroom that morning like it was any other morning. I almost laughed. All those forum posts. All those women describing the apocalypse of their first week on a GLP-1 with SIBO. And I was having a normal Friday.

The first week was not perfect. I had mild nausea for two days. My appetite dropped significantly. But I was still having bowel movements. The cleaning wave was still doing its job.

By week three I was tolerating the medication better than people without SIBO were describing. I read the forums with a different kind of emotion now. Not fear. Something like relief mixed with sadness. Relief that I'd had a doctor who thought ahead. Sadness for the women who didn't.

I'm four months in. I've lost twenty-two pounds. My A1C dropped a full point. My SIBO has not relapsed. I repeat that because I need you to hear it. My SIBO has not relapsed on a GLP-1.

I still take the Motilli every morning. I will take it as long as I'm on this medication. It is as non-negotiable as the injection itself.

I wonder how many of them quit their medication because nobody prepared them. If you have SIBO and you're about to start a GLP-1, please don't just start it cold. Please don't let your doctor tell you to "try it and see." Your gut is not the same as someone without a motility disorder. The standard advice does not apply to you.

Support your gut before you challenge it. Protect what you built before you put it at risk. You fought too hard for your stability to lose it because nobody thought to prepare you.

## AD COPY

**Headline:** She cooked Thanksgiving for 31 years and then one November she couldn't

My granddaughter asked why I wasn't eating and I couldn't answer her.

I haven't made my grandmother's lasagna since August.

I have the recipe memorized. Every layer. Every pinch of oregano that my grandmother measured with her fingers because she never owned a measuring spoon. She passed it to my mother and my mother passed it to me and I have made it from memory for forty years.

I can't be in the kitchen long enough to make it. The smell of the meat sauce browning makes my stomach rise into my throat before the first pound of beef hits the pan.

I need to tell you about last Thanksgiving. Because I think some of you will understand what happened to me in a way that nobody at that table did.

I have cooked Thanksgiving dinner for my family for thirty-one years. Since 1993. The year my mother looked at me across her own kitchen and said I was ready. She handed me her roasting pan — the dark one with the handles worn smooth — and I felt the weight of it in my hands and I understood what she was giving me. Not a pan. A position. This is who you are now. You are the woman who feeds this family.

For thirty-one years I was that woman. Not just at Thanksgiving. At every birthday. Every Easter. Every Sunday when enough people were in town to fill my table. I was in the kitchen by six with coffee going and flour on the counter and a plan in my head that ran like clockwork. I made my rolls from scratch. I brined the turkey two days out. I made four pies because different people like different kinds and I wanted every single person at my table to have their favorite sitting in front of them. That's not a chore. That's a language. Food was how I said I love you. I grew up in a family that didn't say it out loud. We said it with a second helping. With remembering that your nephew liked extra gravy. With making a separate small dish of mashed potatoes without garlic for the one grandchild who complained about it in 2004 and I never once forgot.

I started Wegovy in June. I needed to. My doctor had talked to me about my blood sugar for three years running. My knees hurt when I stood up from a chair. I was sixty-one and I felt like I was borrowing time from a body that was done being patient with me. I knew the medication could help and I wanted the help.

The weight started coming off and I was grateful. Twelve pounds the first month. My rings were loose. My daughter told me my face looked thinner and I smiled because I could see it too.

But something else was happening that I did not talk about.

I couldn't eat.

Not just less hungry. I could not put food in my mouth without my body treating it like poison. The nausea started in week two and it did not leave. It wasn't the kind that comes in a wave and passes. It was a low constant hum of revulsion that sat behind my ribs all day. Like my stomach had decided that food was the enemy and it was going to punish me for every bite I tried to take.

The sulfur burps started in week four. I don't know how to explain them to someone who hasn't tasted one. Imagine something rotten rising from deep in your chest, filling your mouth, your throat, your nose with a sulfur taste so thick you can feel it coat your teeth. Now imagine that happening ten times a day. At the table. In the car with the windows up. In the middle of talking to your seven-year-old granddaughter who is sitting close enough to smell your breath.

I started carrying mints everywhere. I chewed gum until my jaw ached. I turned my face when I talked to people. I stopped leaning in to hug my grandchildren because I was terrified of what they'd smell.

And the constipation. I had been regular my entire life. Sixty-one years. Suddenly I was going five days. Six days. Seven days with nothing. My stomach was distended and hard and I would lie in bed at night pressing on it, trying to feel something move. Trying to feel anything happening in there. Nothing.

I mixed Miralax into water every night. Nothing. I tried Dulcolax and the cramps doubled me over but nothing moved. My doctor prescribed Colace. Same result. A woman in my GLP-1 group on Facebook recommended Mag07. Another one swore by psyllium husk. Someone else said enemas.

I tried every single one. I was willing to try anything because I needed my body to work the way it used to work.

None of it worked. And I didn't understand why.

Meanwhile my kitchen was going quiet.

In July I tried to make my blueberry cobbler for the Fourth. I got the berries washed and into the dish and the butter mixture done and when I opened the oven door, the heat and the smell hit me together and I had to walk outside and sit on the porch with my head between my knees.

My husband finished it. He didn't say a word. He brought it to the party and told everyone I made it. But I saw him watching me from across the yard and I could see it on his face. The worry he was trying not to show me.

In September my daughter asked if I'd make my cinnamon rolls for her son's birthday. I said yes. How do you say no to that. I got up at five in the morning and stood in the kitchen looking at the flour and the yeast and the butter and I felt nothing. No excitement. No warmth. Just the flat certainty that the smell of yeast rising was going to make me gag.

I made them. It took me four hours for something that used to take ninety minutes. I did it in shifts. Mix. Leave the kitchen. Sit down. Wait for the nausea to pass. Go back. Roll. Leave again. Wait again. Go back. My grandson ate three of them and told me they were the best he'd ever had and I smiled at him and inside I was losing something I couldn't name yet.

The stove that I had stood at since I was twenty-five years old. The oven I had opened ten thousand times. The counter where I rolled dough with my mother's rolling pin. These were not just appliances. These were the places where I was myself. And one by one they were becoming places I couldn't stand long enough to use.

Then came November.

My daughter called on November third. Same call she's made every year for fifteen years. Same voice. "So what time should we come over on Thanksgiving?"

And I said, "I don't think I can do it this year."

The quiet on the phone lasted so long I checked to see if the call had dropped.

She didn't argue. She didn't make me feel bad. She said okay and she offered to host and I said that would be fine. And we hung up. And I sat at my kitchen table — my cold, quiet kitchen table that used to be dusted with flour — and I cried for forty minutes. Not gently. The kind where your chest heaves and you can't get a full breath and sounds come out of you that don't sound like your own voice.

I went to my daughter's house for Thanksgiving. I brought a store-bought pie. I carried it in with both hands and set it on the counter next to the food and I hoped nobody would notice that it came from a box. My mother would have come back from the dead to have words with me.

The turkey smelled the way Thanksgiving always smells and I had to excuse myself twice to go to the bathroom because the sulfur burps were so bad I could not sit at the table. I ran the faucet so nobody would hear me.

I put food on my plate because people were watching. A small piece of turkey. A spoonful of potatoes. A roll I didn't make. I pushed it around. I took tiny bites and chewed slowly and swallowed against the nausea and tried to look like a woman who was fine.

My granddaughter is seven. She watched me the entire meal. Children see what adults have learned not to see. They don't filter it through politeness. They just watch, and then they say exactly what they see.

She put her fork down and she said, "Grandma, why aren't you eating?"

The whole table went quiet.

I said I wasn't very hungry, sweetheart.

She said, "But you always eat a lot at Thanksgiving."

My daughter changed the subject. Someone passed the cranberry sauce. The moment ended for everyone at that table except me. I sat there with a rock in my chest and a smile I was holding in place with everything I had and I thought: this medication is saving my life and it is taking me apart at the same time.

I went home that night and sat in my kitchen. The kitchen that used to smell like bread and butter and garlic and onion. It smelled like nothing. The stove was cold. The oven was cold. I was a woman who used to fill this room with warmth and food and love and now I sat in it like a stranger.

I told my husband I was thinking about stopping the Wegovy.

He looked at me and he said, "Don't quit the medication. Let's figure out the side effects."

And I looked at him like he'd just told me to figure out gravity. I had tried everything. Every powder and every pill and every capsule that every woman in every Facebook group had ever mentioned. Nothing worked. Nothing even helped.

But he's a stubborn man and he loves me more than he knows how to say and he took his phone into the living room that night and he went down a rabbit hole I didn't know existed. I went to bed. I was too tired to hope for anything.

At eleven-thirty he came into the bedroom. Reading glasses on. Phone in his hand. And he said, "I think I know why none of it worked."

He sat on the edge of the bed and he explained it to me in the simplest way he could.

The medication slows the stomach. That's how it works. It slows down the stomach muscles so food stays in there longer and you don't feel hungry. But when the stomach slows down too much, food just sits. It doesn't move. And when food sits in a warm stomach with nowhere to go, bacteria start breaking it down. That's what produces the sulfur gas. That's the rotten-egg burps. That's the nausea. The stomach is full of food that's fermenting because the muscles aren't pushing it through.

And everything I had tried — the Miralax, the Dulcolax, the Colace, the Mag07, the fiber, the enemas — all of it works on the colon. The large intestine. Downstream. But the problem wasn't downstream. The problem was in my stomach. Upstream. The muscles in my stomach had been slowed down by the medication and everything I was throwing at the problem was landing in the wrong place entirely.

I was treating the wrong part of my body. For six months.

Every single thing I tried was going to the wrong address.

He said what the stomach actually needed was something to wake those muscles back up. Not a laxative. Not more fiber. Something that gently stimulates the stomach wall to contract and move food through the way it's supposed to. He called it a natural prokinetic. He said there's a compound in celery — apigenin — that does exactly that. It nudges the stomach muscles without fighting the medication.

And the sulfur gas — the burps that had kept me from sitting at my own Thanksgiving table — he said chlorophyll neutralizes the sulfur compounds. Doesn't stop them from forming. Changes their chemistry so they don't rise up tasting like something died in your chest.

And then the piece that I hadn't even thought about. Because I was eating so little — a few bites here, half a meal there — my body wasn't getting the vitamins it needed. A, C, K, B6, folate. The things your body needs to function and I was getting almost none of them because I could barely eat.

He showed me something he'd found while reading a forum thread about stomach motility. A woman had mentioned a celery juice gummy that had all of it — the apigenin for the stomach muscles, the chlorophyll for the sulfur, and the vitamins. It was called Motilli. Pectin-based, which meant it was gentle. Third-party tested. Clean Label Certified.

I told him I didn't believe in gummies. He said he didn't either. He said just try it.

I ordered it at midnight. Like a woman with nothing left to lose. Which is exactly what I was.

It arrived on a Tuesday. I took two. They tasted like green apple. My stomach didn't reject them. That alone surprised me.

By Thursday the sulfur burps were fewer. Not gone. But instead of ten a day, maybe four. And weaker. Less of that thick rotten taste filling my throat.

By the end of the first week, I had a bowel movement without any laxative. It wasn't dramatic. But something was moving. Something was waking up.

I did not let myself believe it yet.

By week three, the nausea was lifting. The constant hum behind my ribs was fading. I ate a full small lunch and my body did not fight me. The sulfur burps were down to one or two a day. Mild. The kind you could live with. Not the kind that sent you running from a Thanksgiving table.

By week six, I did something I had not done since August.

I went into the kitchen on a Saturday morning. Early. Before anyone was up. I took out a pot. I browned a pound of beef with garlic and onion and I stood over the stove and I breathed in.

The smell of my grandmother's meat sauce hit me and I did not feel sick.

I cried standing at that stove. Not the tears from the kitchen table in November. Different ones. The kind that come when something you were sure was gone forever walks back into your life.

I made the lasagna. The whole thing. Every layer. My grandmother's recipe from memory. I did not have to leave the kitchen once. My husband came downstairs and saw me standing at the stove with sauce on my apron and he stopped in the doorway. He didn't say a word. He poured his coffee and sat at the table and watched me cook and his eyes were wet and we didn't need to say anything.

My granddaughter ate two pieces. She looked up at me with sauce on her chin and said, "Grandma, you made the good dinner."

The good dinner. Yes baby. Grandma made the good dinner.

I'm still on Wegovy. I want to say that clearly. I am still on my medication and I am grateful for what it's doing for me. Thirty-four pounds gone. Blood sugar normal for the first time in years. My knees don't ache when I get out of bed.

And I still take the Motilli every morning. Two gummies. It is the thing that gave me back my stove and my oven and my kitchen table and the look on my granddaughter's face when she bites into something I made with my own hands.

If you are the woman who stopped cooking. If you are sitting in a kitchen that used to be warm and alive and it's gone quiet. If you are watching your table get smaller and your plate get emptier and your family is noticing and you can't explain it to them because you can barely explain it to yourself.

The problem is not your appetite. It's your stomach. And the thing that is stealing your kitchen from you has a cause and a fix.

My grandmother's roasting pan is still in my cabinet. It's mine. And I'm not done using it.

Thanksgiving is ten months away. I'm already planning the menu. Four pies. Scratch rolls. A twenty-pound turkey brined for two days.

And my grandmother's lasagna on Christmas Eve. Because that recipe did not survive three generations to die with me.

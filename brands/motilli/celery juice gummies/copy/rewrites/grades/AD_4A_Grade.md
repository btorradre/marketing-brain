# AD 4A GRADE: "The Symptom Stack Nobody Connects"

---

## SUMMARY

**Copy assessed:** AD 4A Rewrite -- "The five symptoms your doctor is treating separately that started together"
**Word count:** ~2,400
**Narrator:** Rachel Odom, functional nutritionist, 14 years practice, Scottsdale. First-person. Converted Specialist archetype.
**Awareness level targeted:** Problem Aware
**Cause-and-effect chain:** GLP-1 slows gastric motility --> mechanical breakdown drops --> nutrient extraction fails --> stored reserves deplete in survival priority order (iron/ferritin --> zinc --> fat-soluble A/C/E --> B12/folate --> combined B12/folate/iron). Plain causal chain. No named framework detected.

## DIMENSION 1: BELIEF-SHIFT ARCHITECTURE (20/25)

### Belief Shift 1 -- "My suffering is real": 4/5

**Evidence:** Five mirror moments delivered through Margaret's monthly cascade:
- "She could see scalp when she parted her hair under the bathroom light"
- "her nail tech mentioned her nails were softer. Peeling at the edges"
- "Like someone had turned the saturation down on her face"
- "on the couch by two in the afternoon, waking up an hour later not refreshed but heavier"
- "lost the word refrigerator. She stood in her kitchen repeating 'the -- the -- the cold thing where the food goes'"

**Assessment:** Five specific mirror moments with life consequences -- the daughter going quiet on the phone, the nail tech noticing, the birthday photo. These are surgically specific. The refrigerator scene is the strongest -- it captures the exact internal experience of word-finding failure. The reason this doesn't reach a 5: each mirror moment is observed from Rachel's clinical distance, not from inside Margaret's head. We see Margaret's symptoms as a clinician catalogs them. The scenes describe what happened TO Margaret but rarely drop us inside her felt experience in the moment. The ponytail thinning is reported ("she noticed her ponytail was thinner") rather than lived. A reader might recognize these scenes but wouldn't screenshot them with "this is literally me" because the narrator is a practitioner retelling, not the sufferer experiencing. That clinical remove costs it one point.

### Belief Shift 2 -- "My approach is wrong about something": 5/5

**Evidence:** The trust vacuum is built through witnessing across five specialists, none villainized:
- "Her doctor said telogen effluvium. Weight loss hair shedding. Very common. It would grow back."
- "The dermatologist put her on retinol, vitamin C serum, and a hydrating mask. Two hundred and forty dollars."
- "Her doctor ran a thyroid panel. Normal. CBC. Normal. He suggested she might need more sleep."
- "She had five things wrong and five different doctors looking at five different pieces and not one of them had stepped back far enough to ask the obvious question."

**Assessment:** This is the strongest belief shift in the piece. Each doctor is shown as competent within their silo -- the endocrinologist IS correct that the medication is working, the thyroid panel IS normal. Nobody is villainized. The trust vacuum forms through accumulation: five well-meaning experts, five isolated treatments, zero pattern recognition. The neurologist waiting room scene -- Margaret crying not because she thinks she has dementia but because nobody is connecting the dots -- is the crisis moment that makes the reader furious on Margaret's behalf without a single editorial sentence telling her to be. The copy shows and never tells. Full marks.

### Belief Shift 3 -- "The problem is in the stomach": 4/5

**Evidence:** The cause-and-effect chain is delivered cleanly through Rachel's clinical explanation:
- "Her medication works by slowing her stomach. That's the mechanism."
- "it reduces nutrient extraction. The stomach muscles that mechanically break food down...those muscles are sluggish"
- "Not all at once. In a specific order. Based on how fast the body burns through its stored reserves."
- Then the month-by-month depletion cascade mapped back to Margaret's timeline.

Villain teardowns are tight:
- "The retinol the dermatologist prescribed treats the skin surface. Margaret's skin isn't failing at the surface."
- "The hair vitamins she ordered add nutrients to a system that can't process them."
- "The B12 protocol addresses one depleted vitamin while five others are equally low."

**Assessment:** The mechanism is clean and the cause-and-effect chain is plain language. The villain teardowns are 1-2 sentences each and reference the causal chain directly. The month-by-month depletion cascade mapped back to Margaret's own timeline is effective architecture -- the reader has already FELT each stage before the mechanism explains WHY each stage happened. However, there is a minor issue: the mechanism section carries slight emotional weight. Sentences like "That's the fog. That's the lost words. That's the chickpeas." reintroduce emotional beats inside the mechanism teaching. This creates a small dual-track bleed -- the mechanism section should be calm explanation in contrast to the visceral storytelling before it. The line "That's the chickpeas" is a callback that adds emotional resonance where the mechanism should be pure causal chain. It works as writing but technically violates the principle that mechanism carries zero emotional weight. One point deducted.

### Belief Shift 4 -- "The solution needs A+B+C": 4/5

**Evidence:** Three solution components established before product:
- Motility support: "Something that woke up the stomach muscles enough to restore mechanical breakdown"
- Sulfur neutralization: implied through the rotten egg burps / bacterial fermentation mention
- Targeted nutrient repletion: "the specific nutrients that deplete first in the cascade I described"

Product discovery: "About eight months ago, I came across a formulation through a colleague who works exclusively with GLP-1 patients."
Skepticism bridge: "She'd been testing it with her own patients and tracking their micronutrient panels before and after."
Confirmation beat: Apigenin as prokinetic, chlorophyll for sulfur, specific vitamins A/C/K/B6/folate, gummy format dissolves on chewing.

**Assessment:** The UMS criteria are established before the product enters, and the product is checked against them. The discovery is sideways (through a colleague), which is the correct pattern. However, the components are not discovered individually with narrative momentum between each. They arrive as a bundled requirement: "Something that addressed the motility itself. Something that woke up the stomach muscles." The three UMS components (motility, sulfur, nutrients) are really discovered as one insight ("fix the stomach") rather than three individually discovered criteria. The reader doesn't build a checklist piece by piece -- they receive a package. Additionally, the confirmation beat runs slightly long at approximately 8 sentences (from "It contains celery juice extract" through the vitamin list), exceeding the 3-5 sentence target. One point deducted.

### Belief Shift 5 -- "This works and I keep what I value": 3/5

**Evidence:** Progressive timeline present:
- Week 2: "talked to her daughter on the phone for forty-five minutes without a single blank"
- Week 3: "made it past four in the afternoon without the couch"
- Week 5: "back to walking her dog in the morning"
- Week 6: "Stronger. Less peeling. Growing at a normal rate."
- Month 3: "her face looked like her face again in photos"
- Month 4: "her ponytail felt heavier in her hand"
- Week 12: Panel confirmation.

Pain-to-payoff callback: The phone call with her daughter directly resolves the "lost the word refrigerator" scene. The ponytail resolves the hair thinning. The nail tech resolves the nail scene.

**Assessment:** The progressive timeline is solid and the pain-to-payoff callbacks work -- particularly the daughter phone call resolving the refrigerator scene. However, there are two problems. First, skepticism is largely absent during the results section. Rachel says "I don't promise my patients fast results" before the results begin, but there's no skepticism AT each stage -- no "she wasn't sure if it was placebo," no hesitation, no moment where Margaret doubts. The results feel like a smooth upward trajectory without friction. Second, the close does not end on a life image. It ends on statements: "You can keep seeing five specialists for five symptoms that will never resolve independently. Or you can address the one upstream cause that's producing all of them." Then a guarantee mention, then a command: "Stop chasing five answers for one problem." This is a philosophical binary resolved through argument/statement, not through emotional weight or a closing image the reader fills with her own meaning. The close shifts slightly toward marketing voice with the guarantee and the imperative. Two points deducted.

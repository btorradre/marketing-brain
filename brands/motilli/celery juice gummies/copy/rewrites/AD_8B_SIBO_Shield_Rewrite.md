## AD COPY

**Headline:** She spent two years rebuilding her gut and almost lost it in one week

---

She told me to prepare my gut before the first injection

I sat across from my gastroenterologist with a Mounjaro prescription in my hand and a knot in my chest so tight I could feel my pulse in my throat.

She could see it. She leaned forward and said six words I will never forget.

"We support your gut first. Not after."

I need to explain why those words mattered so much. Why I almost cried in that office. Why a simple instruction about timing changed everything about how the next four months of my life went.

I have SIBO. Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth. I was diagnosed in 2021 and if you know what that means you already flinched. If you don't, imagine every meal sitting in your stomach like it's rotting from the inside. Bloating so severe your abdomen goes rigid by dinner. Gas that empties rooms. Cycles of constipation and diarrhea so unpredictable you memorize the location of every public bathroom within a fifteen-minute radius of your house, your office, and your mother's apartment.

It took me two years to claw my way out of that.

Two rounds of Rifaximin. A low-FODMAP diet that had me standing in grocery store aisles crying because I couldn't eat bread, couldn't eat garlic, couldn't eat an apple. A prescription prokinetic that my insurance fought me on for three months before they finally approved it.

Careful. Deliberate. Exhausting work.

I rebuilt my gut the way you'd rebuild a house after a flood. One wall at a time. Testing every material. Never rushing. Because rushing is how you end up back where you started and I could not go back there.

By early 2024 I was stable. Not perfect. Stable. I could eat at restaurants again. I could travel without packing an emergency kit. I could sit through a two-hour meeting without calculating escape routes to the bathroom. I was functioning. For someone with SIBO, functioning is the whole victory.

Then my primary care doctor brought up Mounjaro.

My A1C was creeping. My knees ached every morning getting out of bed. I'd watched friends lose thirty, forty pounds on these medications and I saw the energy come back into their faces. I wanted that. I needed it.

But the first thing I did when I got home was open every SIBO forum I could find.

I typed "SIBO and GLP-1" and what came back made me put my phone down and press my hands flat on the kitchen counter to stop them from shaking.

Woman after woman after woman saying the same thing. The GLP-1 destroyed what they'd built. Motility stopped. Bacterial overgrowth roared back. One woman said she'd been in remission for three years and relapsed within six weeks of her first injection. Another said she spent eleven days without a bowel movement and ended up in the ER. Another said the sulfur burps were so bad she couldn't go to work and her boss thought she was making excuses to stay home.

These weren't women with sensitive stomachs complaining about a little nausea. These were women who'd already fought the war I'd fought. Who'd done the Rifaximin. Who'd survived the diet. Who'd rebuilt their guts piece by piece the way I had. And then a medication came along and knocked all of it down in weeks.

I sat at my kitchen table reading those posts for two hours. My husband came in and asked what was wrong and I said nothing because I couldn't explain the particular fear of watching other people lose the thing you spent two years earning. I couldn't explain what it feels like to hold a prescription that could fix your knees and your blood sugar and your energy and your weight and know that the same prescription might take away the only stability your gut has ever had.

I almost threw it away. The prescription. I almost tore it up and decided the weight and the A1C and the knees were a price I'd pay to keep my gut in one piece.

That's the state I was in when I went back to my gastroenterologist. I told her I'd been prescribed Mounjaro. I told her about the forums. I told her I was terrified.

She didn't dismiss it. She didn't say "just try it and see what happens." She didn't hand me a pamphlet. She actually sat with it. And then she explained something to me that no one on those forums had ever mentioned.

Your gut has a cleaning system, she said. Between meals, the muscles in your stomach and small intestine do a sweeping contraction. Like a broom pushing debris through the hallway. It clears bacteria, it clears food particles, it keeps everything moving through so nothing accumulates.

In SIBO patients, that sweeping action is already weak. That's part of why we get overgrowth. The broom barely works. Bacteria collect because nothing is pushing them out.

I stopped her. "So the one thing keeping my SIBO in check is already barely functioning."

She nodded.

Now. GLP-1 medications work by slowing the stomach down. That's how they kill appetite. You feel full because food sits longer. That slowdown is the mechanism. It's not a side effect. It's the whole point.

But slowing the stomach also weakens that sweeping contraction even further.

"And I'm about to take something that slows it down more," I said.

"That's why we prepare first."

She explained it plainly. In a normal gut, the slowdown causes mild side effects. A little nausea. Some constipation. Manageable. In a SIBO gut, it's like turning off the only defense system you have against bacterial overgrowth. You're taking a system that was already limping and you're putting a weight on its back.

She told me most doctors don't think about this. They prescribe the GLP-1 and when the patient comes back eight weeks later with terrible symptoms they say "that's normal, give it time." But for someone with SIBO those aren't startup side effects. That's a relapse. And every week you wait is a week the bacteria are multiplying in a system that can't move them out.

I asked what she does differently. She said she'd had three SIBO patients start GLP-1s the year before without any preparation. All three relapsed. She said watching them lose what they'd spent years building changed how she handles every SIBO patient after that.

Now she supports the stomach's sweeping action before the medication ever starts.

Not the colon. Not downstream. Not Miralax or fiber supplements or anything that works at the end of the system. The problem for SIBO patients on GLP-1s starts in the stomach and the small intestine. You need something that supports the upper part of the system. Something that keeps that sweeping contraction going even while the medication is trying to slow everything down.

She talked about a compound in celery called apigenin. A natural prokinetic. It nudges the smooth muscle contractions in the stomach wall. Not aggressively. Not like a stimulant laxative that forces things through. Gently. In a way that supports the sweeping action without working against the medication.

Then the gas. SIBO patients already produce excess hydrogen and methane. Add a GLP-1 slowing everything down and that gas has nowhere to go. Sulfur builds up. The burps become unbearable. Chlorophyll, she said, neutralizes sulfur compounds. Doesn't stop gas from forming. Changes the chemistry so it doesn't come up as that rotten-egg belch that makes you afraid to open your mouth around other people.

And the nutrition piece. SIBO patients already absorb nutrients poorly. We don't extract what we need from food efficiently. Put a GLP-1 on top and you're absorbing even less from even less food because your appetite is cut in half. The vitamins that drop first are A, C, K, and the B vitamins. The ones your gut lining needs to stay intact. The ones your immune system needs to keep the bacterial balance from tipping.

She said if she could give her SIBO patients one thing that combined all three pieces — the prokinetic support, the sulfur neutralizer, and the key vitamins — and have them start it before their first injection, it would change how SIBO patients experience GLP-1s.

Then she told me what she'd been recommending.

A celery juice fiber gummy called Motilli. Pectin-based, which mattered to me because pectin is a prebiotic fiber that feeds beneficial bacteria without feeding the SIBO bacteria the way psyllium or inulin can. It had the celery juice extract with the apigenin. It had the chlorophyll. It had vitamins A, C, K, B6, and folate. And the fiber was a micro-dose. Not a Metamucil-size serving that would add bulk to an already compromised system. Just enough to support recovery without creating more backup.

I was skeptical. I have been burned by more supplements than I can count. Things that promised to help SIBO and made it worse. I asked her if she'd actually seen results.

She said she'd been having patients start it two weeks before their first injection. And the difference between those patients and the ones who started without it was significant enough that she now considers it standard preparation.

I started taking it fourteen days before my first Mounjaro dose. Two gummies a day. They tasted like green apple. They didn't upset my stomach. They didn't trigger bloating. For someone with SIBO, a supplement that doesn't make things worse on day one is already beating the odds.

Then I took my first injection.

It was a Thursday morning. I did it in my bathroom the way the nurse had shown me. Then I sat on the edge of the tub and waited. If you have SIBO you understand this. Every new thing that enters your system is a potential threat. Every medication, every food you haven't tested, every supplement is a coin flip between fine and catastrophe.

I waited for the nausea. I ate a small lunch. I waited for the sulfur burps the forums had warned me about. I went to bed that night with my hand on my stomach like I was monitoring a sleeping infant.

Friday morning I lay still for a full minute before I opened my eyes. Scanning. A little nauseous. Appetite was down, which is the medication doing its job. But my stomach was moving. I could feel that gentle rolling sensation after I drank water. The sweeping action was still happening.

I went to the bathroom that morning the way I go to the bathroom every morning.

I almost laughed. All those forum posts. All those women describing the complete collapse of their first week. And I was having a normal Friday.

The first week was not perfect. Mild nausea for two days. My appetite dropped hard. But I was still going to the bathroom. The cleaning wave was still working. The bacteria were not accumulating.

Week three. I was tolerating the medication better than women without SIBO were describing on the forums. I read those posts with a different feeling now. Not the terror I'd felt in my kitchen. Something quieter and sadder. Relief that I'd had a doctor who thought ahead. Grief for the women who didn't.

I'm four months in now. I've lost twenty-two pounds. My A1C dropped a full point.

My SIBO has not relapsed.

I need you to hear that again because I need you to understand what it means. Four months on a medication that slows the stomach. Four months on the exact drug that those forum posts told me would destroy everything I'd built. And my gut is stable. Not just surviving. Stable. The way it was before I started.

I still take the gummies every morning. I will take them as long as I'm on this medication. They are as non-negotiable to me as the injection itself. Because I know what's underneath. I know how fragile the system is. I know what happens when the sweeping action stops and the bacteria have nowhere to go and everything you rebuilt comes apart in weeks.

I think about those women. The ones whose posts I read in my kitchen at eleven o'clock at night with my hands flat on the counter. The ones who relapsed. The ones who quit their medication because nobody prepared them. The ones sitting in some forum right now telling the next woman that GLP-1s will destroy her SIBO, because that was their experience and they have no reason to believe it could have been different.

They weren't wrong about what happened to them. They were just never given the chance to prevent it.

If you have SIBO and you're about to start a GLP-1, please don't start it cold. Don't let your doctor tell you to try it and see. Your gut is not the same as someone without a motility disorder. The standard advice was not written for you.

Support the sweeping action before you challenge it. Protect what you built before you put it at risk. You fought too hard for your stability to lose it because nobody thought to prepare you.

Last Saturday I ate dinner at a restaurant with my husband. A real dinner. Pasta. Bread. A glass of wine. I didn't calculate the FODMAP content of every ingredient. I didn't map the bathroom on the way in. I didn't press my hand against my abdomen under the table to check if things were moving.

I just ate dinner. Like a person whose body isn't at war with itself.

That's what four months of keeping both — the medication and the remission — feels like. And I wish every woman on those forums could feel it too.

## AD COPY

**Headline:** The five symptoms your doctor is treating separately that started together

I've been a functional nutritionist in Scottsdale for fourteen years. In the last eighteen months, I've seen the same pattern walk into my office so many times I started keeping a separate intake form for it.

Woman in her fifties or sixties. On Ozempic or Mounjaro or Wegovy. Lost significant weight. A1C improved. Doctor is thrilled. She's thrilled. The medication is doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

And then, somewhere around month four, things start falling apart. Not all at once. One thing at a time. Each one explainable on its own. Each one sent to a different specialist. Each one treated in isolation.

Hair thinning. Nails softening. Skin going dull. Energy collapsing by mid-afternoon. And then the one that scares them. The fog. The lost words. The moment in the grocery store where they're holding something and can't remember why they picked it up.

I need to tell you about Margaret because her story is the story I hear three times a week now and I keep waiting for someone else in medicine to connect these dots and nobody is.

Margaret is sixty-two. She started Ozempic eight months before she sat in my office. Forty-one pounds lost. A1C from 8.2 to 5.9. Her endocrinologist called her a model patient. And she was. The medication was doing its job brilliantly.

Month four, she noticed her ponytail was thinner. She could see scalp when she parted her hair under the bathroom light. Her doctor said telogen effluvium. Weight loss hair shedding. Very common. It would grow back.

Month five, her nail tech mentioned her nails were softer. Peeling at the edges. Margaret switched from gel to regular polish and figured the gel removal had been too harsh.

Month six, a photo from her granddaughter's birthday. She didn't recognize her own skin. Not from the weight loss -- she loved the weight loss. It was the texture. The dullness. Like someone had turned the saturation down on her face. She booked a dermatologist. The dermatologist put her on retinol, vitamin C serum, and a hydrating mask. Two hundred and forty dollars.

Month seven, the fatigue. She'd been a morning person her entire adult life. Now she was on the couch by two in the afternoon, waking up an hour later not refreshed but heavier. Her doctor ran a thyroid panel. Normal. CBC. Normal. He suggested she might need more sleep.

Month eight, the fog. She was on the phone with her daughter and lost the word refrigerator. She stood in her kitchen repeating "the -- the -- the cold thing where the food goes" and her daughter went quiet on the other end. She forgot her neighbor's name. A woman she'd known for nine years. Her daughter sent her an article about early cognitive decline with a text that said Mom, please get tested.

Margaret made an appointment with a neurologist. She sat in the waiting room filling out a cognitive assessment questionnaire and started to cry. Not because she thought she had dementia. Because she had five things wrong and five different doctors looking at five different pieces and not one of them had stepped back far enough to ask the obvious question.

When did each of these start?

That's what I asked her. Not approximately. Specifically. Margaret is a note-taker. She had dates. Hair, month four. Nails, month five. Skin, month six. Fatigue, month seven. Fog, month eight.

I'd seen that exact cascade before. Same order. Same timing. In patient after patient on these medications.

I told Margaret what nobody else had told her. Those aren't five problems. That's one cascade.

Her medication works by slowing her stomach. That's the mechanism. That's how it controls appetite and blood sugar. Food stays longer. She eats less. She feels full sooner. This is the desired effect. This is what's working.

But when the stomach slows down that significantly, it doesn't just reduce appetite. It reduces nutrient extraction. The stomach muscles that mechanically break food down -- the contractions that pulverize what you eat so your intestines can pull nutrients from it -- those muscles are sluggish. Food passes through partially broken down. Nutrients that need stomach acid and mechanical grinding to become absorbable get shortchanged.

Not all at once. In a specific order. Based on how fast the body burns through its stored reserves.

Iron and ferritin deplete first. The body pulls iron from hair follicles before it pulls from vital organs. Hair is not essential for survival. The body treats it as optional. That's Margaret's month four.

Zinc goes next. Keratin production -- what her nails are made of -- requires zinc. When zinc drops, nails soften, peel, ridge. Month five.

Fat-soluble vitamins A, C, and E follow. Those are what skin uses to maintain collagen, drive cell turnover, and hold hydration. When they drop, skin doesn't just get dry. It loses the ability to rebuild itself. It looks like it aged five years in five months. Month six.

B12 and folate go next. Mitochondria need them to produce cellular energy. When they're low, the body can't generate ATP efficiently. That's not poor sleep. That's energy production failing at the cellular level. Month seven.

And when B12, folate, and iron are all depleted simultaneously, the brain doesn't have what it needs to produce neurotransmitters or maintain the insulation around nerve fibers. That's the fog. That's the lost words. That's the chickpeas. Month eight.

Margaret looked at me and said you're telling me all of this is one thing.

One thing. Nutrient depletion caused by impaired gastric processing. Every specialist she'd seen was looking at their piece. The dermatologist saw skin. The neurologist saw cognition. Nobody asked when each symptom started relative to the others because nobody was looking at the sequence.

Margaret asked me the question every patient asks at this point. So I take a multivitamin?

And that's the part that makes my job difficult. Because the answer is no. A standard multivitamin lands in a stomach that's not moving properly. It sits there. It doesn't break down efficiently. She might absorb twenty percent of what's on the label. Maybe less.

Eating more doesn't work either. Eating more works directly against the appetite suppression that's managing her blood sugar and producing the weight loss she needs. The medication is not the enemy. The medication is doing its job. The problem is what happens upstream -- at the stomach muscle level -- when motility drops and stays dropped for months.

The retinol the dermatologist prescribed treats the skin surface. Margaret's skin isn't failing at the surface. It's failing because the vitamins that build it from inside aren't getting absorbed. The hair vitamins she ordered add nutrients to a system that can't process them. The B12 protocol addresses one depleted vitamin while five others are equally low.

Every solution Margaret tried was aimed at the right symptom and the wrong cause.

What she needed was something that addressed the motility itself. Something that woke up the stomach muscles enough to restore mechanical breakdown so nutrients from food and supplements could actually be extracted and absorbed.

About eight months ago, I came across a formulation through a colleague who works exclusively with GLP-1 patients. She'd been testing it with her own patients and tracking their micronutrient panels before and after. The repletion data was striking enough that I started recommending it myself.

It's a gummy. That matters -- a gummy starts dissolving the moment you chew it. It doesn't need to survive a sluggish stomach intact to deliver its contents. But the formulation is what made me pay attention.

It contains celery juice extract at a concentration that delivers apigenin -- a natural prokinetic compound. Apigenin stimulates smooth muscle contraction in the stomach wall. Not aggressively. Not in a way that overrides the medication or undoes the appetite suppression. Just enough to get the muscles contracting again so food actually gets broken down properly and nutrients can be extracted.

It has chlorophyll, which binds to the sulfur compounds that build up when food sits too long in a slow stomach. That's the rotten egg burps some of my patients have been living with for months and attributing to the medication itself.

A micro-dose of prebiotic fiber. Not the aggressive fiber supplements that wreck a GLP-1 stomach. A small amount that supports gut bacteria without overwhelming a system that's already processing slowly.

And it has vitamins A, C, K, B6, and folate -- the specific nutrients that deplete first in the cascade I described. Not a multivitamin with forty compounds at trace levels. The ones that matter for this specific depletion pattern.

The product is called Motilli. Most of my GLP-1 patients have never heard of it. Most of their doctors haven't either.

I don't promise my patients fast results because nutrient repletion doesn't work fast. Stores that took months to deplete take weeks to rebuild. I told Margaret to expect eight to twelve weeks before she'd see the full cascade reverse.

But the fog cleared first. About two weeks in, Margaret told me she'd talked to her daughter on the phone for forty-five minutes without a single blank. She called me after and I could hear it in her voice -- she'd been so scared she was losing her mind and she wasn't. She was depleted. There is a difference and it matters enormously.

The energy came back next. Week three, she made it past four in the afternoon without the couch. By week five, she was back to walking her dog in the morning.

Her nail tech noticed around week six. Stronger. Less peeling. Growing at a normal rate.

The skin and hair took longest. Three months before Margaret told me her face looked like her face again in photos. Four months before her ponytail felt heavier in her hand.

Margaret's panel at the twelve-week mark showed vitamin A back in range. B12 mid-range and climbing. Folate normalized. Zinc still low but trending upward. The numbers confirmed what she was already feeling.

What Margaret tells me she thinks about most isn't the results. It's the day in my office when someone connected the five things into one thing. Because that was the day she stopped being terrified that her body was disassembling itself and started understanding what was actually happening. She wasn't falling apart. She was depleted. And depletion is fixable.

I'm telling you this because I keep seeing it. Three times a week. Women on medications that are working -- genuinely working, producing real results they need and want -- and simultaneously developing a cascade of symptoms that nobody connects because every specialist only looks at one floor of the building.

If you're on a GLP-1 and you've been quietly keeping a mental checklist -- the hair, the nails, the skin, the energy, the fog -- you are not falling apart. Your medication is doing what it's supposed to do. But your stomach muscles are sluggish, your nutrient extraction has dropped, and your body is rationing what it has in survival order.

You can keep seeing five specialists for five symptoms that will never resolve independently. Or you can address the one upstream cause that's producing all of them.

Motilli has a ninety-day guarantee. If your symptoms don't improve, full refund. No practitioner I know in conventional medicine offers that on anything they prescribe.

Stop chasing five answers for one problem.

Last fall on a Saturday evening she developed a sharp pain in her lower abdomen.

By Sunday morning her bowel had ruptured.

I'm 52.

My wife Joanne is 47.

We have been married twenty-two years. Two adult kids, one in grad school, one teaching at a high school in another state.

Joanne started a GLP-1 medication nine weeks before the night this happened. Her primary care doctor had prescribed it for her A1C, which had crept into pre-diabetic territory at her annual physical.

She had also wanted to lose forty pounds for years. The medication was supposed to do both.

In the first nine weeks she lost fourteen pounds. The food noise in her head — the thing she had been trying to describe to me for two decades and had finally given up explaining — went quiet for the first time in her adult life.

She said it was like someone had turned down a radio that had been on in her head since college.

I want all of that on the record before I tell you the rest, because the medication did do what it was supposed to do.

It is what it did to her gut, silently, that I am writing about.

—

For nine weeks, she had been mildly constipated.

Going every four or five days instead of every day.

She had taken Miralax twice that week. She had mentioned to me at breakfast that her stomach felt "heavier than usual" but she had not used the word pain.

I want to say that again.

For nine weeks, no real warning. No emergency, no impaction, no ER trip, no bathroom-floor moment.

Just a slow, quiet slowdown that we both wrote off as a side effect she would adjust to.

—

The Saturday evening this happened was the Saturday before our daughter's birthday.

We were at the kitchen table eating roast chicken and roasted potatoes that I had made.

Joanne put her fork down halfway through her plate and put one hand on her lower right side.

She said: "Something is wrong."

She did not look scared yet. Just confused.

She said the pain was in her lower right abdomen, sharp, "like someone was twisting a knife slowly."

We thought appendicitis.

I drove her to the closest hospital, fifteen minutes away.

—

The first ER did a quick exam.

The doctor on shift was a man maybe ten years younger than us. He pressed on her stomach. He asked when she had last eaten. He ran basic bloodwork.

He came back two hours later and said the bloodwork was fine, the rebound test was inconclusive, it was probably "muscular or possibly an early UTI."

He prescribed her a painkiller and an antibiotic for the possible UTI.

He sent us home.

—

I do not blame him.

I have thought about him a hundred times since and I have decided that he was doing the best he could with what he was trained to look for.

What he was not trained to look for was a slowly perforating colon in a woman nine weeks into a GLP-1 medication.

Almost nobody is, yet.

—

We got home around 1 AM.

I helped Joanne into bed.

I gave her the painkiller.

She fell asleep around 2.

At 3:14 AM she woke me up by getting out of bed and trying to walk to the bathroom.

She did not make it to the door.

She vomited on the carpet.

She tried to stand back up and her knees gave out.

She was white. Whiter than I have ever seen her. Whiter than the morning we lost her father.

I called 911.

—

I want to be honest about something.

The dispatcher told me to drive her instead of waiting for the ambulance because we lived in a part of town where the response time would have been longer than the drive.

I drove her to a different hospital — a bigger one, thirty-five minutes from us, on the other side of town.

I do not remember the drive.

I remember her in the passenger seat with her head against the window. I remember her saying, twice, "I am so sorry, James."

I do not know what she was sorry for.

I have asked her since. She does not remember saying it.

—

We got to the second hospital at 4:02 AM.

A nurse named Dolores took one look at her, put her in a wheelchair, and ran with her to triage. I have never seen anyone run in a hospital before that morning.

The CT scan happened within the first hour.

The surgeon was on the floor within ninety minutes.

She was in surgery by 8 AM.

—

I sat in the waiting room from 8 AM until 12:47 PM, when a man named Dr. Henry Mwamba walked through the swinging doors and said my name out loud.

He was tall, sixty, gray hair cropped close, scrubs. The kind of presence you instinctively trust.

He sat down on the chair next to me.

He said: "Your wife is stable. The surgery went as well as it could go. We removed about ten inches of her colon. We have given her a temporary stoma, which means there is a bag attached to her abdomen that will collect her stool while the part of her colon we left behind heals. In about three months we will do a second surgery to reconnect everything. She is going to be in the hospital for at least two weeks."

I did not cry in front of him.

I waited until I got back to the parking garage.

She was in the hospital for eighteen days.

My Colon Was Dry
Your colon is not supposed to be a dry tube.
Its wall is covered by a thin layer of mucus produced by tiny goblet cells.
That mucus keeps the colon slippery. It coats the stool, helps it hold moisture, and allows it to glide forward without sticking to the wall.
At the same time, your colon is constantly pulling water out of waste.
When the mucus lining is healthy, there is a balance. The colon removes enough water to form the stool while the mucus helps keep it soft, coated, and moving.
But menopause can disturb that balance.
Estrogen helps regulate mucus-producing tissue throughout a womanâs body.
When estrogen falls, women often notice dryness in their skin, eyes, and mouth.
But dryness can also affect tissues they cannot see.
As the mucus lining inside the colon becomes thinner, the stool loses part of the slippery, moisture-holding barrier that once protected it.
The colon keeps pulling out water.
The stool becomes dry.
Dry stool moves more slowly.
And because it moves more slowly, the colon has even more time to pull out more water.
That creates a vicious cycle:
Less mucus. Drier stool. Slower movement. Even more water removed.
The longer the stool sits, the harder it becomes.
That is how a normal bowel movement turns into pebbles, then days of pressure, and eventually a mass that feels cemented inside you.

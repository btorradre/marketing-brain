# BrandProject Intro Call — ICP Q&A Prep
*Prepared 2026-08-06. Every claim below is sourced from our own research: a 4,095-comment voice-of-customer database (YouTube, TikTok, Reddit, Instagram), a live willingness-to-pay survey (498 sessions, 342 priced-intent answers at a stated $29.99), a 1,271-comment brand-vs-generic analysis, and a comment-section mine of Bloom Nutrition, whose ICP mirrors ours.*

---

## 1. What did you initially misunderstand about the customer?

Three big corrections, all forced by the data:

- **We thought the problem was taste.** It isn't the driver. The dominant language in 4,095 comments is physical fear: choking, gagging, the pill stuck in the throat, heart racing, "I end up only swallowing the water." Taste matters as proof, but fear is the engine.
- **We thought "can't swallow pills" was a binary condition.** Our survey says 61% struggle conditionally (certain pills or big pills only) and 39% struggle every single time. It's a spectrum, and severity turns out to be the single best predictor of purchase intent.
- **We thought this was a subscription product.** Pain relief is taken reactively, so there is no honest way to time a rebill. We killed the subscription and rebuilt the model around one-time purchase plus a multi-bottle stock-up offer (the Excedrin emergency-bottle behavior).

A fourth, smaller one: we assumed we'd have to defend efficacy. In every comment mine, nobody ever asks whether pain relief works. She's judging the format and the object, and the molecule rides for free.

## 2. What have customers told you that genuinely surprised you?

- **The shame has a childhood origin story.** Over and over: parents yelling at them to swallow the pill, and the belief they'd grow out of it. "I'm a 29 year old and still struggling." "I thought I'd grow out of it. I didn't."
- **"I thought I was the only one" is the dominant emotional theme.** People report genuine relief just from reading the comment section. Recognition, on its own, converts.
- **The workaround economy.** Adults with entire systems: crushing pills into applesauce, a specific soda to force it down, cutting pills in half, buying children's liquid Tylenol at 30, or simply skipping the dose and suffering through the headache.
- **The intensity of it.** "I would rather get punched in the face than take my medicine" got 100 likes.
- **Our live survey traffic skews older than our locked avatar.** We built for women 25 to 38. The strongest measured intent came from women 35 to 54, and the single biggest respondent block was 55+. More on this in Q3.

## 3. Which customer segment showed the strongest intent?

Two clean answers from the survey (price stated as fact at $29.99, likelihood measured):

- **By severity: the every-single-time struggler.** 40% positive intent ("definitely 2 to 3 bottles" or "very likely") versus 26% for certain-pills-only and 13% for big-pills-only. Severity beats demographics as a predictor, roughly 3 to 1 from top to bottom.
- **By demographics: women 45 to 54** (52% positive, n=25), then women 35 to 44 (42%). The 55+ women were our largest volume but also the most price-resistant (35% said not at that price).

Honest caveat we'll say out loud: the sample is a few hundred people from Meta-optimized traffic, which skews older. But the severity gradient is the finding we'd bet on, and it holds across ages.

## 4. Who showed interest but probably would not actually purchase?

- **The deal-waiters.** In an earlier survey version, 71% picked "wait for a discount." And when we made the mistake of offering price ranges as options, 86% picked the cheapest. That taught us our pricing-research law: never ask people to pick a price, state it and measure likelihood.
- **The casual strugglers.** Big-pills-only respondents: 13% positive, 29% flatly no at the price. They relate to the content but the pain isn't sharp enough to pay a premium.
- **The "reviews first" crowd.** 48% of the current cohort. Real interest, but they're launch-plus-90-days buyers, not preorder buyers. We treat them as the retargeting file, not the forecast.
- **International commenters.** Comment sections fill with "bring this to Brazil/Europe/Dubai." We're US-only. We leave those comments up as social proof and sell them nothing.

## 5. What language do customers use to describe the problem?

Physical and fear-based, almost never clinical:

- **Sensation verbs:** choke, gag, stuck, throat closes up, panic. "Once the pill is in my mouth my heart starts beating like crazy." "I end up only swallowing the water."
- **The worst moment they describe:** the pill that won't go down and starts dissolving, "and you taste all that nastiness."
- **Workaround vocabulary:** crush, applesauce, yogurt, cut in half, kids' liquid, "I have a whole system."
- **The confession register:** age plus "still." "48 here, i crush, chew or liquid."

In our classification of all 4,095 comments, fear language outweighs shame language about 2.6 to 1 by volume. That ratio sets our creative weighting.

## 6. Do they identify as unable to swallow pills, or describe it differently?

They describe episodes, not an identity. Almost nobody says "I have dysphagia" or "I'm unable to swallow pills." They say a specific pill got stuck, they gag on capsules, big ones scare them. The survey confirms the spectrum: only 39% say it happens every single time.

They frame it as an embarrassing personal quirk they should have outgrown, not a condition. That framing is exactly why the market stayed invisible: people don't seek treatment for a quirk, and they don't talk about it until someone else confesses first.

There is one exception: a small, highly motivated Reddit segment with diagnosed conditions (dysphagia, eosinophilic esophagitis) who do self-identify medically. They buy out of necessity and they're a strong secondary market, but we don't market in their language.

## 7. How do you market the product without embarrassing the customer?

The whole system is built around one rule: someone else confesses first, so she never has to.

- **Creator-led confession.** The creator says "wait, you can't swallow pills either?" and the viewer feels seen rather than called out. Our emotional spine is Recognition, Relief, Revelation, Liberation.
- **Sell the upgrade, never the deficiency.** The proposition is "It's just Tylenol. In a gummy." Buying it requires no admission. The convenience angle (no water, works anywhere) gives her a dignity-preserving public reason to own it.
- **Make it a beautiful object.** Our Bloom research shows this buyer shops the whole frame: the packaging, the color, the flavor name. The product gets styled into her life like an accessory, so owning it reads aspirational, not remedial.
- **The brand carries the vulnerability.** The founder story runs through both founders' sisters, who genuinely struggle with pills. Orelli was built for them, and the customer gets to borrow that story instead of exposing her own.
- **Adult-strength framing.** Full 500mg dose, real medicine. That kills the children's-aisle shame that defines every current workaround.

## 8. Is the purchaser always the end user?

At launch, mostly yes: this is a self-purchase driven by creator content and personal recognition. But it's a medicine-cabinet product, and cabinets are shared. Our second survey question asks about keeping it in the cabinet, and the stock-up offer is designed for household coverage, not solo consumption. We expect meaningful household spread (spouse, teens, visiting family) and we'll instrument for it post-launch. We don't yet have hard buyer-versus-user data, and we'll say so plainly.

## 9. Are parents purchasing for adult children?

No direct evidence yet, but two signals make it a live hypothesis: the VoC includes parents describing their kids' and teens' struggles, and our intent peak sitting in 45 to 54 (with heavy 55+ volume) is consistent with household buyers shopping for more than themselves. The reverse direction is just as plausible: adult children buying for aging parents, since swallowing difficulty rises with age. We're treating both as post-launch tests, not launch claims.

## 10. Are caregivers an important audience?

Important, and deliberately sequenced second. The launch wedge is the self-purchasing woman. Phase 2 opens the medically captive segments where a caregiver or constrained user buys out of necessity: pregnant and postpartum women (acetaminophen is the only OTC pain reliever considered safe in pregnancy, and 62% of pregnant women use it), older adults with age-related swallowing difficulty, and the diagnosed-dysphagia segment. The age skew in our survey suggests the older end may pull itself forward faster than we planned, which we'd count as a good problem.

## 11. Is the problem chronic, situational or both?

Both, and the data splits it cleanly: 39% chronic (every single pill, every time) and 61% situational (certain pills or big pills). The structural insight underneath: **the swallowing problem is chronic, but usage is episodic**, because pain arrives on its own schedule. That mismatch is why we rejected subscriptions and built the model around stock-up purchasing and reorder timing instead.

## 12. What causes someone to seek an alternative today rather than six months from now?

Awareness is the trigger. She isn't shopping for a solution because the category has taught her there are exactly two options: pills, or the children's aisle. She has never been told an adult-strength chewable exists. The moment a creator she trusts says "this exists," six months collapses to today. That's why the entire funnel is one step.

The accelerants stack on top: a fresh bad episode (a stuck pill, a gagging scare), a dose skipped while actually in pain, or a new medication she has no choice about. Purchases cluster right after an episode, so creative that recreates the episode is our timing mechanism.

## 13. Which existing behavior is hardest to displace?

The workaround system, because it's free and it more or less works. The applesauce ritual, the specific soda, the pill cutter: she's run that system for 15 years. Right behind it sits the cheapest behavior of all, skipping the dose and suffering through, which costs nothing and requires no decision.

Habit is also the top stated driver in our brand-loyalty research (18% of brand-name buyers: "it's what I grew up with"). And there's a structural piece: pain relief is usually bought reactively at retail, in the moment. We're DTC, so we can't win the pain moment; we have to already be in the cabinet when it arrives. The stock-up model exists precisely to displace the checkout-aisle grab.

## 14. What is the strongest objection?

**Taste.** It's the loudest product fear in the VoC (the dissolving-pill bitterness is the single most visceral memory people describe), and our survey quantifies the cost: 28% of respondents made their cabinet intent conditional on taste, and that group converts at 11% versus 36% for the unconditional group. It's also our biggest execution risk, since masking 500mg of bitter acetaminophen is genuinely hard, which is why taste has a formal gate in the plan and the first-bite reaction on camera is the launch hero asset.

Second place: "is this real medicine, and is it safe?" This buyer carries a quiet ingredient-anxiety layer and asks the comment section, not the brand. We pre-seed those answers (real dose, what else is in it, safety) in pinned comments and FAQ, backed by the doctor layer.

## 15. What is the strongest reason not to purchase?

**Price against the generic.** The same molecule sits at retail for a few dollars, and ours is $29.99. In the survey, 22% said no at that price outright, the earlier cohort was dominated by discount-waiting, and any time we offered price options people collapsed to the cheapest. At preorder this stacks with no reviews and unproven taste, which is exactly the "reviews first" 48%.

Our answer to it: never argue chemistry, because on chemistry we lose to a $4 bottle. We sell the format, the dignity, and the safety story (single-dose clarity versus accidental double-dosing), where the generic can't follow.

## Numbers to have on hand

- Up to 40% of US adults report difficulty swallowing pills (Harris); 32% even among adults with no diagnosed disorder.
- Funnel: 260M US adults → 104M pill-averse → 48M beachhead (weekly OTC users). Sub-doors: choking fear ~20M, adult shame ~17M, untreated pain ~10M. All four angles target the 48M only.
- 52M Americans use acetaminophen-containing medicine every week (CHPA).
- US acetaminophen market ~$4.1B (GM Insights 2024). Use this figure, never the unverified $5.2B.
- VoC database: 4,095 comments, fear-to-shame ratio 2.6 to 1.
- Survey topline (stated $29.99): ~30% positive intent, 48% reviews-first, 22% no at price. Severity gradient 40% / 26% / 13%.
- Unit economics: $29.99 locked, COGS $9.50 all-in, ~$15.18 contribution per order after commission.

## Watch-outs for the call

- Never claim superiority over Tylenol (OTC drug rules: claims stop at relieves pain, reduces fever).
- Don't lead with preorder revenue projections; lead with measured CAC, the creator roster, and the VoC depth.
- Pregnancy is a Phase-2 sub-avatar, never the lead.
- The founders' own story stays truthful: the sisters struggle with pills, the founders don't claim to.
- Financial docs from May 2026 are stale; the cousin owns all current financials.

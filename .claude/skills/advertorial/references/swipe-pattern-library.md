# Swipe Pattern Library

Read this reference before writing any advertorial. It contains headline formulas, social proof architecture, pricing psychology, urgency patterns, CTA strategy, and close components extracted from 66 analyzed real-world advertorials across health, beauty, devices, supplements, and consumer product categories.

## 1. Headline Formulas

Product name NEVER appears in the headline. Each formula creates a different emotional trigger. Choose based on the framework and the audience's primary emotional state.

### Formula A: Age + Dramatic Result + Curiosity Gap
"Phoenix Man, 67, Cancels $65,000 Knee Replacement After Physical Therapist Leaks This 'At-Home' Treatment"

**Why it works:** Age creates identification. Dollar amount creates cost contrast. "Leaks" implies insider knowledge. The reader thinks: "If he's 67 and avoided surgery, maybe I can too."

### Formula B: Expert Authority + Surprising Claim
"Top Orthopedic Surgeons: 'This Is the Fastest Way to Relieve Shoulder Pain for Good'"

**Why it works:** "Top surgeons" transfers institutional credibility. "Fastest way" promises efficiency. "For good" promises permanence. The colon-and-quote format feels like journalism.

### Formula C: Cost Contrast Anchor
"A $39 Drugstore Serum Helped a 52-Year-Old Woman Avoid a $21,500 Facelift"

**Why it works:** The price gap ($39 vs. $21,500) does the selling before the reader opens the page. The reader clicks to find out WHICH $39 serum.

### Formula D: Conspiracy / Suppression Frame
"82-Year-Old Physiotherapist Exposed the Remedy the Medical Industry Buried for Decades"

**Why it works:** "Exposed" and "buried" activate distrust of the system. The reader clicks to see what was hidden. Age adds gravitas — 82 years old means a lifetime of knowledge.

### Formula E: Statistic-Led Fear Hook
"Hidden Invaders Are Living in 85% of Americans Right Now — And the Natural Solutions That Thousands Use to Destroy Them"

**Why it works:** A specific percentage makes the threat concrete. "Right now" creates immediacy. The second clause pivots from fear to solution.

### Formula F: Action + Timeframe
"If Your Hands Go Numb and Burn, Do This for 15 Minutes"

**Why it works:** Symptom-specific opening triggers self-identification. "Do this" implies a simple action. "15 minutes" makes it feel achievable. The reader needs to click to find out WHAT to do.

### Formula G: Question + Authority Answer
"Can You Avoid That Knee Surgery You've Been Dreading? A Prominent Doctor of Physical Therapy Thinks So"

**Why it works:** The question matches what the reader is already asking themselves. The authority answer provides hope. "Thinks so" is softer and more credible than "says yes."

### Formula H: Discovery / Revelation
"What I Discovered About Parasites and Cancer Changed How I Treat Patients Forever"

**Why it works:** First-person discovery creates intimacy. The connection between two concepts (parasites and cancer) creates curiosity. "Changed how I treat patients forever" implies high stakes.

### Subheadline Rules

1-2 sentences expanding the headline. More specific. Include 2-3 symptom or situation callouts that trigger self-identification. The subheadline closes the curiosity gap enough to commit the reader to scrolling, but not enough to satisfy the curiosity entirely.

## 2. Social Proof Architecture

Layer multiple proof categories. No single type is sufficient. The combination creates triangulated belief.

### Individual Transformation Stories

Named people with specific details: age, location, timeline, measurable results.

**Rules:**
- Vary demographics to cover the audience range
- Include at least one deeply skeptical person who was converted
- Specific numbers always (lab results, pain scale scores, measurements, timelines)
- Stories talk about EXPERIENCE, never about "the formula" or "the ingredients"
- Include a restored life moment — not just symptom relief, but something the reader WANTS (played with grandchildren, ate birthday cake without guilt, climbed the stairs, went back to the gym)

**Example:** "Jennifer Hayes, 51, Chicago — 'Seven weeks in. LDL went from 258 to 184. I actually called the lab to double-check. My doctor asked me what I changed and I almost didn't tell him because I knew he'd roll his eyes.'"

### Professional Observation

Proof delivered through an authority's lens. Aggregated patterns, not individual anecdotes.

"I've guided over sixty patients toward this approach in the past eighteen months. The pattern is remarkably consistent: measurable reduction within six to eight weeks, with complete absence of the side effects I spent two decades managing."

**Rules:** Aggregated, not individual. Professional language. Specific enough to feel real. Can include 1-2 brief patient vignettes from the clinician's perspective. Use numbers that feel professional, not promotional (don't say "100% of patients" — say "the pattern is remarkably consistent").

### Community Aggregation

Proof from a community, support group, or user base.

"In our group of 340 members, sixty-two have tried this specific formulation over the past year. Fifty-four reported measurable improvement. The average was 64 points in the first eight weeks."

**Rules:** Numbers feel community-reported, not clinically precise. Include the most common surprise or unexpected benefit ("The first thing people report isn't the cholesterol — it's the energy"). Weave in 2-3 direct quotes from community members.

### Star Rating and Review Displays

Visual credibility signals mimicking review platforms.

**Elements to include:**
- Overall star rating (4.7-4.9 — perfect 5.0 looks fake)
- Total review count (hundreds to thousands: "4,537 reviews," "36,000+ verified reviews")
- Rating distribution breakdown: "5-star: 90%, 4-star: 7%, 3-star: 2%, 2-star: 0.5%, 1-star: 0.5%"
- "Verified Buyer" badges on individual reviews
- Mix of review lengths (one-liners and paragraphs)

### Symptom Recognition Checklists

"Do any of these sound familiar?" followed by 6-10 relatable symptoms. Formatted as a visual checklist. The reader self-diagnoses by recognizing their own experience.

**Rules:** Include both obvious symptoms AND surprising ones. The obvious ones create identification ("Yes, that's me"). The surprising ones create revelation ("I didn't realize THAT was connected"). Place the checklist early in the piece — it's a commitment device. Once the reader checks 4-5 boxes mentally, they're invested.

## 3. Comment Section Architecture

Formatted as social media comments below the article. A conversion tool disguised as community engagement. 8-12 comments serving specific psychological functions.

### Comment Types (Use All)

**1. The Skeptic-Turned-Believer (1-2 comments)**
"I almost scrolled right past this. So glad I didn't. Three weeks in and I can actually make a fist again in the morning."

**2. The Detailed Result (2-3 comments)**
"Seven weeks in. LDL went from 258 to 184. I actually called the lab to double-check. No side effects. None. After four years on atorvastatin that made me feel like I was 90."

**3. The Emotional Breakthrough (1-2 comments)**
"I ate birthday cake for the first time in six years and didn't feel guilty. That probably sounds stupid but if you know, you know."

**4. The Comparison to Conventional Treatment (1 comment)**
"I was on prescription meds for four years. This dropped my numbers more in two months than the meds did, and my legs don't hurt anymore."

**5. The Logistics Question (1 comment)**
"How long does shipping take?" or "Does this come with instructions?" — normalizes the purchase decision by showing other people asking practical questions.

**6. The Newcomer (1-2 comments)**
"Just ordered mine. Nervous but hopeful. Will report back." Creates a sense of ongoing community activity.

**7. The Replier (1-2 reply threads)**
Existing users answering newcomers' questions. "Took about 5 days for me. Worth the wait." Creates social proof through conversation.

### Comment Formatting Rules

- **Name:** First and last, or first name and last initial. Mix both styles.
- **Timestamp:** Relative ("39 min," "2 h," "5 h," "1 d"). Spread across hours — not all posted at the same time.
- **Like counts:** Modest (5-43). Not viral numbers — believable engagement.
- **Reply counts:** 1-3 replies on the most engagement-worthy comments.
- **Authenticity markers:** At least one comment with a typo or grammatical imperfection. Vary length dramatically — some comments are one sentence, others are a paragraph. Nobody uses perfect punctuation in comment sections.
- **Voice:** Comments talk about experience and results, NEVER about "the formula," "the ingredients," or "the proprietary blend." Real people talk about how they FEEL, not how the product WORKS.

## 4. Conversion Close — The Six Components

Not every advertorial uses all six. Select based on framework, product price, and audience skepticism level.

### Component 1: Value Summary (1-3 sentences)

Brief, clean restatement of what the product does — in mechanism terms, not marketing terms. Exists for the reader who scrolled to the bottom.

**Authority voice:** "This formulation addresses all three pathways at clinically validated doses, without the side effects that make the conventional approach intolerable."

**Crisis voice:** "After everything I tried and everything I learned, this is the only thing that matched what the research actually says needs to happen."

**Comparison voice:** "Of the twelve products we evaluated, this is the only one that met all five criteria."

### Component 2: Pricing Psychology

See Section 5 below for all six pricing patterns.

### Component 3: Guarantee / Risk Reversal

Every advertorial needs risk reversal. 60-180 day money-back guarantee is the standard range. Frame through narrator voice:

**The Professional Promise (Authority):** "If this doesn't produce measurable results for you, I don't want your money. Full refund within 90 days."

**The Risk-Reversal Reframe (Crisis/Whistleblower):** "After spending $8,000 on supplements that didn't work, the 90-day guarantee was the only reason I tried again. I figured: if it doesn't work, I get my money back. If it does, I get my life back."

**The Confidence Signal (Comparison):** "This is the only product in our review that offers a full money-back guarantee — which tells you something about their confidence in the formulation."

**The Trial Period:** "You have 90 full days to see what happens to your numbers." Frames the purchase as an experiment with measurable endpoints, not a commitment.

**The Keep-It Guarantee:** "If it doesn't work, keep everything." Use sparingly — it's strong but can feel too promotional.

## 5. Pricing Psychology (6 Patterns)

Never present price without context. Naked price creates sticker shock. Framed price creates perceived value.

### Pattern A: Procedure / Treatment Cost Anchor

Compare product price against the expensive alternative. This is the most powerful pricing frame in the swipe analysis.

"A knee replacement costs $65,000. Recovery takes 6-12 months. And 33% of patients report the surgery didn't fully resolve their pain. This device costs $49.95 and arrives in five days."

"The average facelift runs $21,500 plus two weeks of recovery. This serum is $39."

**When to use:** Product is an alternative to an expensive procedure, treatment, or professional service. The cost gap does the selling.

### Pattern B: Cost-Per-Use Calculation

Break the price down to daily or per-use cost. Makes any price feel negligible.

"At $49.95, that's $0.15 per session if you use it every day for a year. Less than a cup of gas station coffee."

**When to use:** Product is used repeatedly (supplements, devices, creams). The per-use cost is impressively low.

### Pattern C: Failed-Spending Comparison

Total up what the reader has wasted on ineffective solutions.

"The average person with this condition spends $1,800 a year on supplements that don't address the root cause. Add the co-pays, the specialist visits, the OTC remedies that sit in the medicine cabinet gathering dust."

**When to use:** The audience has a history of trying things that didn't work. The product is positioned as the end of wasted spending.

### Pattern D: Competitor Price Comparison

Show the product costs less than inferior alternatives. Table format works well.

| Product | Addresses Root Cause? | Price | Guarantee? |
|---------|----------------------|-------|-----------|
| Competitor A | Partial | $79/month | No |
| Competitor B | No | $59/month | 30 days |
| This product | Yes — all three pathways | $49.95/month | 90 days |

**When to use:** Expert Comparison framework. The comparison makes the product look like better value AND more effective.

### Pattern E: Bundle Pricing

Multiple quantity options with increasing discount. Most buyers choose the middle option.

"1 bottle: $59. 3 bottles: $49 each (save $30). 6 bottles: $39 each (save $120)."

**When to use:** Consumable products (supplements, creams, gummies). Bundle pricing implies long-term use, which implies the product works. Also increases average order value.

### Pattern F: Discount With Story-Driven Reason

The discount exists because of a narrative conflict, not a marketing decision.

"Readers coming through this article currently have access to the research pricing — the same rate the clinical trial participants received. That pricing was extended because the company wants real-world data from informed users, not mass-market customers."

"The company agreed to hold the original launch pricing through the end of this month. After that, it goes to full retail."

**When to use:** Authority or Suppression frameworks. The story-driven reason makes the discount feel earned rather than promotional.

## 6. Urgency Patterns (5 Types)

Urgency must feel story-driven, not promotional. Countdown timers and "ACT NOW" language destroy the editorial frame.

### Type A: Manufacturing / Quality Scarcity
"Every batch requires individual citrinin-free certification. That limits production to what the lab can verify — roughly 12,000 units per month."

"The red yeast rice in this formulation is sourced from a specific fermentation process. There are maybe five suppliers worldwide who can do it reliably."

### Type B: Demand Exceeding Supply
"Since this community started sharing results, the company has been backordered twice this quarter."

Stock percentage indicators: "67% of this month's allocation has shipped." "Currently in stock — estimated to sell out within 5-7 days based on current order volume."

### Type C: Suppression Risk
"We've received two cease-and-desist letters from [industry group]. Our legal team tells us we can keep this page live, but we can't guarantee for how long."

"Surgeons in three states have filed injunctions to block direct-to-consumer sales of this device. Those injunctions are currently stayed pending review — but the page may need to come down."

### Type D: Daily Allocation
"Due to demand, orders are limited to [X] per household per day." Creates artificial but plausible constraint.

### Type E: Discount Expiration
"Readers through this page currently have access to a reduced price. That link may not stay active indefinitely."

"This pricing is only available through editorial partner links. The company's direct site sells at full retail."

## 7. CTA Strategy and Placement

### CTA Language

Must match editorial tone. Never scream "BUY NOW."

- "Check Availability" — implies scarcity. Soft. Both frameworks.
- "See If It's In Stock" — even softer. Community-driven.
- "See Current Pricing" — research-mode friendly.
- "Apply Discount & Check Availability" — combines discount and scarcity.
- "Learn More About This Formulation" — softest. Links to PDP.
- "Claim Your Discount" — action-oriented. Use after urgency section.

### CTA Placement Rules

1. **NEVER before mechanism education is complete.** The reader isn't ready.
2. **Mid-article CTA (optional):** After the escalation/mechanism section, for readers ready early. Soft language: "If you're ready to check it out, here's the link."
3. **Primary CTA:** After the full close (pricing + urgency + guarantee). This is the main conversion point.
4. **Post-CTA CTA:** After the comment section or P.S. Final capture for scrollers.
5. **Sidebar/Sticky CTA (optional):** Always visible, passive. "Check Availability" style.
6. **Post-CTA reinforcement line:** "Click the link above to see if current pricing is still available and to review the third-party testing certificates."

**Rule:** Never more than 4 CTA placements total. Each uses slightly different language. Repetition builds familiarity, not annoyance — but only if the language varies.

## 8. Post-CTA Reinforcement

Content below the primary CTA catches readers who scrolled past without clicking.

### Comment Section
See Section 3 above. The most powerful post-CTA element. Readers who weren't quite ready to click see other people's positive experiences and reconsider.

### P.S. Section
Brief personal note from the narrator with one additional proof point.
"P.S. — Since sharing my results in our group, forty-seven members have ordered and started. The latest update from Barbara: 'My six-month labs were the first normal results since 2014.' That's why I keep sharing this."

### Trust Badges
Visual row: money-back guarantee icon, secure checkout icon, third-party tested icon, "Made in USA" badge. Simple visual trust signals.

### FAQ Section
2-5 questions addressing logistics and final-mile objections:
- "How long does shipping take?"
- "What if it doesn't work for me?" (restates guarantee)
- "How many should I order?" (suggests bundle)
- "Is it safe to use with my current medication?" (addresses safety concern)
- "Will it fit my [size/body type]?" (addresses fit/sizing for devices)

## 9. Editorial Frame Elements

### Publication Masthead
A plausible niche publication name. NOT a real publication. Should feel like something the reader could find on Google but never bothers to verify.

**Examples:** "Health Insider," "Wellness Research Quarterly," "The Joint Health Report," "Cardiology Today," "Natural Health Science"

### Breadcrumbs
Category navigation that mimics a real editorial site.

"Home > Heart Health > Cholesterol Management"
"Home > Joint Pain > Non-Surgical Options"
"Home > Women's Health > Hair Loss"

### Byline
Name, credential/title, date. Must match the narrator.

"By Dr. Sarah Chen, MD | Board-Certified Preventive Cardiologist | March 12, 2026"
"By Michael Brennan, PT, DPT | 55 Years Clinical Practice | Updated February 2026"

### "As Seen On" Logo Bar
Row of recognizable media logos suggesting the publication or research has been featured. Creates peripheral credibility without making specific claims about the product.

## 10. Visual and Formatting Patterns

### Comparison Tables
Side-by-side grids. Works for product comparisons, treatment comparisons, or before/after comparisons. Keep to 3-5 rows and 3-4 columns. Highlight the winning column.

### Feature-Benefit Grids
Product features in one column, what that feature MEANS for the reader in the second column. Translates specifications into outcomes.

### Progress Timelines
"Week 1-2: Initial relief and energy improvement"
"Week 3-4: Measurable changes begin"
"Month 2-3: Significant, noticeable transformation"
"Month 3+: Full results, sustained improvement"

Sets realistic expectations while building hope. Found in 40%+ of swipes.

### Pull Quotes
Large-format quotes from authorities or testimonials. Break up text walls and create visual interest. Use for the single most compelling statement from the narrator or a user.

### Product Specification Blocks
Formatted boxes showing key product details: active ingredients, dosage, certifications, country of origin, third-party testing status. Feels editorial and informational rather than promotional.

### Image Placement Strategy
- Hero image near the top (authority portrait, product in context, or dramatic scene)
- Mechanism diagram or anatomical illustration in the education section
- Before/after or product image near the product introduction
- Trust badge row near the close

# Customer discovery: structure carried by speech

Use for a first-person problem-aware VSL, especially when the user requests the conversational Motilli/Nuora approach. This is a specific archetype, not a claim that every VSL has the same order. The September 9, 2026 supplied Motilli script is the user's preferred voice/structure benchmark; the Nuora PDF is a rhetorical reference. Neither is automatic substantiation for the health claims or testimonial details it contains.

## The default story spine

1. **Hook:** recognize the situation and open an explanation or transformation loop. A visual before/after can provide immediate payoff when appropriate and genuine. Tease the mechanism without giving the full lesson yet.
2. **Agitation:** one recognizable moment where the problem interferes with life.
3. **Twist:** explain the personal contradiction or private consequence. This adds meaning, not merely another symptom.
4. **Renewed curiosity:** after the stakes, promise the specific missing explanation. A second loop is earned here; do not mechanically delete it as a repeated invitation. A time-bound promise must be fulfilled on time.
5. **Discovery:** introduce how the narrator encountered the explanation. Keep the source's role and the narrator's own experience distinct.
6. **UMP:** explain the relevant cause in ordinary language, then let the narrator recognize why it matters to her.
7. **Prior solutions:** reinterpret the particular gap in what she tried; explain rather than ridicule. Some references mention attempts earlier as part of agitation; this is the later explanatory payoff.
8. **Solution question and criteria:** “what would help with that?” naturally opens the UMS. A follow-up question, message or search can carry it when it belongs to the real account.
9. **UMS:** give each necessary component a job: `problem/gap → action → why that action matters`. Name/meaning/action must be understandable without specialist knowledge. A list of ingredients or generic “support” does not complete this beat.
10. **Selection distinction, if needed:** answer why the relevant format, formulation or combination matters. Handle a real DIY objection through the narrator's decision. Do not create “only company,” synergy or special-ratio claims merely to fill this slot.
11. **Product:** the named product answers the requirements already taught. An unnamed product route and spoken brand can be separate moments. Keep these visible in the map.
12. **Use and experience:** make the routine easy to picture. Report actual expectation/experience, including uncertainty if present; do not prescribe a fabricated first-week failure or recovery calendar.
13. **Payoff:** return to the opening activity with its changed meaning. Ordinary relief can carry more emotional weight than a broad transformation slogan.
14. **Offer/action:** actual terms, optional genuine availability information, guarantee and a clear invitation. Scarcity is not mandatory.

Keep this dependency order for briefs choosing this archetype, unless the user approves another order. Individual steps may share a paragraph; they need not become fourteen isolated blocks or fourteen scenes. Product-aware reviews, founder stories and expert interviews have other valid structures.

## Why the narration sounds spontaneous

The useful unit is a thought followed by a response:

`what happened → what I thought it meant → what I learned → how that changed my next question or action`

The listener hears a person working through a problem, although the writer has carefully planned the sequence. At every beat boundary, finish: **“Because she just learned/felt X, she now asks/does Y.”** If that sentence requires an invented premise, repair the transition.

The supplied Motilli example moves from a group comment to understanding, then “And I thought okay,” then a follow-up question, then criteria, then another woman's explanation. The specific Facebook/doctor journey is not a reusable requirement. Its function is a chain of motivated questions. At shorter runtimes, collapse repeated message logistics while preserving that function.

## Voice decisions to reproduce

| Craft choice | What it does | How to apply it |
|---|---|---|
| Plain verbs and concrete nouns | Keeps the story understandable on first hearing | Prefer “I went back,” “I asked,” “my jeans,” “dinner” to abstraction and strategic jargon. |
| Connected clauses | Makes each thought grow out of the preceding one | Use “because,” “but,” “so,” “and” and “then” for real causal, contrasting or sequential relationships. No frequency quota. |
| Brief narrator reaction | Returns ownership of the explanation to the woman | After a lesson, show what she realized or asked. Do not let her become a clinician delivering a lecture. |
| Uneven sentence lengths | Gives explanation room and lets short reactions land | Keep a natural longer thought followed by a brief response where appropriate; do not chop everything into slogans. |
| Understatement | Lets the viewer recognize the stakes herself | The supplied line “That sounds like nothing but I hadn't had that in months” gives an ordinary dinner moment emotional weight. Use the function, not the same sentence in every ad. |
| Specific callbacks | Turns the ending into resolution | If the opening concerns choosing clothes around discomfort, the ending returns to getting dressed or eating comfortably when supported. |
| Imperfect confidence | Reflects a person's actual knowledge | Keep honest uncertainty and supplied skepticism. Do not turn uncertainty into fabricated hesitation or use it to hide an unsupported claim. |
| Native social delivery | Fits how the intended person would speak to a camera | A calm, candid account, contractions and recognizable details; no assumed slang, dialect or artificial verbal stumbles based on age. |

“Camouflaged” should mean natural in-feed language and pacing. Keep the speaker's real relationship to the product and commercial context accurate; a fabricated neutral customer is not a voice technique.

## Calibrations from the supplied script

**Abstract marketer phrasing:** “Despite successful weight reduction, digestive discomfort continued to undermine my confidence.”

**User's natural phrasing:** “My jeans were getting looser but I was still picking outfits around how my stomach felt.”

The second version contains a visible contradiction, familiar objects and one understandable feeling. It does not tell the viewer what emotion to have.

**Decorative transition:** “Now, let's explore the unique mechanism behind the solution.”

**Story transition:** the narrator asks what actually helps with the slowdown. The question follows the explanation and creates a reason to teach solution requirements. Keep this distinction in the actual script, not just the outline.

**Feature recital:** three ingredient names with “powerful,” “advanced” or “clinically inspired.”

**Proper UMS:** one supported action per ingredient, the gap it addresses, and a simple explanation of how. If the action is unsubstantiated, identify the factual gap in notes; do not hide it behind conversational certainty.

## Final listening pass

- Can the viewer tell what happened, what she learned and why she took the next step?
- Does the second curiosity loop follow a changed emotional stake, and does its promised answer arrive?
- Does each explanation return to an ordinary consequence or next question before introducing more biology?
- If beat labels are removed, is this still one connected account rather than assembled modules?
- Does each reaction or detail advance recognition, understanding or a decision? Remove filler that only imitates spontaneity.
- Do the hook and outcome resolve the same problem?
- Does the actual word budget fit without rushing the speaker?

Do not promise that a structure guarantees conversion. The repeatable outcome is a coherent, appropriately paced, naturally spoken script that follows the chosen brief.

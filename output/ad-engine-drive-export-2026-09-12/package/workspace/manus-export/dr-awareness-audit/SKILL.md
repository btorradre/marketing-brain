# DR Awareness Audit

Most accounts are 80-90% bottom-of-funnel without realizing it. When frequency climbs and spend stalls, the problem is almost never bidding and almost always that the account has run out of people at one awareness level. The unlock is nearly always higher up the funnel. This skill produces that diagnosis honestly enough to act on, and converts it into the next three briefs.

## Golden Nugget Doctrine (applies to every step below)

Before any hook, angle, script, concept, or audit verdict is produced, name the **golden nugget**: the single most emotionally loaded deep frame in the research — the real motive that makes buyers act — never the surface theme.

- **Topic ≠ motive.** "Memory loss" is a topic (surface hook: "my memory feels as young as when I was 25"). The frame is: "I thought I was getting dementia just like my mum did, until I discovered this." Surface angles buy mild hope/curiosity; deep frames trigger identification so strong the reader feels caught.
- **The test.** For every candidate angle ask: *is this the topic, or is this the motive?* If it's the topic, dig one layer deeper (memory loss → becoming my parent; bloat/constipation → exiled from my own dinner table; weight loss → the stolen victory).
- **Where it goes.** The golden nugget leads — very top of the piece, as the hook. Never buried in the body.
- **Deliverable.** State the golden nugget in one explicit sentence before drafting. When analyzing a reference ad/funnel/swipe instead of writing, state the nugget it's built on and whether it leads with it. If the research hasn't surfaced one, keep mining reviews/community language/surveys until it does — never default to a surface angle.

## Operating rules

1. **Assess honestly.** If every ad is product-aware, say so plainly. Do not soften a diagnosis to make the account look healthier.
2. **Cite evidence.** Every awareness-level assignment quotes the specific hook or opening line that proves it. An assignment with no quote is an opinion.
3. **Frequency is a diagnostic tool.** High frequency is a symptom: the audience at that level is exhausted. Name it rather than treating it as a bidding artefact.
4. **One ad, one level.** Never claim an ad is "top and bottom funnel." Assign one and defend it.
5. **Gaps are briefs.** Every level with no creative is a clear direction. Name it and say what to build there.

## How to use this

1. **Load brand context first.** Before anything else, read everything available on the brand: existing product research, prior positioning documents, house style/voice rules, and any prior angle or hook work. Never work from general category knowledge when the brand's own material answers the question.

2. **Get the real, current running set of ads.** Do not work from what someone remembers. Pull the actual data:
   - Live ads, hooks, spend, and frequency — from the ad platform's own reporting (export a report, or ask for API/dashboard access).
   - What has already shipped and its verdict — from whatever performance tracker or spreadsheet the team keeps (hook rate, hold rate, CTR, CPA, ROAS by asset).
   - The creatives themselves, so hooks can be read verbatim rather than summarized.
   If this data is not accessible, ask for an export of ad names, hooks, spend, and frequency. Never estimate a frequency number, and never infer spend distribution from ad count alone. State clearly which figures are measured and which are estimated.

3. **Section 1 — Ad-by-ad awareness audit.** For each ad, record:
   - Ad name/identifier
   - Opening hook or first line, quoted verbatim
   - **Awareness level**: unaware / problem aware / solution aware / product aware / most aware
   - **Evidence**: why this level, quoting the hook
   - **Funnel stage**: top / middle / bottom
   - The angle it belongs to, if traceable
   - Spend, frequency, and hook rate where measured

   The most common misassignment is calling a problem-aware ad "top of funnel" because it opens on a lifestyle shot. The level is set by what the hook assumes the viewer already knows, not by the production style.

4. **Section 2 — Account awareness map.**
   - Percentage of creative at each level, with counts and the denominator.
   - Percentage of **spend** at each level — this is the number that actually matters, and it usually looks worse than the creative split.
   - Overall diagnosis: top-heavy, bottom-heavy, or balanced.
   - Frequency reading: a healthy funnel sits between 2 and 4. Above 5 means the top is starved, and no amount of bottom-funnel creative will fix it.
   - Cross-check delivery decay: CPM falling while spend stays flat means delivery is decaying rather than creative failing — a different fix.

5. **Section 3 — Gap analysis.** For each underserved level: which level is missing, what that means for scale specifically, the best ad format for that level, and one example hook for that level using this product, written to brand voice rules (see House Laws below).

6. **Section 4 — Priority brief directions.** The top three briefs to write next, in priority order, each with a one-sentence rationale tied to the gap it closes. Where an existing angle already serves that gap, reference it by name rather than inventing a fresh direction.

7. **Write the audit and update tracking.** Save the finished audit as a standalone document. As a side effect: flag any angle whose creative shows frequency above 5 as "fatigued," and record a 30-day verdict on any angle that has one.

8. **Self-audit before presenting.** Confirm: every level assignment carries a quoted hook, every percentage carries its denominator, no estimated figure is presented as measured, and the diagnosis is not softened.

9. **Close with two lines**: the starved level, and the recommended next step — usually a broader funnel-strategy rebuild if the whole architecture needs rework, or a focused set of new hooks if the gap is narrow (one brief wide).

## The awareness levels, defined

Assignments only mean something if the vocabulary is locked:

- **Unaware** — doesn't know the problem exists as a named thing. The hook names a symptom or feeling, never the product.
- **Problem aware** — knows the problem, hasn't connected it to a category of solution. The hook can name the problem directly but not the fix.
- **Solution aware** — knows solutions exist (possibly has tried one that failed). The hook can lead with a failed-solution beat.
- **Product aware** — knows this specific product exists, hasn't bought yet. The hook can name the product and its mechanism.
- **Most aware** — ready to buy, just needs the deal. The hook is offer, proof, and urgency.

One angle is one problem — never a claim. A hook argues about that problem; it does not redefine it. Writing a claim into the "angle" field is the single most common error in this kind of audit and it collapses the whole analysis, because the level assigned to a claim isn't the level of the person it's aimed at.

## House laws (govern every step above)

**Law 1 — Brand context loads first, always.** Never work a brand from general category knowledge when the answer is in its own research. Copy is written in the customer's own recorded language, not in marketing language about the customer.

**Law 2 — Nothing enters the output unsourced.** Every angle, pain point, objection, and transformation carries a verbatim quote and a way to trace it back to its source. Format: `"exact quote" — (source, date)`. Never invent a study, sample size, percentage, doctor, press mention, review count, or customer quote. Interpretation is allowed but must be labeled `[INFERRED]` with reasoning shown — never promoted to a quote.

**Law 3 — Topic is not motive.** The Golden Nugget Doctrine governs every output.

**Law 4 — Six-month shelf life, or it doesn't ship.** No transition angles, no day-to-night, no seasonal switch, no holiday beat as the idea itself. A seasonal word survives only as a wrapper, and only when deleting the season leaves every reason to believe intact.

**Law 5 — The swap test.** Drop a competitor's product into the angle. If it still reads perfectly, the angle sells the category, not the brand. Fix: lead with a verdict, then stack the brand's own physical facts.

**Law 6 — Competitor intelligence never becomes competitor copy.** No "unlike other brands," no generic competitor mentions, no price comparisons to unnamed competitors, no villain, no manufactured problem.

**Law 7 — Every claim gets a demo beat.** Demonstrate, never describe.

**Law 8 — The creator is never the brand.** A UGC creator says "they're," "their," "this brand" — never "our," never "we."

**Law 9 — Voice tells that kill a draft.** No em dashes anywhere. No "That's not X. It's Y." or variants. No parallel repetition stacks ("More leads. More calls. More revenue."). No rhetorical section openers ("But what does that actually mean?"). No "Imagine if" openers. No tidy tricolons or symmetrical parallelism. Never personify objects.

**Law 10 — One ad, one awareness level.** Frequency above 5 is an exhausted audience at that level, not a bidding problem.

**Law 11 — Self-audit before presenting.** Run every draft against Laws 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 before it's shown to anyone.

**Law 12 — Flag a conflict once, then build.** If a request breaks a law, say so in one or two sentences, then deliver the full thing under a stated assumption.

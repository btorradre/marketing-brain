# Sentence workshop: enough explanation, natural speech

These are original teaching examples, not competitor quotes. LoopDock and the products below are fictional. Facts and personal experiences labeled as supplied are assumptions for the exercise, not claims available for another product. Transfer the editing decisions, not the fictional details.

## 1. Repair a missing causal link

Exercise facts: LoopDock has separate cable channels and reusable straps. The straps retain each coiled cable. A supplied customer account describes loose cables getting tangled in a shared pouch. No time-saving measurement is available.

**Under-explained**

“My cables were a mess. Then I found LoopDock. It has channels and straps, so everything is easier.”

The feature names do not explain what changed. “Easier” leaves the result unspecified.

**Over-explained**

“Whenever multiple cables occupy the same storage area, their elongated shapes can interact through repeated movement, creating overlapping arrangements. A dedicated channel introduces separation between these objects. The strap then maintains the coiled configuration of the cable, limiting its interaction with the other cables in adjacent channels.”

This makes an ordinary action difficult to picture and takes three formal sentences to describe separation and retention.

**Calibrated**

“In my old pouch, every cable was loose, so pulling out my charger dragged the headphones out with it. LoopDock gives each cable its own channel. The strap keeps it coiled there, so I can lift out one cable without emptying the pouch.”

The passage retains the necessary chain: loose storage → familiar problem → separation and retention → relevant use. The longer sentences carry relationships; the short middle sentence introduces the feature. The final use claim belongs to the exercise's described functionality, not a quantified performance promise.

**Decision:** a simple physical feature does not need an invented technical mechanism.

## 2. Repair a technical-name shortcut

Exercise facts: a hypothetical filter uses activated carbon to reduce chlorine taste. The provided product evidence establishes that role only. It does not establish removal of pathogens or every contaminant.

**Under-explained**

“Activated carbon fixes the water.”

**Over-explained**

“Activated carbon is a porous carbonaceous material with an extensive internal surface structure that facilitates adsorption through interactions at numerous available surface sites.”

**Calibrated**

“The filter contains activated carbon. Its porous surface captures compounds that cause chlorine taste as water passes through. That's the specific taste problem this filter is designed to reduce.”

The relevant meaning replaces the technical recital. Do not expand the mechanism into “pure,” “toxin-free,” or “safe from bacteria.” If an explanation's scientific detail is not supported by the supplied evidence, verify it or use a narrower feature statement.

**Decision:** define a term by the job the buyer needs to understand, not by everything known about it.

## 3. Keep cause and consequence together

Exercise facts: the pouch in example 1.

**Over-compressed**

“Loose cables. Tangled together. Pull one. Everything comes out. Every morning. Again.”

**Overloaded**

“Because my cables were loose in a pouch that I moved between bags every morning while trying to pack for work, pulling one of them out, which was usually my charger, meant that everything else would come out too and I would need to put it all back before leaving.”

**Calibrated**

“My cables were loose in the pouch, so pulling out my charger usually brought the rest with it. I'd put them back and deal with the same tangle the next morning.”

The first sentence contains a cause and its direct consequence. The second adds recurrence. Breaking the first into four fragments would remove the relationship the listener needs; adding commute details would distract from it.

**Decision:** sentence length follows thought complexity. Shorter is not automatically clearer.

## 4. Fix ambiguity before adding detail

Exercise facts: LoopDock has a rigid base that prevents the organizer itself from collapsing when moved. Its straps retain coiled cables.

**Ambiguous**

“It keeps it in place, so it doesn't move when you move it.”

**Calibrated**

“The strap holds the coiled cable in its channel. The rigid base keeps the organizer from folding when I move it to another bag.”

There are two features with different jobs. Naming each subject is worth the extra words. Do not imply the rigid base is what straps the cable down.

**Decision:** sometimes the correct fix increases word count while reducing mental effort.

## 5. Use one analogy, then return to the literal process

Exercise facts: an organizer separates cables into individual channels. It does not prevent cable wear or repair damaged cables.

**Over-explained or misleading**

“It's a traffic controller for your cables, a personal filing cabinet and a protective fortress that keeps every connection healthy.”

**Calibrated**

“Think of each channel as a parking space for one cable. The strap holds that cable in its own space instead of leaving every cable loose in one pouch.”

Only one analogy is used, and it is immediately mapped to the actual feature. The example does not turn organization into protection against electrical failure. If the literal explanation is already simpler, skip the analogy altogether.

**Decision:** an analogy earns its place by clarifying one relationship. It is not additional evidence.

## 6. Build the bridge before the product list

Exercise facts: the viewer wants separated cables that stay organized when transferred between bags. LoopDock has channels, straps, and a rigid base. No exclusivity evidence is supplied.

**Under-explained bridge**

“So you need better organization. Buy LoopDock. Channels, straps, rigid base, premium design.”

**Over-explained bridge**

“Any organizer requires a highly intentional combination of features, each selected to contribute to the comprehensive organization system that allows the product to function as a complete solution to multiple organizational needs.”

**Calibrated**

“I wanted each cable separate, and I wanted the whole setup to stay together when I changed bags. That's why these three features matter: channels for separation, straps to keep the cables coiled, and a base that doesn't fold during the move. LoopDock puts them in one organizer.”

The first sentence establishes requirements. The next maps each feature to a job. The product answers the selection question. A product-aware demonstration can name LoopDock first and explain the same relationships afterward.

**Decision:** “complete” must refer to specific established requirements, not serve as an unsupported superlative.

## 7. Remove repetition without removing useful recognition

Exercise facts: a supplied customer describes repeatedly unpacking a pouch to find a charger and wanting that routine to change.

**Repetitive**

“I was frustrated. It was so frustrating. I couldn't find my charger. It was a mess every morning. It felt chaotic. I was tired of dealing with all that frustration.”

**Calibrated**

“Every morning, I'd empty the pouch just to find my charger. I wanted to grab one cable and get on with my day.”

One scene establishes the frustration; one desire makes its consequence clear. More emotion words do not deepen the scene. However, do not strip an emotionally important scene merely because it contains no new product fact.

**Decision:** retain the lived consequence; remove interchangeable declarations of how bad it felt.

## 8. Know when more explanation would fabricate a claim

Exercise facts: a supplement contains magnesium and ginger. The brief provides no dose, finished-product trial, approved outcome claim, or evidence for a disease-specific mechanism.

**Invalid expansion**

“This formula restarts the digestive signals your medication switched off and clears the backup overnight.”

Polishing that sentence cannot make the unsupported causal and timing claims valid. Do not borrow a competitor's explanation to fill the gap.

**Appropriate response**

For a product-specific mechanism VSL, ask for the approved benefit, doses, and supporting material in one targeted request. Draft the independent audience/format outline if useful. If the user wants a narrow ingredients-only introduction, write within the confirmed facts and avoid implying a clinical result.

**Decision:** a missing factual premise is different from a missing explanatory sentence. The first needs evidence or a narrower claim; the second needs clearer writing.

## 9. Compress a short script by narrowing the job

Exercise brief: 20-second, product-aware demonstration of LoopDock's bag-to-bag transfer. No need to persuade a cold audience about every organizational frustration.

**Wrong compression**

Remove all explanation and retain a feature list, several emotional promises, and a hurried offer.

**Calibrated scope**

“Moving to another bag? Lift out LoopDock and take your cables with it. Each cable stays coiled in its own strapped channel, and the rigid base keeps the organizer from folding during the move. See the layout on the product page.”

The copy selects one use case. It omits an origin story, failed purchases, and secondary objections while preserving the feature-to-function relationship. Delivery time still needs checking against the speaker's pace; this example is not a rendered 20-second claim.

**Decision:** when duration shrinks, narrow the promise before breaking the explanation.

# Compare competitor ad copy strategies — Session Summary

**Date:** 2026-03-26
**Category:** competitor-analysis
**Transcript:** [[conversation-log/transcripts/2026-03-26_compare-competitor-ad-copy-strategies_e5373f5e.md]]

## What Happened
Scraped and analyzed Alevia's 4 active Meta ads to benchmark their copy strategy. Extracted full ad texts, measured lengths, identified architecture patterns (mechanism-dominant vs. story-dominant), and then used the findings to make 4 major updates to the long-form-copy skill.

## Key Decisions Made
- Alevia runs BOTH architectures: short mechanism-dominant (~850 words) and long story-dominant (3,300-5,500 words) — average ~3,400 words
- Four new additions to long-form-copy skill based on Alevia analysis:
  1. **Mechanism-Dominant Architecture** — inverted ratio (30-40% emotional / 60-70% mechanism) with symptom stacking replacing extended emotional clearing
  2. **Law 10-B: The Inevitable Stumble** — hybrid product entry where mechanism builds criteria so tight only one product qualifies, but narrator still "stumbles" across it during research
  3. **Law 11-B: The Category Differentiator** — one binary technical comparison that kills every generic alternative (cold-extracted vs. heat-extracted, etc.)
  4. **Close Type 9: The Instruction Close** — product name, differentiator, usage instruction, urgency — four sentences max

## Insights & Learnings
- Alevia's mechanism-dominant ads (like the parasites/Amla ad at ~850 words) prove that short-form can work in health supplements when the mechanism is simple enough to educate quickly
- "Then I found X" is banned as too endorsement-like — the new pattern has the narrator collide with the product accidentally during research while the mechanism's criteria make it intellectually inevitable
- The Category Differentiator is a one-to-two sentence move placed immediately after the product name drops — it's not a section, it's a surgical strike

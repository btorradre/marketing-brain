Our house had a smell and I couldn't figure out where it was coming from.

Not like something rotting. Not like garbage or mold or anything you could point to. Just this stale, heavy thing that was always sort of there. In the living room in the morning. In the bedroom if the door had been closed overnight. Around my husband's recliner.

I figured it was the house. We've been here 31 years. Old carpet, old air ducts. Houses just get a smell after a while. That's what I told myself.

So I cleaned. And I mean I CLEANED.

Carpets, curtains, threw out half the pillows. Three kinds of air fresheners. Had the couch steam cleaned. Got new fancy soap for both of us.

It would smell better for a day. Maybe two. Then that same staleness came right back. Like it was part of the house now.

And the thing is, people had started pulling away from us too.

Our neighbor used to come inside when she dropped off our mail. Then she started just handing it to me at the door. A friend I've had for years used to take turns having coffee at each other's houses. Suddenly it was always a restaurant. A woman who sat next to me at church for years moved over two seats.

And the worst part — my daughter stopped bringing my granddaughter Lily over on Sundays.

That was our thing. Sunday pancakes at Nana's house. Lily would "help" me mix the batter and get flour everywhere and I'd pretend to be mad and she'd laugh until she got the hiccups.

Then the excuses started. Lily had a playdate. A stomach ache. Soccer — on a Sunday. Three weeks in a row something came up.

I asked my daughter if something was wrong. She said no, Lily's just getting older and busier.

She was 7. Seven year olds are not busy.

Nobody was telling me what was going on. They were all just quietly pulling away at the same time.

And one day I was standing in the hallway and I just stopped. Because I knew where I'd smelled that smell before.

My grandmother's apartment.

When I was little, Grandma Kay's place had this exact same stale warmth. It was always there. I loved her so much but I remember holding my breath when she hugged me and pressing my face into her sweater so she wouldn't notice.

And now my house smelled the same way. At 67 years old, my house smelled like Grandma Kay's apartment.

I tried not to think about what that meant. But I started doing this thing where I'd go outside for ten minutes, breathe fresh air, and walk back in. And every single time — there it was.

I was starting to think this is just what happens when you get older. Your house smells. People stop coming over. Your world gets smaller.

There's a woman at our church named Margaret. She's 74, Japanese-American. We've been church friends for about twelve years.

One Sunday after service we were folding tablecloths in the kitchen and I just told her. About Lily. About people pulling away. About the smell I couldn't fix.

"I don't know if it's the house or if it's us, Margaret. I honestly don't know anymore."

She put the tablecloth down and looked right at me.

"It's you. It's not the house. You've been around it so long you can't even smell it on yourself anymore."

I just stood there. Because she was right. I only ever noticed the smell when I came back inside after being out. The rest of the time I had no idea. My husband had no idea. We were just living in it.

She said it's something called nonenal. As you get older, the oils on your skin start to break down. Like how a sliced apple turns brown sitting on the counter. When that happens, your skin makes this thing called nonenal. That's what the smell is. It has nothing to do with being clean or dirty. It just happens after a certain age. Like gray hair.

The problem is that nonenal sticks to your skin in a way that regular soap just isn't strong enough to break down. You can shower twice a day with the nicest soap in the world and it doesn't matter. It's still on you when you get out.

And that's when I realized — it's been getting on everything this whole time. Every time we sat on the couch, laid on our pillows, wore our clothes. It was building up on every surface in the house, day after day. We weren't living in a house that smelled. We were making it smell. Just by being in it.

Grandma Kay's apartment? Same thing. It was never her apartment. It was her.

I asked Margaret what you're supposed to do about it if soap doesn't work.

She said in Japan everybody knows about this. They even have a word for it — "kareishū" — it just means aging scent. It's not something embarrassing there. It's just a normal thing that happens. Like gray hair.

And they figured out how to deal with it a long time ago. There's something in persimmon fruit that grabs onto nonenal and actually breaks it down. Doesn't cover it up with perfume. Actually dissolves it right on your skin so it never gets the chance to get on your furniture or your clothes or anything else. That's why regular soap doesn't work but this does.

In Japan, persimmon soap is just normal soap. Older people use it the way we use Dove.

Margaret's own mother used it her whole life. Margaret has been using it since she was 60. She told me to make sure I get one that uses real persimmon extract and not just persimmon scent because some of them are basically just regular soap with a label on it. She wrote down the exact one her mother always used.

When her mother came to America she told Margaret "you will need this some day and nobody here is going to tell you about it."

I asked Margaret why SHE never told me.

She got quiet and said "how do you tell a friend something like that?"

She's right. Nobody tells you. They just stop coming over.

I sat in my kitchen that night thinking — how has this been known for this long and I've never heard a word about it?

I ordered it that same night. I figured worst case it doesn't work and I just go back to my old soap.

It's just soap. Nothing fancy. You use it like normal soap in the shower.

After about three days my husband said the pillowcases smelled different. Lighter. Then I noticed the recliner. The throw blanket. Even the hallway — the one that used to smell like Grandma Kay's place. The staleness was going away. Not because I scrubbed anything. Because the source had stopped.

After about a week I walked in from the store and just stood there. There was nothing. No stale air hitting me at the door. Just a house.

I had a friend over for coffee at MY house. First time in months. She stayed two hours.

About three weeks in, my daughter called. "Hey Mom, Lily's been asking about making pancakes."

I couldn't even talk for a second.

That Sunday Lily came through the door and pulled her chair up to the counter before I even had the flour out. She stood right next to me the whole time. Elbow bumping my arm while she stirred. And she didn't hold her breath.

I think about Grandma Kay a lot now. Nobody told her either. She probably never knew why little me pushed my face into her sweater every time I hugged her.

If your family visits got shorter, or your grandkids act different at your house, or you've been cleaning and cleaning and nothing helps — it might not be the house. I blamed the carpet for months. It was us the whole time.

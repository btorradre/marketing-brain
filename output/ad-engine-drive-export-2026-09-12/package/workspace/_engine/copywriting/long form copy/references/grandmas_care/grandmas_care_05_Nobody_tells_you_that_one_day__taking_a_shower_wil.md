Nobody tells you that one day, taking a shower will feel like going to the gym.

I don't mean a little tiring. I mean gripping the towel rack at 7 in the morning, trying to talk your legs into stepping over the edge of the tub — and your whole body saying no. Not today.

I'm 73. And that's where I am now. But it didn't happen overnight.

Five years ago I was still fine. I'd get up, shower, do my hair, put my lipstick on before I walked out the door. 

That's who I've always been. 
The woman who shows up looking like she's got it together. 
I don't know how to be anything else.

Then my hip started going. Slowly at first. A little stiffness getting out of bed. Then one morning I stepped into the tub and my knee buckled and I grabbed the shower curtain rod so hard I nearly pulled it out of the wall.

I didn't fall. But after that, every single time I stood on that wet tile, all I could think was — what if I do? 

What if this is the one? The fall where somebody has to come take care of me?

I will not be that person. Not yet.

So the showers got less frequent. 
Not because I stopped caring. 
Because my body started making it a fight every single morning.

The dogs need out at six. I get dressed. And once I'm dressed and moving, the thought of stripping down again, getting the water right, standing on that tile, twenty minutes on my feet, getting out, drying off, getting dressed all over —
Not today. Tomorrow.
And then the next day. And the next.

I think about it constantly. 
This voice that won't stop — just do it, what's wrong with you, just get in. 
It drives me crazy because I know it shouldn't be this hard. 
But knowing that doesn't make my hip stop aching.

And here's the thing — on the days I did fight through it, when I used the nice lavender soap my daughter bought me and stayed in for twenty minutes because honestly, once I'm in there, I love it — I'd step out feeling wonderful. Clean. Like myself.

And it would last until about dinnertime.

Then something would settle back in. Not the lavender. 

Something heavy and stale underneath it. Like the morning never happened.

At first I didn't think much of it. I figured — it's been ten hours, of course. That's normal. Just shower again tomorrow.

But tomorrow was another fight with my body. And most days, my body won.

So the smell would sit. Another day. I'd spray a little perfume, change my blouse, tell myself it was fine.
That's how it starts. You manage. You get by.
But over the months, it added up. 

The staleness wasn't just on me anymore. 
It was settling into the couch where I sit every evening. My pillowcases. The cardigan I wear around the house. This heaviness I couldn't get ahead of, no matter what I did on the days I managed to shower.
And then the little things started.

My granddaughter — she's seven, used to tuck herself right under my arm every Sunday on the couch — started sitting on the other end. Not dramatically. Just a little further each time.

My neighbor Linda, who I've had Tuesday coffee with for eleven years, started opening her kitchen window when I came over. In February.

Nobody said a word. That's what gets you. They don't say anything. They just quietly create distance. And you sit there watching your world shrink, one person at a time, wondering if you're imagining it.

You're not imagining it. And somewhere inside, you know.
I stopped going to Linda's. Told my daughter Sundays weren't working for me anymore. 

I sat in my living room with the TV on and told myself I just needed a quiet week.

One quiet week turned into months.

And the loneliest part isn't the empty house. 

It's believing you did this to yourself. 

That if you could just get into that shower every morning like you used to, none of this would be happening. That you're the problem.

That's what I believed for almost two years.
Carol was the one person who still came by. She lives two streets over, about my age, same hip problems. She never made me feel like something was wrong. She just showed up. Some people are like that.

One afternoon in March, she came over with a bar of soap and set it on my kitchen table like it was nothing.
Her son married a Japanese woman a few years back. Sweet girl. 
For Carol's birthday, this daughter-in-law had given her a set of soap from Japan. Persimmon soap. Made from the fruit. Carol said she'd never heard of it either. Apparently in Japan this is just what older people use. Normal as Dove is here.
She'd been using it about two months. And she said something I almost didn't catch.
She said the stale smell that used to come back every evening — same thing I'd been dealing with — just stopped coming back. Not covered up. Not perfumed over. Gone.

And she said she could go two, three days between showers and still feel fresh. Actually fresh.
I looked at her like she'd lost her mind. Two or three days? I couldn't make it to dinnertime.
She said just try it.

So I did. Thursday morning. My hip was cooperating for once, so I got in, used the persimmon bar instead of my regular soap. Nothing special about the routine. Scrubbed, hot water, got out. Didn't expect anything.
That evening I was on the couch watching the news and I realized I was bracing for it — that heavy, stale thing that always crept back around dinner.
It wasn't there.

I lifted my collar. Nothing. Just fabric.
Friday morning — I hadn't showered, my hip was bad — I lifted my sleeve. Nothing.
Saturday. Still clean.
I stood in my bathroom on Saturday afternoon holding my shirt to my face and I cried. Because it smelled like a shirt. Just a clean, normal shirt. Three days after one shower.
That had not happened in years.
I called Carol that night. I said — what is this? Why does this work?

She told me what her daughter-in-law had explained. After a certain age — around 55 or so — your skin starts making something called nonenal. It's not sweat. 
It's not dirt. It's what happens when the oils on your skin start breaking down, like how a sliced apple turns brown on the counter. It just happens as you get older. Like gray hair.

In Japan everybody knows about it. They have a word — kareishū. Aging scent. It's not embarrassing there. It's just something that happens, and they deal with it.
The reason nothing ever lasted for me — the reason I only got six hours before the smell came back — is that regular soap can't wash off nonenal. 
It handles sweat and dirt just fine. But nonenal sticks to your skin in a way that normal soap slides right over. So you step out of the shower smelling like lavender, thinking you're clean — but the nonenal is still there. And a few hours later, that's all you can smell.
That's why the freshness never held. The soap was washing off everything except the one thing actually causing the smell.

Persimmon does what regular soap can't. Something in the fruit grabs onto nonenal and breaks it apart. Dissolves it right on your skin. Not masks it. Removes it. So it's genuinely gone — not sitting there rebuilding on your clothes and your couch and your pillowcases hour after hour.
And because the nonenal is actually gone, there's nothing left to come back. That's why the freshness holds. Not six hours. Days.

When Carol told me that, the last two years made sense. Every evening the smell crept back. Every time Linda opened her window. Every Sunday my granddaughter sat a little further away. I wasn't failing. 
My soap was just never built to handle what my skin was actually producing.

That was four months ago.
I shower every second or third day now. That's what my body can manage. And for the first time in years, it's enough. Actually enough.
The thing I didn't expect — it wasn't just the freshness. It was the fight that stopped. When you know one shower lasts two or three days, you stop dreading the tub every morning. The voice in your head goes quiet. You shower when your body says yes instead of forcing it when your body says no. That's what gave me my life back more than anything.

I don't lie awake worrying about who might stop by tomorrow. I don't change my shirt at noon. I don't cancel on my own family.
My granddaughter sat right next to me last Sunday. Tucked herself under my arm like nothing ever changed. Didn't say a word about it. Just came back.

I went to Linda's for coffee again last month. She didn't open the window. We sat at her kitchen table for two hours and laughed about things I can't even remember now. I just remember sitting there thinking — I missed this so much.

I have my days back. The energy I used to burn fighting the bathtub or worrying about the smell — I spend it on the dogs, on cooking, on being outside. On actually living, instead of just getting through.

If you know what it's like to stand at that tub and not have it in you — if you know the guilt, the voice that won't stop — and if you've noticed that even when you do get in, it doesn't hold the way it should —

It might not be about how often you shower. 

It might be about what your soap is leaving behind.

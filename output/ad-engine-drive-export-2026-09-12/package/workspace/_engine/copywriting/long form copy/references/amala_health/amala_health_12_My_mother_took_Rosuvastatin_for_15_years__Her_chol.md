My mother was a sharp woman. I need you to understand that before anything else.

She was an executive assistant to the CFO of a manufacturing company for twenty-two years. Managed his calendar, his correspondence, his travel, his files. That was the brain she had before the medication.

At 58, her doctor put her on Rosuvastatin. 10 milligrams. LDL was 181. Family history of heart disease — her father had died of a heart attack at 71. The medication worked. On paper. Her LDL dropped to 103, then settled around 96 where it stayed for fifteen years. Her doctor was delighted. "Your numbers are textbook, Margaret."

The first thing I noticed was the sticky notes.

My mother had never needed lists. She kept everything in her head — groceries, appointments, birthdays, phone numbers. All organized, all accessible, all perfect.

My own birthday. On a sticky note. From the woman who'd never forgotten a single date in her life.

I asked her about it. She laughed. "Getting older, I guess. Memory's not what it used to be."

She was 61. That's not old.

And her cholesterol? 96. Perfect.

"Your numbers look wonderful, Margaret. Keep doing what you're doing."

I don't think she was sad. I think she was terrified. Because she knew something was happening that she couldn't stop.

And her cholesterol was still perfect.

One morning — I'll never forget this — she looked at the framed photo on the hallway wall. Our family photo. Taken at Christmas. Her, my father, me, my husband, my daughter.

She touched the glass. Touched the image of herself.

"Who is this?" she asked.

My throat closed.

"That's you, Mom."

She frowned. Looked closer. Shook her head.

"No. That's not me."

She was 72. Her LDL was 94. The medication was working perfectly.

She died at 74. Pneumonia. Her body just stopped fighting. But my mother had already been gone for years. The woman who remembered every phone number, every birthday, every meeting — she'd been gone since somewhere around year eight. What was left was her body, walking the hallways of my house, looking for someone or something she couldn't name.

And the whole time — the WHOLE TIME — she took her Rosuvastatin every single night. Her cholesterol was perfect. Her doctor was satisfied.

Nobody ever asked whether the medication that was lowering her cholesterol was also lowering the cholesterol her brain needed to survive.

Eight months ago, I was measuring my hallway for grab bars.

It started three years ago, about four months after I finished chemo.

They told me the chemo might cause some tingling. "Peripheral neuropathy," the oncologist called it. "It usually resolves on its own within a few months."
It didn't resolve.

The tingling never went away. It settled into my toes like it had moved in permanently. A low-grade buzzing that I noticed most at night.

I mentioned it at my follow-up. My oncologist said, "Give it time. The nerves are still recovering."

So I gave it time.

Six months. A year.

The tingling didn't recover. It turned into burning.

Not a gentle warmth — a deep, searing heat across the soles of both feet that started every evening around 7 p.m. and didn't let up until morning.

I'd lie in bed staring at the ceiling with my feet sticking out from under the covers, tears running down my temples, thinking: I survived cancer for this?

I survived the diagnosis. The surgery. The chemo that made me lose my hair, my appetite, and 22 pounds I couldn't afford to lose.

I rang the bell. I was supposed to be done.

Because at least with cancer, there was a plan. A protocol. An end date.

With neuropathy, there's nothing. Just "manage it."
My doctor's first move? Gabapentin. 300 milligrams, three times a day.

I trusted her. I'd trusted her through cancer. So I took every pill.

The first two weeks, it dulled the burning. Maybe 20 percent.

But by week three, I was a different person.

I couldn't remember my students' names — children I'd taught for years. I'd run into former students at the grocery store and stand there smiling while my brain scrambled for a name I'd said ten thousand times.

And the fog. God, the fog.

My son would call and I'd forget what we talked about by the time I hung up. I started writing everything down — conversations, appointments, where I parked my car. My kitchen counter looked like a crime scene investigation board. Sticky notes everywhere.

I told my doctor the gabapentin was ruining me.
She increased the dose.

$140 a month for a pill that made me drowsier, foggier, and didn't stop the burning.

A retired teacher on a pension. Paying $140 a month to feel worse.

Nobody pointed out the absurdity.

While the medications were failing me, the neuropathy was advancing.

The numbness crept from my toes through the soles of my feet. Then to my arches. Then past my ankles.
I couldn't feel the floor.

People who don't have neuropathy don't understand what that means. It means you stand up and your brain gets no feedback from your feet. No texture. No temperature. No sense of where the ground is relative to your body.

So you compensate. You grip things. You shuffle. You stop trusting your own legs.

I stopped going to the farmer's market on Saturdays — the thing I looked forward to most every week — because the uneven ground terrified me.

I stopped visiting my former school for the holiday concert because I couldn't navigate the crowded hallway.
I stopped gardening. I stopped walking the neighborhood. I stopped accepting dinner invitations because I was afraid of falling in someone else's house.

My world shrank to my living room, my bedroom, and the path between them.

I used to be the kind of woman who could talk to a wall and the wall would talk back.

Church every Sunday, stayed an hour after just catching up. At my daughter Karen's house so much her husband set a place for me without asking. Ran the bake sale twelve years. Knew every checker at the Kroger by name.

Sixty-five years old and I was still that person.

Then I was at the store one afternoon, standing in the card aisle. A woman came around the corner with her cart, got close to me, and her face changed. Her nose scrunched up — quick, like a reflex. The kind of face you make before you can catch yourself.

She backed up and went the other way.

Now maybe that was nothing. Maybe she forgot something. I told myself that the whole way home.

But once you see something like that, you can't unsee it.

Karen hugging me at the door but letting go real quick. My grandbaby Lily — used to crawl into my lap and stay — wiggling off after a minute. The neighbor suddenly always in a hurry.

Were these things always happening? I didn't know. But I couldn't shut it off.

Then one morning I smelled something on my pillow. So faint I almost wasn't sure it was real. Just stale. A little sweet, a little off. I changed the pillowcase. Next morning — there it was again.

And that's when the really ugly thoughts started.

Maybe this is just what happens when you get older. Maybe your body turns on you and everybody knows except you.

My grandmother — God rest her — always said "Cleanliness is next to Godliness." And I've been a clean person my whole life. Same soap for thirty years.

But what do you do when clean isn't working?

That's when I started pulling away. And I want to be honest — nobody told me to stop coming around. I did it to myself.

I stopped going to church — what if the person next to me can smell it. Stopped dropping in at Karen's — what if that's why Lily won't sit in my lap. Stopped the bake sale, the farmers market, stopped picking up the phone.

I told myself I was just tired. Slowing down. That this is what sixty-five looks like.

But the real thought — the one I couldn't say out loud — was: maybe the world is done with me. Maybe after a certain age you're just supposed to step aside and stop bothering people.

And let me tell you about being lonely. Not alone — alone is fine. Lonely is when you're sitting in your kitchen at 2 in the afternoon and you realize the last voice you heard was the TV three days ago. When the phone doesn't ring and there's nothing on your calendar between now and next week.

That was my life for almost five months.

And I might still be sitting in that kitchen if it weren't for my friend Keiko.

Keiko is sixty-eight. Three years older than me. And I could not figure out how her life looked so different.

She was out every day. Community center, walking group, grandkids every weekend. People pulled toward her, not away.

I told myself — she's Japanese, her family's from Osaka. They just age different. That was the story I needed so I didn't have to ask the real question.

But one afternoon she came by with soup and I told her everything. The store. The pillow. How lonely I'd been. How I thought my body was just giving up on me and everybody could tell but me.

She didn't interrupt. Didn't tell me I was being silly. She just sat there and listened. Then she looked at me and said — you know what your problem is? It's not what you think.

She told me that in Japan, they don't pretend aging doesn't change things. But they don't hide from it either. They deal with it. She said: "Every morning you get up, you get dressed properly, you take care of yourself. That's just living. It's a project every day."

And I wanted to say — Keiko, I DO shower every day. That's the whole problem. I'm doing everything right and it's still not working.

That's when she told me something I'd never heard in my life.

She said when you get older, the oils on your skin start breaking down. She said think of it like a sliced apple sitting on the counter — how it turns brown just from the air hitting it. Same kind of thing happens on your skin. And when it does, your body produces something called nonenal. That's what the smell is.

It has nothing to do with being clean or dirty. It just happens after a certain age. Like gray hair.

I just sat there. Because nobody — not my doctor, not a single person — had ever told me this.

But here's the part that really got me.

Nonenal doesn't just sit on your skin like dirt. It bonds to it. Gets into the surface in a way that regular soap — no matter how expensive, no matter how long you scrub — isn't strong enough to break apart. You can shower twice a day and it's still there when you get out.

And your own nose adjusts to it. You stop smelling it on yourself. But the people around you don't.

That was my pillow. That stale smell every morning. It wasn't the pillowcase. It was nonenal, soaking into everything — my sheets, my clothes, my robe. Everything that touched my skin carried it.

I felt like the floor dropped out. Because all those months — the store, Karen, Lily — I thought I was getting pushed out. I thought I was too old. And it was something on my skin I couldn't even smell.

In Japan, Keiko said, everybody knows about this. They even have a word for it — kareishū. It just means aging scent. It's not embarrassing over there. It's just normal — like gray hair. And they figured out how to deal with it a long time ago.

There's something in persimmon fruit — in the skin of the fruit — that grabs onto nonenal and actually breaks it apart. Doesn't cover it up with perfume. Doesn't just mask it. It dissolves it right at the surface so it never gets the chance to soak into your clothes or your sheets.

That's why regular soap doesn't work. Regular soap washes away dirt and oil. But nonenal isn't dirt. It's bonded to your skin. You need something that can break that bond. Persimmon does.

In Japan, persimmon soap is just normal. Older people use it the way we use Dove. Nobody thinks twice about it.

Here in America? Nobody talks about it. You're just supposed to figure it out on your own — or not figure it out, and wonder why people stopped coming around.

Keiko handed me a bar that week. Not some random bar from the store — her family had been using this one since she was a girl. Made the traditional way, with real persimmon extract. She said most of what you'd find in America barely has enough in it to do anything. This was the one her mother used. And her grandmother.

I wasn't convinced. But I also hadn't talked to a friend in four days, and my pillow smelled like something I couldn't wash out. So I figured — what's the worst that happens.

Within three days the pillowcase smelled different. By the end of the week — nothing. Not perfume. Not soap. Just clean. The way my pillow smelled five years ago. I put my face in it and I cried.

And here's what surprised me. It wasn't just the pillow. Something shifted in my head. I kept hearing Keiko's voice: it's a project every day. You get up. You take care of yourself. Not because someone's watching. Because that's how you live.

I put on earrings the next morning. A nice top. Not because I had anywhere to be — because I felt like someone who should.

Two weeks later I went back to church. Sandra — she sits two rows behind me — grabbed my arm after and said "Where have you BEEN?" She didn't make a face. She pulled me closer.

Three weeks after that, Karen dropped by on a Saturday. Hadn't done that in months. She sat at my table, drank coffee, and when she left she hugged me. Really hugged me. The kind where nobody lets go first.

Then Lily came back. Friday sleepovers. Little arms around my neck that held on.

My sister-in-law Donna — I'd told her what Keiko told me, felt like I had to — she ordered a bar of her own. Called me four days later. She said "Ruthie, my husband hugged me this morning and just stayed there. He hasn't done that in I don't know how long."

I couldn't say anything. I just cried again. Because I knew exactly what she meant.

They didn't come back because of a bar of soap. They came back because I did. Because I stopped sitting in that kitchen. Because I started treating myself like someone who still had something to give.

But I'll be honest — I don't think I come back without the soap. Because the soap is what proved Keiko right. It proved that nothing was wrong with me. It proved that this wasn't aging catching up. It was one thing — one specific thing — and it had a fix.

Nobody in your life will tell you. That's the cruel part. They won't say you smell — they'll just come around less. And you'll blame it on getting old.

But that's not what it is.

If any of this sounds like your life right now — the pulling away, the loneliness, that feeling that something changed but you can't name it — I'm telling you what I wish somebody told me a year ago.

Every day I waited was another day in that kitchen. Another Friday without Lily. Another morning staring at a pillow I couldn't fix. I can't get those five months back. But I can make sure I don't lose another one.

The 30-Year-Old Constipation Protocol That Was Never Built for GLP-1 Patients—And What Happens When You Follow It Anyway

A pharmacist who fills 200+ GLP-1 prescriptions a month explains why fiber, Miralax, and probiotics keep failing—and what she tells patients when their doctors won’t

Gut Health Insider | March 2026 | 6 min read

Rachel Yuen has filled over 4,000 GLP-1 prescriptions in the last two years. Ozempic. Wegovy. Mounjaro. Zepbound. She’s the pharmacist at a high-volume retail pharmacy in suburban New Jersey, and she sees the same pattern play out so often she could time it with a stopwatch.

“Week one, they pick up the injection. Week three, they’re back for Miralax. Week six, they’re adding fiber gummies. Week ten, they’re at the counter asking me what else there is because nothing’s working.”

She pauses.

“By month four, about a third of them are picking up a prescription for something stronger. And by month six, some of them tell me they quit the GLP-1 entirely. Not because it didn’t work for weight loss. Because the constipation broke them.”

When I asked Rachel why she thinks the standard remedies keep failing, she didn’t hesitate.

“Because every single product in this aisle was designed to treat a problem in the colon. And that’s not where GLP-1 constipation happens.”

The Protocol That Doesn’t Know Where to Look

If you’ve told your doctor about constipation on Ozempic or Mounjaro, you already know the script. Eat more fiber. Take Miralax daily. Drink more water. If that doesn’t work, we’ll try a prescription.

That protocol is not wrong. For regular constipation, it works. It’s worked for thirty years.

But GLP-1 constipation is not regular constipation. And the protocol doesn’t distinguish between the two.

“Regular constipation is a colon problem,” Rachel explains. “The stool moves too slowly through the large intestine, loses too much water, and gets hard. Fiber adds bulk. Miralax adds water. Problem solved. But GLP-1 constipation starts six feet upstream—in the stomach. And nothing in the constipation aisle was ever designed to reach the stomach.”

This is what Rachel calls “protocol blindness.” The protocol exists. Doctors follow it. Patients follow it. Everyone is doing the right thing according to the guidelines. And it still doesn’t work—because the guidelines were written before 40 million Americans started taking a drug that paralyzes a completely different organ.

What Actually Happens Inside a GLP-1 Stomach

GLP-1 medications work by slowing gastric emptying—the rate at which food leaves the stomach and enters the small intestine. A normal stomach empties in about four hours. On semaglutide or tirzepatide, that window stretches to ten, twelve, sometimes eighteen hours.

That’s the mechanism behind the weight loss. You feel full longer. You eat less. Your appetite quiets.

But when food sits in the stomach for that long, it doesn’t just wait. It ferments.

“Bacteria start breaking down that food and producing gas—hydrogen sulfide, methane, hydrogen,” Rachel says. “The sulfur burps. The bloating that makes you look six months pregnant by 2 PM. The pressure that never lets up. That’s all fermentation. It’s happening in the stomach, not the colon.”

And here’s the part that should change how you think about every product you’ve tried:

Miralax draws water into the colon. It cannot reach the stomach.

Fiber adds bulk to the colon. On a stomach that isn’t emptying, fiber just feeds the fermentation.

Probiotics colonize the large intestine. They don’t address gastric motility.

Stool softeners work at the exit. The blockage is at the entrance.

“I watch women spend $80, $90, $100 a month cycling through products that physically cannot reach the organ where their problem is,” Rachel says. “And then they come back and ask me if they should try a different brand of the same thing. A different probiotic. A different fiber. It’s not the brand. It’s the location.”

Why the Protocol Hasn’t Caught Up

This is the question that keeps Rachel up at night. If the mechanism is this clear—medication slows the stomach, products target the colon—why hasn’t the standard of care changed?

Three reasons.

1

Speed of adoption.

Clinical guidelines take years to update. GLP-1 prescriptions went from niche to 40 million users in under three years. The protocol for managing side effects hasn’t had time to catch up with the volume of patients reporting them.

2

The appointment is too short.

A typical follow-up is twelve minutes. That’s enough time to check weight, adjust dosage, and address the top complaint. It is not enough time to explain that GLP-1 constipation originates in the stomach, why colon-targeted products can’t reach it, and what the alternative approach looks like.

3

There’s no pharma incentive.

The constipation aisle is a $4.2 billion market built on colon products. Every product—the Miralax, the Colace, the Metamucil—targets downstream. A product that supports the stomach doesn’t compete with them. It replaces the entire category. No legacy brand wants to fund that research.

“Your doctor isn’t failing you on purpose,” Rachel says. “She’s following the only protocol she was trained on. That protocol was built for colon constipation. And it works for colon constipation. It was never updated for a drug that shuts down your stomach.”

What the Stomach Actually Needs

Once you understand that GLP-1 constipation is a stomach problem being treated with colon products, the question becomes obvious: what actually works at the stomach?

Rachel breaks it into three requirements. All three have to be met. Miss one and the cycle continues.

1

Motility support—at the stomach level.

Apigenin, concentrated in celery juice extract, acts as a natural prokinetic—it promotes the contractions that push food out of the stomach. Not the colon. The stomach. The organ the medication actually slowed down.

2

Fermentation neutralization—where the gas is produced.

Chlorophyllin, a stabilized form of chlorophyll, binds directly to the sulfur compounds and methane being generated by the fermentation. It doesn’t mask the smell. It neutralizes the gas at the molecular level, in the stomach, where it’s being created.

3

Downstream support—once the stomach starts moving again.

Soluble prebiotic fiber feeds beneficial bacteria in the lower gut and produces butyrate, which keeps the colon moving smoothly once the stomach begins emptying again. This is the downstream support. Not more bulk piled onto a backed-up system.

“Every product in the constipation aisle addresses requirement three at best,” Rachel says. “None of them address one or two. You cannot fix a stomach problem with colon products. You have to go upstream.”

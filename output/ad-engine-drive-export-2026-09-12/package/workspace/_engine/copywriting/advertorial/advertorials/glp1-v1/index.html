Why Your GLP-1 Shot Makes You Feel Like Something Died Inside You

A GI specialist explains the real reason behind the sulfur burps, concrete constipation, and “rotting” feeling—and why laxatives make it worse

Gut Health Insider | October 2025 | 5 min read

If you’re on semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) or tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound)—and you feel like food is rotting in your stomach—you’re not crazy.

You did everything right. You finally found something that actually works. The “food noise” went quiet for the first time in decades. The scale is finally moving.

But now you’re dealing with something nobody warned you about.

Sulfur burps that can clear a room. Constipation so bad you haven’t gone in 11 days. That “brick in your stomach” feeling that never goes away. And the worst part? Your doctor keeps telling you to “take more Miralax” or “drink more water.”

You’ve done that. It doesn’t work.

Dr. Maria Santos, a GI specialist who has treated over 2,000 GLP-1 patients, just published research explaining why so many people on these medications feel terrible—and why everything they’ve tried has failed.

Here’s what’s actually happening inside your body:

Both semaglutide and tirzepatide work by slowing down your stomach. That’s the whole point—they make you feel full longer so you eat less.

Semaglutide (the drug in Ozempic and Wegovy) mimics a natural gut hormone called GLP-1. It signals your brain that you’re full and slows stomach emptying.

Tirzepatide (the drug in Mounjaro and Zepbound) does the same thing—but it mimics two hormones: GLP-1 and GIP. That’s why some people say it works faster. But it also means it can slow digestion even more.

For a lot of people, things slow down too much. Your stomach basically falls asleep. Food just... sits there.

And when food sits in your stomach for 12, 18, even 24 hours—it starts to ferment. Like leftovers that sat out too long. That’s where the sulfur burps come from. That’s why you can taste Tuesday’s dinner on Thursday morning.

This isn’t just “constipation.” It’s actually three problems happening at once:

1

Motility

Your stomach muscles stop moving. Food can’t get out. It’s like a conveyor belt that someone unplugged.

2

Deodorization

Food rots in your stomach, producing hydrogen sulfide gas. That’s the “rotten egg” taste you can’t escape. Nothing is neutralizing it.

3

Hydration

The stool downstream hardens into “concrete” because there’s no moisture being drawn in to soften it.

Here’s the kicker: Miralax, Dulcolax, fiber supplements—they all work on your colon. That’s the exit. Your problem is in your stomach. That’s the entrance.

Laxatives are downstream solutions for an upstream problem. It’s like trying to fix a clogged sink by turning on the faucet harder. The problem isn’t at the drain—it’s at the top of the pipe.

Why Nothing Else Has Worked

“I drank an entire bottle of Miralax over a weekend and didn’t go.” That’s a real quote from a real patient.

She wasn’t doing anything wrong. The Miralax was just working in the wrong place.

If you tried more fiber...

Bulk fiber (like Metamucil) makes it worse. You’re adding volume to a stomach that already can’t empty. It’s like putting more clothes in a dryer that won’t spin.

If you tried juicing...

Celery juice has real benefits for digestion. But drinking 16 ounces of anything when your stomach is paralyzed? That’s torture. Plus, who wants to drag out a juicer at 6 AM when you’re already exhausted?

If you tried probiotics...

Most don’t survive stomach acid anyway. And they don’t address the motility problem—the fact that your stomach muscles aren’t moving.

If you tried Zofran for nausea...

It helps with the nausea, but it makes constipation worse. You’re trading one problem for another.

The Discovery That Changed Everything

Dr. Santos was reviewing research on gastroparesis—the medical term for “paralyzed stomach”—when she noticed something interesting.

Patients who consumed celery (in any form) showed better gastric motility. Not because celery is magic—but because it contains a compound called Apigenin that helps “wake up” the nerves controlling stomach muscles.

Think of it like physical therapy for your gut. Instead of forcing things through (like a laxative), it gently nudges the muscles to start moving again.

But that was only one piece of the puzzle. Only one pathway.

What about the sulfur burps? The fermentation? The “I haven’t had a vegetable in a week because I’m terrified of what it’ll do to me” malnutrition?

Dr. Santos found that Chlorophyll—the green stuff in plants—acts like an internal deodorant. It actually neutralizes the hydrogen sulfide gas in your gut. The gas that smells like rotten eggs. That’s Pathway Two: Deodorization.

And for the constipation? She discovered that soluble prebiotic fiber (not the bulk fiber that makes things worse) actually dissolves in water and draws moisture into the stool—softening the “concrete” without adding volume to your stomach. That’s Pathway Three: Hydration.

Three pathways. Motility. Deodorization. Hydration.

Not one. All three.

“Every piece had to work together,” Dr. Santos explains. “The celery to wake up the stomach. The chlorophyll to scrub the sulfur. The soluble fiber to soften things downstream. And vitamins to fill the gaps from not being able to eat. Miss any piece and you’re still stuck.”

Why You Can’t Just “Do It Yourself”

“Can’t I just juice some celery and take chlorophyll drops?”

Dr. Santos tried that with her patients. Here’s what happened:

The volume problem. Drinking 16 ounces of celery juice when your stomach won’t empty? Most patients threw it right back up. Or just stopped doing it because it hurt.

The timing problem. Celery juice needs to be fresh. That means buying pounds of organic celery, dragging out a juicer, and cleaning up sticky green pulp—every single morning. For someone already dealing with fatigue and nausea, that’s a non-starter.

The compliance problem. Even motivated patients lasted about a week. The mess, the taste, the effort—it was too much on top of everything else they were dealing with.

“I needed something that gave them the benefits without the burden,” Dr. Santos says. “Something they could actually stick with.”

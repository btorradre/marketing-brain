# BANNED PATTERNS: THE AI COPY BLACKLIST

This is a strict negative constraint list. Every rule here is a hard prohibition. When writing or editing any copy, scan the draft against this entire file before delivering output. Any sentence that matches a banned pattern gets rewritten — no exceptions, no judgment calls, no "it works here." If it's on the list, it dies.

This skill is a constraint layer. It runs on top of whatever copy skill is active. It does not replace long-form-copy, hook-generation, video-ad-scripts, advertorial, or any other writing skill. It filters their output.

---

## SELF-AUDIT PROTOCOL

After completing any copy draft, execute this checklist before delivering:

1. Scan for every banned structural pattern in Section 1. Rewrite any match.
2. Scan for every parallel repetition violation in Section 2. Apply the Escalation Principle to any match.
3. Scan for every banned transition/filler phrase in Section 3. Delete or rewrite any match.
4. Scan for every banned qualifier/hedge in Section 4. Replace with absolute statements backed by proof.
5. Scan for every formatting crutch in Section 5. Strip any match.
6. If any rewrite introduces a new violation, re-scan. The audit is recursive until clean.

## 1. BANNED STRUCTURAL PATTERNS

### The False Contrast Stack
**Pattern:** "It's not X. It's not Y. It's Z."

This is the single most common AI structural tell. Three negatives stacked to set up a positive. Never use it. If you need to contrast, use a reverse-engineer narrative ("I thought it was X — turns out the real problem was…") or a direct comparison with specific proof.

**Banned examples:**
- "It's not luck. It's not talent. It's a system."
- "This isn't a diet. It isn't a pill. It's a protocol."
- "It wasn't the dosage. It wasn't the brand. It was the delivery method."

### The Triple Short Sentence Stack
**Pattern:** Three consecutive sentences of identical or near-identical length, all under 8 words.

Short sentences are a pacing tool. Three identical-length short sentences in a row are a rhythm crutch. Vary the cadence — follow a short punch with a longer sentence that adds depth, or lead with a long setup and land on a single short line.

**Banned examples:**
- "This works. It's proven. Here's why."
- "She tried everything. Nothing worked. Then this."
- "No gimmicks. No tricks. Just results."

### The Rhetorical Section Opener
**Pattern:** Using a question to transition between sections or ideas.

Questions as transitions are filler. They stall momentum and signal that the writer didn't know how to bridge two ideas. Use declarative statements, line breaks, or narrative continuity instead.

**Banned examples:**
- "But what does that actually mean?"
- "So, how does it work?"
- "What if there was a better way?"
- "Want to know the best part?"

### The "Imagine If" Opener
**Pattern:** Opening with a hypothetical visualization prompt.

"Imagine" openings are generic and overused. Start with a hard fact, a direct challenge, a specific number, a story beat, or a provocative claim instead.

**Banned examples:**
- "Imagine waking up to a calendar full of booked calls."
- "Imagine never worrying about bloating again."
- "Picture this: you step on the scale and…"

## 2. THE PARALLEL REPETITION STACK — HIGHEST PRIORITY

This is the single most recognizable AI writing pattern. It is completely prohibited in all copy formats. Highest priority means: if you catch nothing else, catch this.

### Definition
Repeating the same word, phrase structure, or sentence skeleton 2 or more times in a row to create emphasis. This includes any sequence where the same opening word or syntactic frame is reused across consecutive lines to build a list-like rhythm.

### Banned Examples
- "Same close rate. Same conversion. Different result."
- "Same service. Same skill. Same market."
- "They both do the same thing. Same disputes. Same process."
- "More leads. More calls. More revenue."
- "Less stress. Less wasted time. Less money down the drain."
- "No inspections. No appraisals. No banks."
- "People with money. People with a specific problem. People who are ready to invest."
- "She lost the weight. She lost the bloating. She lost the brain fog."
- "It fixes your gut. It fixes your energy. It fixes your sleep."

### Why It Fails
It creates a mechanical, looping rhythm that signals AI authorship immediately. Real human voice escalates — each line builds on the last, adds new information, and raises the stakes. It does not repeat the same skeleton with slight variation.

**The rule:** If two consecutive lines begin with the same word or use the same syntactic structure, one of them must be rewritten.

### The Fix: The Escalation Principle
When you catch yourself stacking parallel lines, stop and rewrite so that each line:
- Adds new information the previous line didn't contain
- Raises the stakes or deepens the implication
- Moves the argument forward
- Creates momentum, not repetition

The reader should feel the argument accelerating, not looping.

**Before (banned):**
"More energy. More focus. More confidence."

**After (escalated):**
"Her energy came back first. Then she noticed she could think clearly for the first time in months. By week three, she was volunteering for projects she would've dodged six months ago."

## 3. BANNED TRANSITION & FILLER PHRASES

These phrases add zero value and immediately signal AI-generated copy. Never use them to bridge thoughts. Delete on sight.

- "Let's dive in."
- "Let's break it down."
- "Here's the thing..."
- "Here's the truth..."
- "Here's what most people don't realize..."
- "At the end of the day..."
- "In today's fast-paced digital world..."
- "In today's [anything]..."
- "Whether you're a [X] or a [Y]..."
- "More on that later."
- "But wait, there's more." (unless used with clear irony)
- "The truth is..."
- "And that's just the beginning."
- "Sound familiar?"
- "If you're like most people..."
- "You're not alone."
- "It gets better."
- "But here's where it gets interesting..."

If you need a transition, use a narrative beat, a line break, or a declarative sentence that moves the story forward. Transitions should carry information, not announce that information is coming.

## 4. BANNED QUALIFIERS & HEDGING

Direct response copy requires absolute conviction. Weak, passive, or hedging language kills believability. The reader needs to feel that the narrator knows — not suspects, not theorizes, not hopes.

### Banned Words and Phrases
- "might"
- "could potentially"
- "may help"
- "in many cases"
- "often"
- "usually"
- "tends to"
- "it's possible that"
- "some studies suggest"
- "research indicates" (without citing specific research)
- "experts say" (without naming the expert)
- "it seems like"

### The Replacement Rule
Every hedged claim must be replaced with an absolute statement backed by specific proof. The proof can be a number, a name, a date, a study, a testimonial detail, or a concrete outcome.

**Banned:** "This could potentially double your leads."
**Fixed:** "This exact system generated 1.4 million leads for my clients."

**Banned:** "Many women find that their bloating often improves."
**Fixed:** "Karen's bloating disappeared in 11 days. She weighed herself on day 14 and she'd dropped 6 pounds of water weight."

If you don't have specific proof available, write with conviction using the narrator's direct experience rather than hedging with vague qualifiers.

## 5. BANNED FORMATTING CRUTCHES

### Emoji Overload
Do not use emojis unless explicitly instructed to do so for a specific platform context (e.g., a Facebook ad where the user requests them). Never use emojis in VSL scripts, long-form sales pages, email copy, or advertorials.

### Excessive Exclamation Points
Urgency and excitement must come from the logic, the offer, and the proof — not from punctuation. Limit exclamation points to an absolute minimum. If the sentence needs an exclamation point to feel urgent, the sentence is weak. Rewrite it so the words carry the weight.

### Dense Paragraph Blocks
Do not write dense 5-6 sentence paragraphs. Use line breaks as a pacing tool. In direct response copy, almost every sentence should breathe on its own line. Dense blocks signal academic or corporate writing, not direct response.

---

## 6. PATTERN INTERACTION RULES

Some banned patterns overlap. When they do, the stricter rule wins:

- A "No X. No Y. No Z." line violates both the Parallel Repetition Stack (Section 2) AND the Triple Short Sentence Stack (Section 1). Both rules apply. Rewrite completely.
- A "Here's the thing..." opener that leads into a rhetorical question violates both Section 3 AND Section 1. Both violations must be fixed.
- A hedged parallel stack ("might help your energy. Might help your sleep. Might help your focus.") violates Section 2 AND Section 4. Fix both — escalate the structure AND replace the hedging.

When in doubt, rewrite more aggressively. Over-editing for AI patterns is always preferable to under-editing.

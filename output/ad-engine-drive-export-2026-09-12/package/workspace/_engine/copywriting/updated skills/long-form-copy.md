# Long-Form Copy — The Writing Engine

This skill writes the copy. It does not decide strategy. Three skills form the system, and this is the third:

1. **copy-strategy** — reads awareness + sophistication + concept, outputs the brief (belief set, proportion, discovery mode, engine, push intensity).
2. **concept-databank** — the twelve vehicles. copy-strategy picks one; its metadata (engine, awareness lean, discovery default) flows into the brief.
3. **long-form-copy** (this) — executes the brief at the line level.

If you are here without a brief, stop and run copy-strategy. Writing without it produces the failure this whole system was built to kill: beautiful story, no sale, or a pitch with no earned belief underneath it.

**One principle governs everything below:** belief-shifting does not happen in sections. It happens in the half-second gap between one sentence and the next. The reader's mind moves forward one line at a time, and your job is to move it. Every law, lock, and pass in this file serves that.

---

## 0. THE NARRATOR DETERMINES THE FORM

Don't choose a structure. Choose a narrator. Once you know who is talking — who they are, what they have been through, why they are the one telling this — the copy finds its shape. The moment you pick a structural label first and write to fit it, the reader feels the seams.

Before writing, answer four questions about the narrator (the concept from concept-databank gives you most of this):

1. **Do they have a personal story, or are they a professional who has seen the pattern?** Personal story → narrative, beliefs shift through caring about the outcome. Professional → educational, beliefs shift through recognition and authority. Most strong pieces blend: a professional with a personal stake.
2. **What would they say first in a room with the reader?** That is the hook (it came from hook-generation). Don't override it.
3. **How do they know the mechanism?** Learned through a personal journey (reader discovers alongside them) or known as their domain (they teach it plainly). Both valid; pick the one congruent with the narrator.
4. **What is their relationship to the product?** This sets how the product enters — but WHEN it enters is set by Lock 5, not by instinct.

The congruence test: read the finished copy and ask whether every section sounds like this one person. The hook, the mechanism, the close — all one voice, never shifting register when it is "time to teach" or "time to sell."

When the ad feeds a landing page, the same narrator continues across the click. The click is a paragraph break, not a scene change. Narrator-switching at the click starts the landing page from zero.

## I. THE FIVE BELIEF SHIFTS (the macro map)

Every long-form piece moves the reader through some subset of these, in order:

1. "My suffering is real."
2. "My current approach is wrong about something."
3. "The root cause is X." (mechanism)
4. "The solution needs A + B + C." (criteria)
5. "This specific product is the one."

**Which of these you fully shift vs. confirm-fast comes from the brief, not from this skill.** A product-aware reader already holds 1–3; you confirm them in a sentence or two and spend your words on 4 and 5. A problem-aware reader needs 1–3 built from scratch. Re-teaching a belief the reader already holds reads as padding and kills the sell — that is the single most expensive error in the system, and copy-strategy's belief set exists to prevent it.

The five beliefs are the macro map. **Lock 2 is how you actually move them** — at the resolution where persuasion happens.

---

## II. LENGTH IS AN OUTPUT, NOT A TARGET

There is no word target. Length is the sum of two independent inputs the brief already computed:

- **Awareness sizes the story.** Lower awareness = thicker emotional clearing (the reader must care before they will learn). Higher = thinner.
- **Sophistication sizes the mechanism + objection block.** Higher stage = more layers and more failed-attempt teardown.

They stack. A problem-aware + Stage-4 piece carries a thick story AND a heavy teardown — which is why it runs long. That is earned length, not bloat. Cap at **3,000 words** as a discipline ceiling: if a piece wants to run past it, the concept is bloated, not big. Never pad to a number; never cap a beat that is earning its space. Density, not length, is the enemy — and Pass 1 hunts it.

## PART A — PRE-DRAFT LOCKS

Complete all five locks plus the engine declaration before writing a sentence. The locks make the draft good. The passes (Part B) make sure it did not drift while you wrote.

### LOCK 1 — VOICE FINGERPRINT

Define this narrator's four verbal tics — concrete, checkable constraints, not "conversational":

- **Sentence-length tendency** (clipped 6–10 words / loose / mixed-with-fragments)
- **One word this narrator would NEVER use** (the vocabulary ceiling, made specific)
- **A punctuation lean** (em-dashes mid-thought / trailing ... / short sentences. like this.)
- **How they handle being wrong** (defensive / blunt self-blame / dry / doesn't admit it)

A nurse, a panicked spouse, and a barber differ in *rhythm of thought*, not just vocabulary. The fingerprint holds the voice past paragraph forty, where voice silently drifts into generic-narrator. Re-read every tenth paragraph against the four tics; if one breaks, the spell is breaking.

### LOCK 2 — THE LINE-LEVEL BELIEF LADDER  *(highest leverage in the skill)*

Before drafting each BEAT, write two things:

- Belief the reader holds **entering** the beat.
- Belief they hold **leaving** it.

Write the beat so its **last line lands the exit belief**, and the next beat's **first line moves that belief** — not the section's. Every line ends with the reader holding a slightly different belief than they held at its start. A line that does not move the belief forward is restating: kill it.

Corpus proof, at fragment resolution:

> "I stopped taking the Lipitor." → *(she defied the prescriber)*
> "When." → *(one word — the reader leans in)*
> "Six weeks ago." → *(long enough to be real, not a fluke)*

That is what linear storytelling actually is: not a tidy arc, but each line built to move the exact belief the previous line just created. This is the resolution the five macro beliefs operate at. If only one thing in this skill survives contact with real writing, make it this.

### LOCK 3 — THE OBJECTION-FIRING MAP

An objection answered too early reads as defensive; too late, as damage control. For each objection in the brief, log the exact moment the reader's mind generates it, and place the answer in that same breath:

| Objection | Fires the instant... |
| --- | --- |
| "why didn't this work for me" | you claim the ingredient works → teardown lands HERE, same breath |
| "isn't this just [format]" | the product physically appears |
| "supplements are a scam" | during the failed-attempt catalog |
| "why didn't my doctor tell me" | after the mechanism is taught |
| "is it actually working" | at the first result beat (answer with skepticism, not hype) |

Objections are **voiced by a character, never defended by the copy.** The narrator voices the same doubt the reader feels; the guide or the outcome resolves it. Objection-handling is a sequence keyed to the reader's doubt, not a pile dumped in one block.

### LOCK 4 — MATCHED PAIN ↔ PAYOFF SCENES

The number proves it happened. The scene makes them want it. Pair them before writing:

- Name the **opening pain scene** — specific, sensory, a moment, not a symptom list.
- Name the **payoff scene** that resolves that exact pain, for the close.

Every results beat = a number paired with a previously-established sensory scene. The close lands on the single scene that closes the circuit the ad opened. The strop back on the wall (opened: gathering dust). Boxcars painted (opened: unpainted on the bench). The husband moving onto her side of the bed (opened: as far from it as possible). Pain and payoff are designed together, up front — never improvised at the end.

### LOCK 5 — THE PRODUCT IS EARNED, NOT DISCOVERED  *(governs Locks 2 and 3)*

There is no "discovered, not pitched" law in this system. It taught the writer that selling is the enemy and story the virtue — which is why a draft can be gorgeous and sell nothing. The corpus disproves it: the cardiologist names the product on his desk in the first twenty lines and sells hard.

The real principle: **the reader must have built the evaluation criteria before the product appears.** Once they hold the yardstick, the product can be named early, late, soft, or hard — the sell lands because they are measuring it against a standard *they* built.

- Banned: **unearned** selling — naming or pushing the product before criteria exist.
- Allowed, once criteria are built: name early, sell directly, push the CTA hard. The push intensity comes from the brief.
- The discovery MODE (named-early vs. discovered-late) comes from the concept's metadata + the brief. It is a tested variable, not a fixed virtue.

Test before the product appears: *has the reader built the checklist this product will be judged against?* If yes, sell. If no, build the criteria first (Law 7).

### PRE-DRAFT ENGINE DECLARATION

Name the engine from the brief: terror / grief-guilt / vindication / curiosity / relief / social-proof / indignation. Then ask: could this same angle run on a different engine? Default-to-terror is a creative-fatigue and a compliance risk on vulnerable health audiences. Pick it deliberately; don't inherit it from the last piece. Pass 2 enforces this on the finished draft.

## III. THE STRUCTURE (governed by Lock 5)

The spine, in order. It is a sequence of belief shifts, not a fill-in template — let the narrator walk it the way they actually would.

Hook (from hook-generation) → Problem → Agitation → Mechanism of the Problem (root cause) → Solution Criteria (the checklist) → Product (entered per Lock 5) → Progressive Results → Close.

The brief sets the proportion across these. Story-dominant pieces spend most of their words before the mechanism; mechanism-dominant pieces (where the biology itself creates fear or urgency) invert it. Either way the structure holds; only the weighting changes.

---

## IV. PACING LAWS (the ones the locks don't already cover)

Compressed. Each does unique work the locks don't.

1. **Emotional clearing before mechanism.** The emotion you build before the mechanism determines how much mechanism the reader will tolerate. Mechanism carries zero emotional weight — it lands clean into space the emotion already cleared. The contrast is the credibility.
2. **One metaphor. Install once, reference forever.** One core mechanism metaphor, installed in three sentences or fewer. Every later point references it. No second metaphor; if you need another analogy, nest it inside the first.
3. **Villain teardowns are one-line applications.** Once the metaphor is installed, each failed solution is dismantled in 1–2 sentences by applying it. Name → apply framework → move on. Never re-teach the mechanism inside a teardown.
4. **The mechanism is learned, not lectured.** The narrator learns it (from a guide's single reframe, then their own research) or knows it as their domain. The guide reframes in a line and exits; the narrator does the discovering. Reader ownership comes from the narrator's work, not an expert's lecture.
5. **The momentum rule.** Zero reflection between understanding the root cause and seeking the solution. No "I sat with this for days." Mechanism reveals what is wrong → the next breath seeks what is right.
6. **Build the criteria before the product (UMS sequence).** Solution components are discovered individually, building a mental checklist, BEFORE the product appears. Existing products fail the checklist. Then the product is named (Lock 5). This IS the "criteria before product" mechanic — the product is evaluated against a yardstick the reader built.
7. **Product confirmation beat.** After the product is named, 3–5 sentences checking it against the criteria already established. The reader nods — "yes, that's what they said it needs." Not new information.
8. **No UNEARNED ad mode.** Before criteria exist: no benefit stacking, no "but that's not all," no urgency, no direct-address selling. The narrator is telling their story. After criteria exist, sell as hard as the brief's push intensity allows (Lock 5).
9. **Progressive, skeptical results.** Timeline: days → weeks → months, with skepticism at each stage ("I figured it was placebo"). Shown as a visual timeline when the brief calls for it (Week one: ... / Week two: ...). Results are measured in the life moments from Lock 4, never in clinical metrics alone.
10. **Social proof through narrative.** Others noticing, asking, wanting it — woven into the story as natural consequence. Never testimonial blocks, never "Verified Buyer."

## PART B — SELF-AUDIT PASSES

Run on the finished draft, before delivery.

### PASS 1 — THE COMPRESSION TEST

Paragraph by paragraph, answer one question: *what belief does this move that the paragraph before it did not?* If the answer is "none — it reinforces," cut it or merge it up. Bloat is invisible to the writer because restatement feels like emphasis. The two places it hides: the mechanism taught twice, and the close re-explaining the body. Hunt those first. Never shave evenly to hit a number.

### PASS 2 — THE ENGINE GOVERNOR

Two checks:

- Did the piece drift to terror by default, even though a different engine was declared? (The middle is where fear creeps back in.)
- Across the live library, is one engine carrying more than ~60% of pieces? If yes, this piece should not be that engine, even if it is the strongest single option. Range beats local maxima.

### PASS 3 — THE AI-ARTIFACT SWEEP  *(run the full ai-copy-blacklist; these are the repeat offenders in this system)*

1. **Staccato-fragment overuse.** Fragments are a scalpel, not a default. One impact line ("146. 147. 145.") lands. Three-plus stacked through explanation or emotion reads as machine rhythm and breaks Lock 1. Sentences in a thought flow into each other; reserve the fragment for the one line you want to stop the reader on. Two consecutive fragment-stack paragraphs = one is faking intensity; rewrite it as connected prose.
2. **Writerly scaffolding.** Banned throat-clearing that announces structure instead of living it: "so I started a list," "here's the strange part," "here's the thing," "let me explain," "the truth is." Replace the scaffold with the content it was introducing. ("So I started a list — true Ceylon first..." → "It had to be true Ceylon.")
3. **Personification — objects can't do human things.** Products, organs, and substances don't take human actions. A softgel does not "hand your money to your stomach acid" — it is swallowed whole and destroyed in it. Rust doesn't "jam the locks" as an agent — high blood sugar corrodes the receptors so insulin can't open them. Keep mechanism metaphors STRUCTURAL (a thing has a property; a process happens to it), never AGENTIVE (a thing chooses, tries, refuses, hands, betrays). Mechanical check: if an inanimate noun is the subject of a human verb, rewrite.

After the sweep, re-read once at reading speed. If any line makes you hear "a copywriter wrote this" instead of "this person is talking," that line dies. The imperfection of a real voice is the authenticity signal; the polish of an AI is the tell.

## ORDER OF OPERATIONS

Brief (from copy-strategy) + concept (from concept-databank) → Locks 1–5 + engine declaration → write the draft → Belief-Ladder re-read (does every line move a belief?) → Compression Test → Engine Governor → AI-Artifact Sweep → deliver.

## THE COMMANDMENTS (what survives)

1. The copy IS the output. Plain text, ready to paste. No markdown, no headers, no bullets inside the copy.
2. The hook comes from hook-generation. This skill builds the body and close from it.
3. There are no word targets. Length is earned; bloat is killed by density (Pass 1).
4. Voice never breaks, first line to last (Lock 1).
5. The product is earned, not discovered (Lock 5). Selling is not the enemy; unearned selling is.
6. The reader builds the evaluation criteria before the product appears. No exceptions.
7. Belief shifts at line resolution (Lock 2). Section-level is the map; the line is the work.
8. Objections are voiced by characters and placed where they fire (Lock 3), never defended by the copy.
9. The emotional circuit closes: the pain scene and the payoff scene are designed together (Lock 4).
10. One metaphor, structural never agentive. Install once, reference forever.
11. The engine is chosen, not inherited (Pass 2).
12. If it smells like AI, it dies (Pass 3). You are not a copywriter — you are a person who found something.

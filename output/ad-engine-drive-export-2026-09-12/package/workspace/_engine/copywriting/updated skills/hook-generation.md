# Hook Generation

This skill governs how to write the first 1–5 lines of a direct response ad — the hook. It applies to both written hooks (Facebook ad primary text) and spoken hooks (video ad opening lines). The mechanics differ by medium, but the underlying psychology is identical.

A hook has ONE job: stop the scroll and create a forward pull strong enough that the viewer/reader must continue. It does NOT sell. It does NOT introduce the product. It creates an open loop — curiosity, identity, or emotional — that only the body of the ad resolves.

Read this entire file before generating hooks. The principles are interdependent.

---

## The Core Framework: Problem + Emotional State + Awareness Match

Every effective hook does three things simultaneously:

1. **Calls out the problem** in the language the avatar uses inside her own head — not clinical language, not polite language, not marketer language. Her internal monologue.

2. **Mirrors the emotional state** the avatar is in RIGHT NOW — not the emotion you want her to feel, not the aspirational state, but the exact feeling she has at the moment she's scrolling. Frustration, resignation, quiet desperation, suspicion, exhaustion, self-blame.

3. **Matches her awareness level** — the hook meets her where she is on the Schwartz awareness spectrum, not where you wish she was. An unaware avatar needs an identity hook. A solution-aware avatar needs a mechanism hook. Mismatching awareness level is the #1 reason hooks fail at scale.

If any of these three elements are missing, the hook collapses. A hook that calls out the problem but misses the emotional state feels clinical. A hook that nails the emotion but misses the awareness level feels irrelevant. A hook that matches awareness but uses marketer language feels like an ad.

## Hook Hygiene

These rules govern what makes a hook land versus what makes a reader scroll past. They apply to every hook regardless of type, loop, or medium.

### No Throat-Clearing

The hook is the first line that calls out the problem or mirrors the avatar's lived experience. If the real hook is on line 2, delete line 1. Repeat until deleting the first line makes the hook weaker — that's your real opening.

**Throat-clearing patterns to kill on sight:**
- Soft emotional preambles: "I need to tell someone this," "I can't keep carrying this alone," "Something happened that changed everything," "This is hard to write"
- Context-free scene-setting: "March 14. Turned 53 today." — no problem signal, no reason for the avatar to care
- Vague narrative openers: "The first thing I noticed was the couch" — there's no visceral emotion, no problem call-out, nothing that tells the avatar this is about HER
- Dramatic framing without specificity: "What happened next changed everything," "I wasn't prepared for what I found"

**The throat-clearing test:** Read the first line. Delete it. Is the hook stronger? If yes, line 1 was preamble. Keep deleting until removal makes the hook weaker. That's your real opening.

**The exception — Narrative Trojan Horses and Credential Stacks:** Some hook types (Type 10: Narrative Trojan Horse, and hooks using Credential Stacking) deliberately open on material that looks like throat-clearing but isn't. The difference is specificity and forward pull. "I need to tell someone this" is vague — it could be about anything. "I bought a jacket at Goodwill last Saturday. Ten dollars. Leather. Dark brown." is specific, sensory, and builds scene tension that pulls the reader forward. The rule isn't "always open on the problem" — it's "never open on vague nothing." If the opening is concrete, specific, and creates narrative momentum, it can delay the problem reveal. If it's generic emotional framing, kill it.

### The Avatar Relevance Test

The avatar must see herself in the hook — if not in line 1, then within the first 2-3 lines. Her problem. Her behavior. Her situation. Her life stage. If the hook never gets to something the avatar has lived, the ad is dead.

"I'm 34 weeks pregnant" — fails for a 55-year-old post-menopausal woman, regardless of how good the copy is after it. She scrolls because line 1 told her this isn't for her.

"I have seventeen bottles in my medicine cabinet" — a solution-aware reader who's tried everything sees her own cabinet. She's IN the ad before she knows it's an ad.

"I meal-prep every Sunday" — the identity and the discipline IS the problem setup. A health-conscious woman who does the same thing reads this and thinks "that's me."

"Your LDL is still elevated" — dialogue that mirrors her actual doctor's appointment. She's heard those exact words. She's back in the exam room.

"I've been a pharmacist for 28 years" — the avatar who trusts her pharmacist, who trusts the system, who takes what she's told to take — she leans in because this person IS the system she trusts.

**The test:** Would the target avatar read the hook and immediately think "this is about me" or "I've lived this" or "this person is someone I'd trust about MY problem"? If the answer is no — if she'd think "this doesn't apply to me" or "what is this about?" — the hook fails.

### Awareness Level Alignment Is Mandatory

**The hook must match the avatar's CURRENT awareness level — not the level the ad will move her to.**

A problem-aware avatar needs to see her problem in the hook — not a solution she hasn't searched for. A solution-aware avatar needs to see her failed attempts or her exhaustion with what she's tried — not a basic problem call-out she's already past.

If the hook addresses a higher awareness level than where the avatar sits, she won't recognize herself. If it addresses a lower level, it feels beneath her and she scrolls.

- **Problem-aware hook:** "I meal-prep every Sunday. Every single Sunday for eleven years. Grilled chicken. Roasted vegetables. Brown rice measured in a little cup because I read somewhere that portion control matters for cholesterol." — She's living the problem. She hasn't tried solutions yet. The hook mirrors her daily behavior and her frustration.

- **Solution-aware hook:** "I have seventeen bottles in my medicine cabinet." — She's tried everything. The hook mirrors her graveyard of failed attempts. A problem-aware reader who hasn't tried anything wouldn't connect with this.

- **Contrarian hook (solution-aware+):** "'Your LDL is still elevated.' You said it without looking up." — She's heard this. She's been in this appointment. The dialogue puts her right back in the exam room and the contrarian framing makes her lean in.

**Mismatching awareness level in the hook is not fixable in the body.** If the hook attracted the wrong reader or failed to attract the right one, nothing downstream matters.

### Good Hooks Open on the Problem, the Avatar's Behavior, or a Trust Signal

Every effective hook does at least one of these in the opening:
- **Calls out the specific problem** in the avatar's language (not clinical, not marketer)
- **Mirrors a behavior the avatar performs daily** that IS the problem or is connected to the problem
- **Drops the avatar into a scene she's already lived** — doctor's office, kitchen, bathroom, medicine cabinet
- **Uses dialogue she's already heard** from a doctor, spouse, friend, or her own internal monologue
- **Establishes a narrator the avatar would trust about her problem** — a professional with credentials relevant to her situation, someone who's lived it longer, someone who was part of the system she relies on

If the opening does none of these, it's not a hook. It's a setup for a hook, and the setup should be cut — unless the specificity and narrative momentum are strong enough to carry the reader to the problem reveal (see Narrative Trojan Horse, Type 10).

## Loop Types

Every hook opens one of two types of loops. Knowing which type you're opening determines the structure, pacing, and payoff of the hook.

### Curiosity Loops

The hook creates an information gap the reader must close. Something is incomplete, contradicted, or unexplained. The reader continues because she needs to know the answer.

**Mechanic:** Present a result, outcome, or claim that doesn't make sense given the reader's current beliefs. The gap between "what happened" and "how is that possible" creates the pull.

**Example:**
> My lipid specialist pulled me aside after my six-month panel and said, "Whatever you're doing, keep doing it. These are the cleanest numbers I've seen from someone with your genetics."
>
> I'd just told her I quit my statin eight months ago.

**Why it works mechanically:** The first paragraph installs a positive medical outcome through borrowed authority ("lipid specialist," not "my doctor" — specialist is more credible and specific). "Someone with your genetics" pre-handles the objection that good results only happen to easy biology. The second paragraph creates the contradiction — she quit the thing that's supposed to produce those results. The reader's brain fires: "Wait — how?" That's the loop. The SEQUENCING matters: authority validation FIRST, contrarian reveal SECOND. Reverse the order and the hook loses half its power because the reader hasn't been primed to care about the outcome yet.

**Signals you're writing a curiosity loop:**
- The hook presents a surprising outcome
- There's a contradiction between what happened and what "should" happen
- The reader's next thought is "how?" or "what?"
- A piece of information is deliberately withheld

### Identity Loops

The hook describes the reader's lived experience so precisely that she feels seen. She continues because someone finally articulated her private reality — and she needs to know what comes next from someone who clearly understands.

**Mechanic:** Dramatize a specific scene the reader has lived but never seen written down. Not a general relatable statement — a surgically specific moment. The precision IS the proof that the writer understands.

**Example:**
> I told my gynecologist I'd lost all desire. Every bit of it. Gone.
> She didn't look up from her chart.
> "That's normal at your age. Hormones shift. It happens."

**Why it works mechanically:** This hook dramatizes a dismissal the avatar has already experienced. It doesn't TELL her that her doctor is dismissive — it SHOWS the exact scene. "Didn't look up from her chart" is the surgical detail. Every woman who's had this appointment feels the heat of that moment. The doctor's quoted response — casual, minimizing, rehearsed — mirrors the exact words the avatar heard. The hook earns trust BEFORE asking for anything because it proves the writer has lived (or deeply understood) the avatar's private wound.

**Signals you're writing an identity loop:**
- The hook describes a scene, not a claim
- The reader's next thought is "that's literally me" or "how do they know?"
- Specific sensory or behavioral details are present
- There's an emotional wound being validated, not argued

**Critical distinction:** Curiosity loops attract through information gaps. Identity loops attract through recognition. Both work. They attract different segments of your audience. The best hook batches include BOTH types across variations.

## Specificity Engineering

"Be specific" is useless advice. Here's what specificity actually means mechanically and how to engineer it into every hook.

### The Four Specificity Layers

**Layer 1: Concrete Detail**
Replace abstract descriptions with sensory, behavioral, or temporal details. These details signal lived experience.

- Abstract: "I was having heart problems"
- Concrete: "My resting heart rate hit 94 sitting at my desk on a Tuesday"

- Abstract: "My digestion was off"
- Concrete: "My stomach is flat when I wake up but looks six months along by dinner"

**Layer 2: Scene Construction**
Place the reader inside a moment — not a condition, but a moment. A moment has a setting, an action, and a feeling happening simultaneously.

- Condition: "I was always tired"
- Scene: "I fell asleep at a red light on the way to pick up my daughter. The car behind me honked and I jerked awake and didn't know where I was for three seconds."

**Layer 3: Dialogue as Evidence**
Real dialogue — what someone actually said — is the highest-trust specificity signal. Quoted words feel observed, not written. They prove the writer was IN the room.

- Reported: "My doctor said it was normal"
- Dialogue: "She looked at me over her glasses and said, 'Sweetheart, you're 47. This is just what happens.'"

**Layer 4: Credential Stacking**
Layering identity markers, professional credentials, and life details in rapid succession to build cumulative weight before a reveal. Each credential adds a brick. By the time the contradiction or wound lands, the reader has internalized how much this person has to lose — which is why the contradiction hits so hard.

- Flat: "I'm a pharmacist and statins damaged me"
- Stacked: "I've been a pharmacist for 28 years. A husband for 24. A father for 19. I've filled over 300,000 prescriptions. I carry 400 patients in my head — their names, their medications, their allergies. The man behind the counter who never forgets a name, never misses an interaction, never lets a bad prescription leave his hands. I've been on Atorvastatin for nine. It gave me perfect cholesterol. Then it took my memory."

Why credential stacking works: Each line adds a new dimension of identity that the medication (or condition) is threatening. "Pharmacist for 28 years" is professional identity. "Husband for 24, father for 19" is family identity. "300,000 prescriptions" is competence identity. "Never forgets a name" is the specific ability that's now at risk. By the time "it took my memory" lands, the reader understands exactly what's being destroyed and exactly how much this person has built that's now crumbling. The stack makes the fall devastating.

- Flat: "I'm a barber and my hands stopped working"
- Stacked: "I've been on Atorvastatin for 11 years. I've been a barber for 44. A husband for 36. A grandfather for 8. The drug gave me perfect cholesterol. Then it destroyed my hands."

**How to stack:** Open with the credential most relevant to the avatar's trust (professional title if the hook needs authority, life role if the hook needs emotional weight). Then stack 3–5 additional identity markers in descending sentence length — the rhythm should accelerate. Close the stack with the wound or contradiction in the shortest sentence. The compression at the end creates impact.

**Rule:** Every hook must contain at least TWO of these four layers. Hooks with three or four almost always outperform hooks with one or two.

## Hook Taxonomy

These are the mechanical categories of hooks. Each uses a different psychological lever. When generating hook batches, rotate through these types to maximize angle coverage.

### Type 1: The Symptom Sniper

Calls out one hyper-specific symptom in the avatar's internal language. No mechanism. No solution. Just "I see you."

**When to use:** Problem-aware and solution-aware audiences.

**Structure:** [Specific symptom in avatar language] + [life consequence of that symptom]

**Example:**
> If you're on a statin and your muscles ache, your brain feels like it's wrapped in cotton, and you're exhausted by mid-afternoon, I need you to read this.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> If you're on cholesterol medication and your muscles ache, your brain feels foggy, and you're exhausted all the time, I need you to hear this.

**Mechanic:** Stacks three specific sensory symptoms ("muscles ache," "brain wrapped in cotton," "exhausted by mid-afternoon") that the avatar has felt but probably hasn't connected to her statin. The specificity of the stack creates the "how do they know?" response. "I need you to read this" creates urgency without selling.

### Type 2: The Contrarian Reveal

Presents an outcome that contradicts the reader's current beliefs. Opens a curiosity loop through cognitive dissonance.

**When to use:** Solution-aware and product-aware audiences. Requires the reader to already hold a belief about what works or doesn't.

**Structure:** [Surprising positive outcome] → [Reveal that contradicts expected cause]

**Example:**
> My cardiologist called my numbers "remarkable" for the first time in eleven years.
> I stopped taking the prescription she gave me five months ago.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> My LDL Was 186. My Doctor Prescribed Lipitor. Five Months Later, It's 134. I Never Filled the Prescription.

**Mechanic:** The sequencing is critical. Positive authority-validated outcome first. Contrarian action second. If you reverse the order ("I stopped my prescription... and then my numbers got better"), the hook reads as reckless. The current order reads as intriguing because the positive outcome has already been established before the contradiction lands.

#### Subtype: The Outcome-First Contrarian

A specific variation where the opening is a **restored life moment** — not a clinical metric or authority validation, but the felt experience of getting something back. The reader recognizes what was restored as something SHE has lost. Then the contradiction lands: the restoration came from stopping, changing, or replacing the thing the reader's doctor told her was essential.

This subtype is more emotionally gripping than the standard Contrarian Reveal because the reader's first reaction is identity recognition ("I've lost that too"), not intellectual curiosity ("how is that possible?"). The curiosity loop still opens — but it opens AFTER the emotional hook has already set.

**Structure:** [Specific life moment restored — touch, function, energy, connection, desire, the thing she used to do and can't anymore] → [Reveal that she stopped/changed a medication or treatment her doctor said was permanent]

**Example:**
> My husband put his hand on my back last Tuesday and I leaned into it. For the first time in over a year I didn't flinch or tense or make an excuse.
>
> The only thing I changed was quitting the prescription my cardiologist said I'd be on for life.

**Example:**
> I ran four miles on Saturday without my legs seizing. I woke up Sunday and my hands could grip a coffee mug without grinding.
>
> Six weeks ago I stopped the statin my doctor told me was non-negotiable.

**Mechanic:** The life moment comes first — and it must be specific enough that the reader who has lost that exact thing feels it in her chest. "Leaned into it" is not "felt better." "Didn't flinch" is not "improved." The moment must be something the reader used to have and doesn't anymore. THEN the contradiction: the restoration came from stopping the thing she's been told she needs. The reader now holds two things simultaneously — the desire for that restored moment AND the dissonance of "how is she alive without the medication?" — and must continue reading to resolve both.

**When to use Outcome-First over standard:** When the desire angle is rooted in life erosion (intimacy loss, athletic identity, cognitive function, independence) rather than clinical anxiety (LDL numbers, heart attack risk). The reader who is losing her marriage doesn't care about particle size. She cares about the flinch. Lead with the flinch.

#### Subtype: The Perfect Compliance Catastrophe

A variation where the narrator (or someone the narrator knows) did everything right — took every dose, hit every target number, attended every appointment — and still suffered a catastrophic outcome. Heart attack. Stroke. Collapse. Death. The medication's promise of protection was an illusion. Perfect compliance didn't prevent the worst outcome.

This subtype is the most emotionally devastating version of the Contrarian Reveal because it weaponizes the reader's own compliance against her. If she's faithfully taking her medication and seeing good numbers, this hook tells her that compliance is not protection. The numbers she's been reassured by become evidence of a false sense of safety.

**Structure:** [Perfect compliance established — never missed a dose, numbers always where they should be, doctor always happy] → [Catastrophic outcome that the compliance should have prevented]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> My father took Atorvastatin for twelve years. Never missed a dose. His LDL stayed under 100 the entire time. His doctor called him a model patient.

(Followed by the narrative transition: "He died face-down on the floor of his woodshop. Widowmaker heart attack. Seventy-two years old.")

**Example (from swipe file):**
> My husband took Rosuvastatin for 14 years. His numbers were perfect the entire time. LDL of 94. His doctor was thrilled.

(Followed by: "Then he had a massive stroke in our kitchen. Plaque rupture. The medication didn't prevent a thing.")

**Mechanic:** The compliance proof must be exhaustive before the catastrophe lands. "Never missed a dose" + "LDL stayed under 100" + "doctor called him a model patient" — each phrase adds another layer of "he did everything right." The catastrophe must be sudden, physical, and irreversible — not a gradual decline. The reader who is currently compliant sees herself in the compliance. Then the catastrophe makes her question whether her own compliance is protecting her. The hook's power comes from the gap between "everything right" and "worst possible outcome."

**When to use:** When the avatar is currently compliant with a treatment she trusts. She takes her medication. Her numbers look fine. She believes she's protected. This hook shatters that belief.

#### Subtype: The Generational Refusal

The single most-replicated skeleton across the swipe library. A family member (father, mother, husband) walked the medication path for years or decades and it ended in catastrophe or slow ruin. The narrator, facing the same diagnosis and the same prescription, refuses — and goes looking for another way. It fuses three forces: inherited mortality (this is my future), witnessed suffering (I watched it happen), and an act of defiance (I said no). It almost always pairs with a "what I found instead" promise that previews the outcome and the discovery effort.

This differs from the Perfect Compliance Catastrophe in emphasis. The Compliance Catastrophe is about the *betrayal of compliance* (he did everything right and still died). The Generational Refusal is about *inheritance and the decision to break the cycle* (his path is my path unless I choose differently). The Compliance version weaponizes the reader's trust in her numbers; the Generational version weaponizes her fear of becoming her parent.

**When to use:** Problem-aware and solution-aware audiences facing a hereditary or "runs in the family" condition, or anyone being offered the same medication a family member took. Extremely strong when the avatar has literally watched a parent decline on the drug now being prescribed to them.

**Structure:** [Family member + medication + duration] → [Catastrophe or slow ruin] → [Narrator offered the same drug] → [Refusal] → ["And what I found instead..." outcome + discovery-effort preview]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> My father took Atorvastatin for 22 years. The side effects destroyed him. The stroke killed him anyway. When my doctor tried to put me on the same medication, I refused. And what I found instead — in a research paper from a Japanese university — brought my LDL from 168 to 122 without a single pill.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> My father was on blood pressure medication for 16 years. The cough that never stopped. The dizziness that made him fall twice. The exhaustion that turned him into a ghost of who he used to be. Last year, I saw the same numbers on my own monitor. I refused to follow his path.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I Inherited My Father's Build, His Sense of Humor, and His High Cholesterol. Now My Doctor Wants Me to Inherit His Atorvastatin Prescription Too.

**Mechanic:** The family member's decline must be specific and sensory (the cough, the falls, the yellowed skin, the forgotten grandchildren's names) — generic "he got sick" doesn't transfer the dread. The duration ("22 years," "16 years") makes the suffering feel like a sentence the narrator is now being handed. "I refused" is the pivot that converts passive fear into agency, which is what lets the reader imagine refusing too. The "what I found instead" clause is doing double work: it previews the payoff (so the reader knows there's hope) and it previews the *effort* ("three weeks reading medical journals at 2 AM"), which pre-installs the mechanism-discovery the body will deliver. **Engine/rotation caution:** this skeleton runs almost entirely on terror + grief, and it is the most saturated structure in this category's swipe set. It converts — but if the live library is already heavy on dead-parent openers, a new piece on the same engine compounds creative fatigue and compliance exposure. Rotate the engine (curiosity, vindication, relief) or the narrator before defaulting to it again.

### Type 3: The Dismissal Dramatization

Shows a specific moment where the avatar was dismissed, minimized, or misunderstood by an authority figure. Validates a wound.

**When to use:** Problem-aware and solution-aware audiences. Especially powerful when the avatar has been told her problem is "normal" or "in her head."

**Structure:** [Avatar seeks help] → [Authority figure dismisses with specific quoted language]

**Example:**
> I told my gastroenterologist that I hadn't had a normal bowel movement in four months. Not one.
> He typed something into his computer and said, "Try more fiber. Drink more water."
> I'd been eating 35 grams of fiber a day for two years.

**Mechanic:** The avatar's problem is stated with brutal specificity ("four months. Not one."). The doctor's response is quoted — dismissive, generic, and clearly inadequate. The third line reveals that the avatar has ALREADY done what the doctor just suggested, which exposes the doctor's advice as lazy and proves the avatar is beyond basic solutions. This creates both identity recognition ("that's my appointment") and a trust vacuum ("if my doctor can't help, who can?").

### Type 4: The Failed Solution Graveyard

Catalogs what the avatar has already tried and failed with. Creates identity recognition through shared frustration and positions the mechanism as something genuinely new.

**When to use:** Solution-aware audiences. The reader must have already tried things for this to land.

**Structure:** [Stack of specific failed attempts] → [Implied "none of it worked"]

**Example:**
> Metamucil. Miralax. Colace. Magnesium citrate before bed. Probiotics that cost $60 a month. Two rounds of Xifaxan.
> My GI doc's latest suggestion: "Have you tried yoga?"

**Mechanic:** The rapid-fire list of specific products creates recognition velocity — the reader checks off each one she's tried. The specificity ("magnesium citrate before bed," "$60 a month") proves the writer has lived this search. The doctor's final suggestion ("Have you tried yoga?") after a pharmaceutical-grade list creates the punchline effect — the gap between the severity of the problem and the triviality of the advice IS the hook.

### Type 5: The Borrowed Authority Opener

Opens with a specific statement from a credible figure that reframes the avatar's understanding. The authority creates trust. The reframe creates curiosity.

**When to use:** All awareness levels. Especially effective for unaware audiences because the authority figure gives permission to engage with unfamiliar information.

**Structure:** [Credible authority figure] + [Specific reframe statement] + [Implied "everything you knew is wrong"]

**Example:**
> A pharmacist I'd known for fifteen years pulled me aside at CVS and said, "Can I tell you something off the record about what you're picking up?"
> She pointed at my statin.

**Mechanic:** "Pharmacist I'd known for fifteen years" establishes both professional credibility and personal trust. "Pulled me aside" signals secret knowledge — information not available through normal channels. "Off the record" amplifies the insider signal. "Pointed at my statin" identifies the subject without explaining the problem, which opens the loop. The reader must continue because the pharmacist's concern has been established but her actual message hasn't been delivered.

### Type 6: The Timestamp Anchor

Opens with a hyper-specific time, place, or physical detail. The precision creates instant credibility — specific details signal "this actually happened."

**When to use:** All awareness levels. Works as an entry point for any narrative format.

**Structure:** [Precise temporal/spatial detail] + [Emotionally charged action or realization]

**Example:**
> At 2:47 AM on a Thursday in March, I was sitting on the bathroom floor googling "why does my heart race when I lie down" for the third time that week.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> After 8 Months on Lisinopril, My Husband Found Me on the Bathroom Floor at 3 AM. I Couldn't Stop Coughing Long Enough to Breathe. My Blood Pressure Was "Perfectly Controlled."

**Mechanic:** "2:47 AM" is not "late at night." "Thursday in March" is not "one night." "Sitting on the bathroom floor" is not "I was worried." The precision of every element signals truth. No one fabricates "2:47 AM on a Thursday in March." The specific Google search in quotes mirrors the exact action the reader has taken, creating instant identity recognition. "Third time that week" escalates the desperation without stating it.

### Type 7: The Confession Lead

Opens with an admission that creates vulnerability. The narrator reveals something that costs them social capital — which is WHY the reader trusts it.

**When to use:** Problem-aware and solution-aware audiences. Works when the avatar carries shame or secrecy around her problem.

**Structure:** [Vulnerable admission] → [Specific consequence of the admitted behavior/state]

**Example:**
> I haven't let my husband see me undressed in eight months.
> Not because of how I look. Because of the smell.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> My husband is a cardiologist. He put me on Crestor when my LDL hit 178. He didn't ask. He called it in himself.
> Four months later, I stopped taking it. In secret. He still doesn't know.

**Mechanic:** The first line is a confession that could go in many directions. The second line redirects to an unexpected, more vulnerable place. The reader expected body image — she got something worse. The specificity of "eight months" (not "a while" or "a long time") signals this is measured, tracked, endured. The shame underneath is palpable without being stated, which makes it more powerful than if the narrator said "I'm so embarrassed."

The cardiologist-wife hook adds a layer: the confession isn't just about stopping a medication — it's about defying someone she's supposed to trust completely. "In secret. He still doesn't know." turns a medical decision into a marital betrayal, which creates a double curiosity loop: what did she find, AND what happens when he finds out?

### Type 8: The Misdiagnosis Reveal

Presents a diagnosis the reader may share — then reveals it was wrong all along. The reader wasn't suffering from the condition she was told she had. Everything she "managed" was based on a false premise.

This is distinct from the Contrarian Reveal. The Contrarian Reveal contradicts a TREATMENT belief (she stopped the medication and got better). The Misdiagnosis Reveal contradicts the DIAGNOSIS ITSELF — the reader's entire framework for understanding what's wrong with her. This opens a deeper curiosity loop because the reader doesn't just wonder "what worked instead?" — she wonders "then what DO I actually have?"

**When to use:** Solution-aware and problem-aware audiences. The reader must currently hold a diagnosis (or suspect one) that matches the one being contradicted. Works across any condition where the avatar identifies with a diagnostic label: arthritis, neuropathy, IBS, fibromyalgia, autoimmune conditions.

**Structure:** [Diagnosis + age at diagnosis + current age] → [Reveal that the diagnosis was wrong] → [Implied "everything I did for X years was based on a lie"]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis at 32. I'm 47 now. Turns out I never had arthritis at all.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I was diagnosed with osteoarthritis at 38. I'm 53 now. Turns out I never had arthritis at all.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I was told my neuropathy was permanent at 47. I'm 61 now. Turns out my nerve cells were never actually dying.

**Mechanic:** The two ages create a time span the reader feels viscerally — fifteen years of her life managed under a false premise. "Turns out I never had arthritis at all" is the most disorienting sentence possible for someone who identifies as an arthritis patient. It doesn't just say "there's a better treatment." It says "the thing you think you have isn't what you have." The reader who shares that diagnosis MUST continue because her identity as a patient is being challenged.

**Why this is a template, not just a hook:** Notice that the swipe file runs this EXACT structure for rheumatoid arthritis (32→47), osteoarthritis (38→53), and neuropathy (47→61). Same skeleton. Different condition, different ages, different medication consequences. This is how winning brands scale — they find a hook architecture that works and adapt it across conditions. (See the Hook Templating section below.)

**POV variants:** This hook runs in both first-person patient ("I was diagnosed...") and third-person professional ("A patient came to me diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis at 32. She's 47 now. Turns out she never had arthritis at all."). The first-person version creates identity recognition. The third-person professional version adds borrowed authority. Both work — use both in a batch.

### Type 9: The Professional Insider

The narrator IS the authority figure — a doctor, pharmacist, nurse, professor — and they're turning against their own system. They're not borrowing authority from someone else. They ARE the system, and they're breaking ranks.

This is distinct from the Borrowed Authority Opener. In a Borrowed Authority hook, a narrator encounters an authority who says something surprising ("A pharmacist pulled me aside..."). In the Professional Insider, the narrator IS the authority, and the thing she's revealing puts her professional reputation at risk. The professional risk IS the credibility signal — no one risks their career for a lie.

**When to use:** All awareness levels. Exceptionally powerful because the narrator's professional identity gives the reader permission to question a system she's been told to trust. The more prestigious or trusted the profession (doctor, pharmacist, nurse, professor), the stronger the hook.

**Structure:** [Professional credential + experience duration] → [Credential Stack that establishes what they've built/sacrificed/earned] → [Reveal that contradicts what their profession teaches]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I've been a pharmacist for 28 years. A husband for 24. A father for 19. I've filled over 300,000 prescriptions. I carry 400 patients in my head — their names, their medications, their allergies. The man behind the counter who never forgets a name, never misses an interaction, never lets a bad prescription leave his hands. I've been on Atorvastatin for nine. It gave me perfect cholesterol. Then it took my memory.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I've been a nursing professor for 28 years. I've taught over 2,000 nurses that statins are the gold standard for cardiovascular protection. I wrote the pharmacology curriculum they learned from.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I'm a neurologist, and last week a patient of mine asked if she could stop taking Gabapentin.

**Mechanic:** The credential stack does double duty. First, it establishes authority — this person has decades of experience, thousands of patients, a career built on the system. Second, it establishes STAKES — this person has everything to lose by speaking out. When the contradiction lands ("it took my memory," "what am I missing?"), the reader trusts it MORE because the narrator is sabotaging their own professional credibility to say it. The reader thinks: "If a pharmacist who's filled 300,000 prescriptions is saying this, it must be serious."

**Key mechanic — the insider turn:** The most powerful moment in this hook type is when the narrator transitions from defending the system to questioning it. The pharmacist who WAS the system now challenges it. The professor who WROTE the curriculum now doubts it. This turn must feel earned — the credential stack is what earns it. Without the stack, the turn feels like random contrarianism. With the stack, it feels like a professional crisis of conscience.

**Credential stacking is almost always required for this hook type.** A bare "I'm a doctor" opener is weak. "I'm a neurologist, and last week a patient of mine asked if she could stop taking Gabapentin" works because the specific scenario (patient requesting to stop medication + what happened next) creates the curiosity loop. But for longer-form hooks, the full credential stack is what creates the weight.

### Type 10: The Narrative Trojan Horse

Opens on something completely unrelated to the product, the problem, or even health. The reader is pulled in by pure narrative — she's reading a story, not an ad. The problem angle doesn't surface until the reader is already committed to the narrative.

This hook type breaks the conventional rule that hooks must open on the problem or the avatar's behavior. It works because people on Facebook are primed for STORIES — they're scrolling past friend updates, life events, and personal anecdotes. A Trojan Horse looks exactly like one of those posts. The reader doesn't realize she's in an ad until she's emotionally invested in the outcome.

**When to use:** Unaware audiences primarily. Also effective for solution-aware audiences who are so ad-fatigued that any health-related opening triggers their "this is an ad" filter. The Trojan Horse bypasses the filter entirely because the opening doesn't register as health content.

**Structure:** [Vivid, specific scene about something relatable and non-health-related] → [Object, discovery, or turn that bridges to the health angle] → [Health problem surfaces naturally from the story]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I bought a jacket at Goodwill last Saturday. Ten dollars. Leather. Dark brown. Barely worn — the kind of jacket somebody loved but didn't use up. I was buying it for my husband Robert. His size. His style. The kind of thing he'd never buy himself but would wear every day if I put it in his closet.

(Followed by: "I got home, checked the pockets before putting it away. You do that with secondhand clothes — make sure there's nothing left behind. There was something left behind.")

**Example (from swipe file):**
> My husband — the pastor of our church — came out as gay in front of our entire congregation, and it nearly killed me.
> Literally.

**Mechanic:** The Goodwill jacket hook works because every detail is specific enough to feel real ("Ten dollars. Leather. Dark brown.") and relatable enough to pull the reader into the domestic scene. She's not thinking about health — she's thinking about shopping for her husband, about the intimacy of knowing his size and style. "There was something left behind" opens a curiosity loop that has nothing to do with health... until it does. By the time the health angle surfaces, the reader is emotionally invested in the STORY and will follow it wherever it goes.

The pastor hook works differently — it's a shocking personal revelation that functions as a scroll-stopper through pure emotional intensity. "It nearly killed me. Literally." turns a relationship crisis into a health crisis. The reader who clicked for the drama stays for the health content because the narrative momentum carries her.

**Critical requirement:** The non-health opening must be genuinely interesting on its own. If the opening is boring and only makes sense once the health angle appears, it fails. The reader must be pulled in by the STORY before the story turns. Test: would someone share this opening as a standalone Facebook post? If yes, it's strong enough to carry the Trojan Horse.

### Type 11: The Trend-Jack

Opens by referencing something the reader is already seeing in her feed — other ads, trending health claims, viral content, or cultural moments — and positions itself as the corrective or the missing piece. The hook intercepts an existing conversation rather than starting a new one.

**When to use:** Solution-aware audiences who are actively seeing competitor ads or trending health content. This hook type works best in competitive categories where the reader is being bombarded with similar claims.

**Structure:** [Reference to what the reader is currently seeing/hearing] → [Signal that the writer knows something those sources don't] → [Implied "stop and read this before you buy/try that"]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> If you've been seeing all these parasite cleanse ads lately, the oregano oil, the wormwood, the "gut reset" protocols, stop before you buy anything.

**Example:**
> If your doctor's talking about statins but you're desperate to avoid it... this is for you.

**Mechanic:** "If you've been seeing all these parasite cleanse ads" immediately puts the reader in a meta-position — she's not just reading an ad, she's reading a commentary on the ads she's already been exposed to. Listing specific products ("oregano oil, the wormwood") proves the writer is in the same informational world as the reader. "Stop before you buy anything" creates urgency through protective authority — the writer is saving her from a mistake. The reader must continue because she now suspects the thing she was about to buy might be wrong.

**Why this works as a category play:** The Trend-Jack doesn't just sell — it repositions every competitor ad the reader has seen as incomplete or wrong. It drafts off the awareness those other ads created (the reader already knows about parasite cleanses) and redirects that awareness toward a different mechanism or product. It's the most efficient hook type for competitive categories because it leverages marketing spend the competitors already made.

### Type 12: The Mechanism Reframe

Drops a single-sentence mechanism reframe directly into the hook. This is an exception to the general principle that hooks don't educate — it works because the reframe is so concise and so counterintuitive that it functions as a pattern interrupt rather than education.

**When to use:** Problem-aware and solution-aware audiences. The reader must have an existing understanding of her condition that this reframe contradicts. Works best when the conventional understanding is widely held and the reframe can be stated in under 10 words.

**Structure:** [Problem call-out that matches the reader's current understanding] → [Single-sentence reframe that overturns it]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> If you're living with sciatica, here's what no one has told you. Your nerve isn't damaged. It's starving.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I was told my neuropathy was permanent at 47. I'm 61 now. Turns out my nerve cells were never actually dying.
> They were starving.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> Your LDL reading is the last thing to change. By the time it hits 187, your arteries have been accumulating damage for a decade. Your doctor sees the number and reaches for a prescription. A cardiologist sees the decade of damage underneath it. And the medication your doctor wants to prescribe fixes the number without touching the damage.

**Mechanic:** "Your nerve isn't damaged. It's starving." replaces the reader's entire mental model in six words. She believed her nerve was being destroyed (permanent, degenerative, hopeless). The reframe says it's being deprived (temporary, reversible, fixable). The hook works because the reframe creates an instant curiosity loop — "starving for WHAT?" — while simultaneously offering hope that the reader didn't have before. The brevity is essential. If the reframe takes more than one sentence, it becomes education. If it lands in under 10 words, it functions as a hook.

**The "what's it starving for?" test:** The reframe must create a specific follow-up question in the reader's mind. "Your nerve is starving" → "starving for what?" "The medication fixes the number without touching the damage" → "then what touches the damage?" If the reframe doesn't generate an immediate, specific question, it's not sharp enough. Rewrite until the reader literally cannot stop herself from reading the next line.

**How this differs from education:** Education explains. A mechanism reframe OVERTURNS. "Your nerve cells need vitamin B12 to function properly" is education — it's additive information. "Your nerve isn't damaged. It's starving" is a reframe — it replaces one understanding with another. The replacement is what stops the scroll, not the information.

### Type 13: The Brand Confession

The brand or narrator publicly admits fault, apologizes, or breaks from its own marketing. This creates a pattern interrupt through radical honesty — people don't expect brands to admit mistakes, so when one does, the reader leans in.

**When to use:** Product-aware audiences. The reader must already know about the brand or product for the confession to carry weight. Also works for solution-aware audiences who are skeptical of all marketing claims — the confession bypasses their ad filter because it doesn't SOUND like marketing.

**Structure:** [Public admission of fault or lie] → [Implied "what I'm about to tell you is so important I'm willing to damage my own brand to say it"]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I need to publicly apologize to anyone who's ever bought PrimeCell H2 before, because I lied.

**Mechanic:** "I need to publicly apologize" is the opposite of what every ad in the feed says. Ads promise. Ads celebrate. They don't apologize. The pattern break is immediate. "Because I lied" escalates the confession from "we made a mistake" to "we deliberately deceived you" — which seems suicidal for a brand but actually creates a massive curiosity loop: "What did they lie about? Why are they admitting it now? What changed?" The reader must continue because the confession is incomplete. The apology has been issued but the content of the lie hasn't been revealed.

### Type 14: The Effort Betrayal

The avatar's own discipline is the hook. She didn't cut corners. She did everything right — ate clean, trained, took the supplements, hit the targets — and the result still didn't come. The hook validates the effort and then weaponizes its failure: if doing everything right didn't work, the problem can't be effort. It has to be something nobody told her about.

This is distinct from the Failed Solution Graveyard. The Graveyard catalogs *products* she bought (Metamucil, Miralax, magnesium). The Effort Betrayal catalogs *behaviors she performed* — the runs, the clean eating, the weighed portions, the early mornings. The Graveyard says "everything I bought failed." The Effort Betrayal says "everything I *did* failed, and I did it perfectly." The emotional core is the injustice of unrewarded discipline, which is why it carries indignation rather than the resignation of the Graveyard.

**When to use:** Problem-aware and solution-aware audiences who identify as disciplined, compliant, or "good patients." Especially powerful for avatars whose self-image is built on effort — the runner, the meal-prepper, the rule-follower. The hook flatters that identity, then tells her the identity wasn't the problem.

**Structure:** [Specific, disciplined behaviors stacked] → [The result that should have followed but didn't] → [Implied "something else is going on"]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I run four times a week. Haven't eaten red meat in two years. Lost twenty-two pounds. Eat salmon, oatmeal, vegetables. Track every meal. Run 25 miles a week in the dark before sunrise.
> My LDL is 189. My doctor wants to put me on the same Simvastatin he gave my brother — who eats fast food four times a week.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> You're eating clean. You're working out. You're tracking your macros. You're drinking the water. You're getting the sleep.
> And the scale. Won't. Move.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I spent $800 on supplements, ate cleaner than I did in my 30s, and my doctor said my hormone levels were the worst she'd seen in a woman my age.

**Mechanic:** The disciplined behaviors must be specific and verifiable — "25 miles a week in the dark before sunrise," not "I exercise a lot." The specificity proves the effort was real, which is what makes the betrayal land. The result is then placed in direct contradiction to the effort, often sharpened by a comparison (the brother who does everything wrong and gets the same or better outcome). The reader who has also "done everything right" feels the same injustice and must continue to find out what she's been missing — because the alternative, that her effort was worthless, is intolerable. Note the natural pairing with the Exoneration Reframe (Type 18 below): Effort Betrayal sets up "it's not your fault" perfectly.

### Type 15: The Distant Authority / Blue Zone Discovery

Opens with a surprising outcome in a specific population, place, or culture — and frames the reader's problem as something that population has already solved. The curiosity loop is geographic or cultural: "why do *they* not have this problem?" The distance itself is the credibility signal — if a remote community or foreign researcher figured this out, it's outside the system the reader has lost faith in.

**When to use:** Solution-aware and unaware audiences. Works when the avatar is jaded with domestic medicine and the conventional solution set. The foreignness/remoteness bypasses the "I've heard all this before" filter because the source is outside her informational world.

**Structure:** [Surprising statistic or outcome tied to a specific place/group] → [Signal that they do something different] → [Implied "and you're about to learn what"]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> Why do Norwegian physical therapists have a 91% success rate with neck pain? It's not because they have better doctors. And it's not because people in Norway somehow have healthier necks.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> Then I attended a medical conference in Okinawa, Japan. Okinawa is a "Blue Zone" — a place where people live exceptionally long, healthy lives. Obesity is virtually non-existent there. At the conference, I met a Japanese researcher who specialized in the gut microbiome.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I refused. And what I found instead — in an Amish farming community in Pennsylvania — brought my blood pressure from 147/92 to 121/75 without a single pill.

**Mechanic:** The place must be specific and carry an intuitive logic for why it would hold a secret — Blue Zones (longevity), Scandinavia (advanced PT), Amish communities (pre-industrial living), a Himalayan fruit (ancient remedy). The reader's brain accepts the premise because the place already has cultural shorthand for "they're healthier there." The statistic ("91%," "obesity is virtually non-existent") makes it feel like data, not folklore. The loop is "what do they know that my doctor doesn't?" — and the distance from her failed system is exactly why she trusts it. **Compliance note:** the geographic frame must rest on a defensible claim. A vivid "Amish farm at 6 AM" scene is fine as narrative color; a fabricated success statistic attributed to a real institution is not. Keep the number directional or drop it.

### Type 16: The Headline Stack

A written-hook construction (not a spoken one) that front-loads the entire emotional premise into one dense, declarative run of short sentences before the body even begins. It reads like a newspaper headline that kept going — each clause adding a fact, the facts compounding into dread or intrigue. This is the hook AS the "above the fold" content: by the time the reader hits "See More," the whole stakes are already loaded.

This is distinct from Credential Stacking (which layers *identity markers* to build a person) and from the Timestamp Anchor (which opens on one precise moment). The Headline Stack layers *plot facts* — the number, the age, the family history, the prescription, the death — in compressed sequence, so the reader absorbs an entire situation in three seconds.

**When to use:** All awareness levels, written hooks only. Especially effective when the avatar shares a specific, statable situation (a lab number, a diagnosis, a family history) that can be stated as fact. Works as the headline/first-line layer that the narrative body then slows down and inhabits.

**Structure:** [Fact] . [Fact] . [Escalating fact] . [The fact that detonates] — all in short, clipped, declarative sentences.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> Total Cholesterol 267 at Age 59. My Father Hit 264 at Age 57. His Doctor Started Atorvastatin. He Died at 68 With Liver Damage and Memory Loss. Yesterday My Doctor Pulled Out Her Prescription Pad.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> Got my lab results back today. LDL 216. Total cholesterol 271. HDL 48. Triglycerides 229.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I was spending $197 a month on blood pressure supplements. Eight different bottles. Taken at three different times of day. My blood pressure dropped 4 points in 5 months. Four. Points.

**Mechanic:** The power is in compression and rhythm. Each sentence is a closed fact; the periods act as drumbeats. The reader can't skim it as marketing because it reads as reporting — flat, factual, unembellished. The detonation usually lands in the final clause ("He Died at 68," "Yesterday My Doctor Pulled Out Her Prescription Pad"), which reframes every preceding fact as a countdown. Note: this construction is often the SAME ad body with different headline stacks bolted on — the pipi_tea family runs one identical narrative behind a dozen different Headline Stacks. That is the testing logic: hold the body constant, rotate the stack. (See Hook Templating.)

### Type 17: The Observed-Change Hook (Someone Noticed First)

Opens not with the avatar's symptom but with another person *detecting a change in her* — usually before she can explain it. A spouse asks if she's seeing someone. A guest leaves a tearful review. A husband says she seems like herself again. The hook works because the change is externally witnessed, which makes it feel real and earned rather than self-reported.

This is the inverse of most hooks. Most hooks open on the avatar's internal experience. This one opens on the *evidence of transformation as seen from outside* — which doubles as social proof and as a curiosity loop ("what changed in her?"). It is most often used as an Outcome-First structure: the result is witnessed first, the cause revealed later.

**When to use:** Problem-aware and solution-aware audiences, particularly for desire angles rooted in relationship erosion, vitality loss, or invisible suffering (menopause, fatigue, intimacy, mood). The external observer restores what the avatar feels she's lost — the sense of being *seen*.

**Structure:** [Another person observes/reacts to a change in the narrator] → [Narrator's surprise or emotional response] → ["Let me back up" — the story of what changed]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> "Are you seeing someone?" That's what my husband asked me last Tuesday. Dead serious. I was so shocked. I just stared at him confused.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> I run an Airbnb. The other day, I got a text from a guest that made me cry. "I'm not joking, I woke up and cried. I forgot what it felt like to actually rest. Please share with me where you got those."

**Mechanic:** The observer's reaction must be specific and slightly destabilizing — "Are you seeing someone?" is alarming before it's flattering, which is what stops the scroll. The narrator's confusion ("I just stared at him") signals she didn't engineer the change, which makes it credible. Then "let me back up" opens the loop: the reader now wants the cause of a transformation she's already seen the proof of. This hook smuggles social proof into the opening line — the change is validated by a witness before the product is ever mentioned.

### Type 18: The Exoneration Reframe

A specific, high-value subtype of the Mechanism Reframe (Type 12). Where the general Mechanism Reframe overturns an *understanding* ("your nerve isn't damaged, it's starving"), the Exoneration Reframe overturns *self-blame*. It tells the avatar the failure was never hers — her body isn't broken, her willpower isn't weak, her effort wasn't wasted. The thing she's been blaming herself for has a mechanical cause she couldn't have known about.

**When to use:** Problem-aware and solution-aware audiences carrying self-blame — weight that won't move, effort that didn't pay off, a body that "betrayed" them. This is the natural payoff to an Effort Betrayal opening (Type 14), but it also stands alone. It is one of the most powerful reframes available because it converts shame into permission.

**Structure:** [Name the thing she blames herself for] → [Reframe that relocates the cause outside her control] → [Implied "and that means it's fixable"]

**Example (from swipe file):**
> It is NOT your fault. And your metabolism isn't "broken." It's blocked.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> Your doctor is looking at the fuel gauge. The tank is full. But the fuel line to the engine is completely blocked. Your cells are starving for thyroid hormone, even though there's plenty of it floating in your blood.

**Example (from swipe file):**
> If you're on levothyroxine, your TSH is normal, but you still feel like garbage and the scale keeps creeping up... you are not crazy. It is not your fault.

**Mechanic:** The reframe must do two things at once: remove the blame AND name a mechanical cause. "It's not your fault" alone is empty reassurance. "It's not your fault — your metabolism isn't broken, it's blocked" relocates the cause to something specific and, crucially, reversible. ("Broken" is hopeless; "blocked" implies a blockage that can be cleared.) The reader feels the relief of exoneration and the pull of curiosity ("blocked by what? how do I unblock it?") simultaneously. The relief is what disarms the ad filter; the curiosity is what carries her into the mechanism. **Voice caution:** "It's not your fault" is high-impact but high-frequency in the category — if the competitor set is saturating it, vary the construction ("you weren't doing it wrong," "the effort was never the problem") rather than running the exact phrase.

## Awareness Level → Hook Type Mapping

This maps which hook types are effective at each awareness level. Mismatching is the most common hook failure.

### Unaware
The reader doesn't know she has the problem your product solves. She might have symptoms but hasn't connected them to a condition.

**Effective:** Timestamp Anchor, Confession Lead, Borrowed Authority, Narrative Trojan Horse, Professional Insider, Distant Authority / Blue Zone Discovery, Observed-Change (all indirect — they pull the reader into a story before revealing what the story is about)

**Ineffective:** Symptom Sniper (she doesn't identify with the symptom category yet), Failed Solution Graveyard (she hasn't tried solutions), Effort Betrayal (she isn't yet framing her effort as failing at anything), Contrarian Reveal (she doesn't hold the belief being contradicted), Misdiagnosis Reveal (she doesn't hold a diagnosis), Exoneration Reframe (she isn't yet blaming herself for a named problem), Trend-Jack (she hasn't seen the competing ads), Brand Confession (she doesn't know the brand)

### Problem-Aware
The reader knows she has the problem. She feels it daily. She may not know what's causing it or that solutions exist.

**Effective:** Symptom Sniper, Dismissal Dramatization, Confession Lead, Timestamp Anchor, Borrowed Authority, Professional Insider, Misdiagnosis Reveal, Mechanism Reframe, Effort Betrayal, Exoneration Reframe, Headline Stack, Observed-Change

**Ineffective:** Contrarian Reveal (she doesn't hold strong beliefs about solutions yet), Brand Confession (she doesn't know the brand)

### Solution-Aware
The reader has tried things. She knows solutions exist but hasn't found one that works for her. She's skeptical.

**Effective:** Contrarian Reveal (all subtypes), Failed Solution Graveyard, Effort Betrayal, Symptom Sniper (re-framed around failed solutions), Dismissal Dramatization, Borrowed Authority, Professional Insider, Misdiagnosis Reveal, Trend-Jack, Mechanism Reframe, Exoneration Reframe, Distant Authority / Blue Zone Discovery, Headline Stack, Narrative Trojan Horse (bypasses ad fatigue)

**Ineffective:** Confession Lead (she's past shame — she's in frustrated-searcher mode). Observed-Change works only if her problem is one others can see; for invisible/internal problems she's already tried to fix, it can feel beside the point.

### Product-Aware
The reader knows about your product (or your category). She needs a reason to believe THIS one is different.

**Effective:** Contrarian Reveal, Timestamp Anchor (specific results story), Borrowed Authority (credible endorsement reframe), Professional Insider, Brand Confession

**Ineffective:** Symptom Sniper (she already knows her symptoms), Failed Solution Graveyard (she's past the search — she needs proof of results), Dismissal Dramatization (she's moved past the doctor stage)

## Hook Templating

The most successful native brands don't just write good hooks — they build hook SYSTEMS. They find a structural skeleton that converts and then adapt it across conditions, medications, demographics, and points of view.

The swipe file proves this definitively. The Misdiagnosis Reveal runs identically for rheumatoid arthritis (32→47), osteoarthritis (38→53), and neuropathy (47→61). The Professional Insider runs for pharmacists, nurses, nursing professors, neurologists, and orthopedic surgeons. The Contrarian Reveal runs for LDL cholesterol AND blood pressure with the same "I never filled the prescription" skeleton.

### How to Identify a Templateable Hook

A hook is templateable when:
1. The structure works because of the ARCHITECTURE, not because of the specific details
2. The specific details (condition, medication, age, profession) can be swapped without breaking the mechanic
3. The emotional lever (curiosity, identity, dissonance) stays the same across swaps

### The Five Adaptation Axes

Once you've identified a winning hook skeleton, adapt it across these axes:

**Axis 1: Condition Swap**
Same structure, different health condition. If it works for arthritis, test it for neuropathy, sciatica, IBS, etc. The avatar's emotional state (frustration, resignation, hope) must be analogous across conditions.

- Template: "I was diagnosed with [CONDITION] at [AGE]. I'm [CURRENT AGE] now. Turns out I never had [CONDITION] at all."
- Arthritis version: "I was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis at 32. I'm 47 now. Turns out I never had arthritis at all."
- Neuropathy version: "I was told my neuropathy was permanent at 47. I'm 61 now. Turns out my nerve cells were never actually dying."

**Axis 2: Medication Swap**
Same structure, different medication being contradicted. The reader must be currently taking (or considering) the medication for the hook to land.

- Template: "My [METRIC] Was [NUMBER]. My Doctor Prescribed [MEDICATION]. [TIME] Later, It's [BETTER NUMBER]. I Never Filled the Prescription."
- Cholesterol version: "My LDL Was 186. My Doctor Prescribed Lipitor. Five Months Later, It's 134. I Never Filled the Prescription."
- Blood pressure version: "My Blood Pressure Was 147/94. My Doctor Prescribed Lisinopril. Four Months Later, It's 124/78. I Never Filled the Prescription."

**Axis 3: Professional POV Swap**
Same structure, different professional narrator. Each profession brings different credibility and different stakes.

- Template: "I've been a [PROFESSION] for [YEARS]. [CREDENTIAL STACK]. [MEDICATION] gave me [BENEFIT]. Then it [TOOK SOMETHING ESSENTIAL TO MY PROFESSION]."
- Pharmacist version: "I've been a pharmacist for 28 years... It gave me perfect cholesterol. Then it took my memory."
- Barber version: "I've been a barber for 44... The drug gave me perfect cholesterol. Then it destroyed my hands."

**Axis 4: POV Shift**
Same story, told from a different narrator's perspective. First-person patient. First-person professional. Third-person observer. Spouse. Family member. Each POV creates different emotional access points.

- First-person patient: "I was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis at 32."
- Third-person professional: "A patient came to me diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis at 32. She's 47 now."
- Spouse: "My husband took Rosuvastatin for 14 years. His numbers were perfect the entire time."
- Child: "My father took Atorvastatin for twelve years. Never missed a dose."

**Axis 5: Emotional Consequence Swap**
Same structure, but the thing the medication/condition took from the narrator is different. Memory, hands, independence, intimacy, identity.

- Memory: "It gave me perfect cholesterol. Then it took my memory."
- Physical function: "The drug gave me perfect cholesterol. Then it destroyed my hands."
- Independence: "Now he can't button his own shirt. Can't drive."

**Axis 6: Place / Source Swap (Distant Authority hooks)**
Same "they solved what your system can't" structure, different population or origin. The place must carry intuitive shorthand for why it would hold a secret.

- Template: "[Surprising outcome] in [specific place/group]. It's not because [obvious explanation]."
- Norway version: "Why do Norwegian physical therapists have a 91% success rate with neck pain?"
- Blue Zone version: "Obesity is virtually non-existent in Okinawa. A researcher there told me why."
- Amish version: "What I found in an Amish farming community in Pennsylvania brought my blood pressure from 147/92 to 121/75."

**Axis 7: Headline-Stack Rotation (hold the body, rotate the opener)**
This is the highest-leverage testing axis and the one most underused. Keep the entire ad body identical and rotate ONLY the Headline Stack / first line in front of it. The pipi_tea family runs one identical narrative ("My father died at 68. I'm 59...") behind a dozen completely different opening headlines — a timestamp version, a misdiagnosis-fear version, a prescription-on-the-counter version, a question version. Each tests a different emotional entry point into the same story.

- Body held constant; openers rotated:
  - Headline Stack: "Total Cholesterol 267 at Age 59. My Father Hit 264 at Age 57... He Died at 68."
  - Timestamp Anchor: "At 2:19 AM I Typed 'Can Atorvastatin Cause Liver Damage Like It Did to My Dad.'"
  - Confession framing: "For Three Weeks the Atorvastatin Prescription Has Been Sitting on My Kitchen Counter, Unfilled."
  - Question framing: "Cholesterol Too High. Father Dead From Statins at 68. Prescription Unfilled. What Now?"

This axis is cheap to produce (the body is already written) and isolates the hook as the single variable — which is exactly what makes it the fastest way to learn which emotional entry point the audience responds to. When a body is working but a hook is fatiguing, rotate the opener before rebuilding anything downstream.

### How to Use Templates in Generation

When generating a hook batch:
1. If a proven template exists for the product category, generate at least 2 hooks using template adaptation (different axes)
2. Generate at least 2-3 hooks from NON-template types to ensure angle diversity
3. Never generate a batch that is ALL templates — you'll saturate the audience with structurally similar hooks
4. When adapting a template, change at least TWO axes simultaneously (e.g., condition swap + POV shift) to prevent the hooks from feeling like obvious variations of each other
5. When a winning ad body already exists, consider Axis 7 (Headline-Stack Rotation) before writing anything new — rotating the opener over a proven body is the cheapest high-leverage test available

## Medium-Specific Rules

### Written Hooks (Facebook Ad Primary Text)

- The "See More" cut on mobile falls at approximately 125 characters / 3 lines. The cut MUST fall MID-THOUGHT. If the reader reaches a complete thought before the cut, she has no reason to tap "See More." An incomplete sentence, an unresolved contradiction, or an unanswered question at the cut point is mandatory.

- Written hooks can use paragraph breaks as pacing tools. Short paragraphs (1–2 sentences) create white space that draws the eye downward. A single-sentence paragraph after a multi-sentence paragraph creates emphasis.

- Written hooks can be 1–5 lines. The hook ends when the open loop is fully established and the body begins resolving it. Exception: hooks using Credential Stacking (especially Professional Insider hooks) may run 5–8 lines because the stack itself is building tension — each credential is a line of the hook, not preamble.

- The hook must look and feel like an organic Facebook post. No headlines. No ALL CAPS (exception: a single all-caps sentence used for emphasis as seen in swipe file examples like the blood pressure/cholesterol hooks — but use sparingly). No emoji unless the avatar uses emoji in her own posts. No "ATTENTION:" or "WARNING:" prefixes. If it looks like an ad above the fold, it dies before "See More."

### Spoken Hooks (Video Ad Opening Lines)

- The first 3 seconds determine whether the viewer stays. The first sentence must contain either a specific symptom call-out or an emotionally charged statement — there is no time for scene-setting.

- Spoken hooks are shorter: 1–3 sentences max before the body begins.

- Spoken hooks must sound THOUGHT, not READ. Test by reading aloud. If any word sounds like it was chosen by a writer instead of spoken by a person, replace it.

- Spoken hooks benefit from sentence fragments. "Constant bloating. Every single day. Didn't matter what I ate." beats "I was experiencing constant bloating every single day regardless of what I ate."

- The video hook must work WITHOUT the visual. Many viewers scroll with sound on but eyes half-focused. If the hook only works when you're watching, it fails half your audience.

## Pattern Interrupt

The hook must break the pattern of the organic feed. This means it must LOOK native (no ad formatting, no brand language) but FEEL unexpected. The unexpected element is what stops the scroll.

**Five pattern interrupt mechanics:**

1. **Contradiction** — Two things that shouldn't both be true. "My doctor said my cholesterol was perfect. I stopped taking everything she prescribed." Or the more devastating version: "His LDL stayed under 100 the entire time. He died face-down on the floor of his woodshop."

2. **Confession** — Vulnerability that's rare in a public feed. Most feed content is curated, positive, or neutral. A raw admission breaks the pattern. "I haven't told anyone this, not even my husband." Or: "I need to publicly apologize to anyone who's ever bought PrimeCell H2 before, because I lied."

3. **Borrowed urgency** — An authority figure treating something as urgent that the reader treats as normal. "My pharmacist grabbed my arm and said, 'Are you still taking this?'" The urgency comes from the authority's reaction, not the narrator's language.

4. **Precision** — Numbers, dates, dosages, and details that are too specific to be fabricated. "112 over 78. For the first time in nine years. Without the Lisinopril." Precision breaks the pattern of vague health claims in the feed.

5. **Category intercept** — Referencing other ads, trending content, or cultural moments the reader is already aware of. "If you've been seeing all these parasite cleanse ads lately..." This breaks the pattern because the reader is used to being SOLD TO, not having someone comment on the selling. It positions the narrator as an ally, not a marketer.

6. **Promised indignation** — Telling the reader, up front, that they're about to be angry — then enumerating why. "By the end of this, you're gonna be pissed. Because there are three things happening right now." This breaks the pattern because it makes a promise about the reader's future emotional state and frames what follows as a list of withheld grievances. It pairs with another opener rather than standing alone (it usually follows a Father's-Path or Effort-Betrayal first line) and acts as an escalator into the body. The numbered "three things happening right now" structure converts a vague threat into a checklist the reader feels compelled to complete. Use the indignation engine sparingly and only where it's earned — stacking promised-anger on a vulnerable health audience is exactly the kind of emotional manipulation that draws compliance scrutiny, so the grievances listed must be real and substantiated, not invented to manufacture rage.

## The "See More" Engineering Protocol (Written Hooks Only)

The mobile "See More" cut is the single most important conversion point in a Facebook ad. If the reader doesn't tap, the ad doesn't exist.

**Rule 1:** Count characters. The cut falls at approximately 125 characters (varies slightly by device). Map your hook against this cut point BEFORE finalizing.

**Rule 2:** The cut must fall in one of three positions:
- Mid-sentence (the sentence is grammatically incomplete)
- After a contradiction has been established but before it's explained
- After a question has been asked but before it's answered

**Rule 3:** Never let the cut fall after a period that closes a complete thought. A complete thought before the fold = permission to stop reading.

**Rule 4:** The ideal cut point creates a question in the reader's mind that is SO specific she physically taps to resolve it. Vague curiosity ("I wonder what happens next") is weak. Specific curiosity ("wait — she quit her statin and her numbers got BETTER?") is strong.

## Generation Process

When generating hooks, follow this sequence. Do not skip steps.

### Step 1: Confirm Inputs

Before writing a single word, confirm or request:
- **Avatar:** Who is she? What does she feel daily? What has she tried?
- **Awareness level:** Unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, or product-aware?
- **Desire:** What is the visceral felt problem? (Not the clinical problem — the FELT problem.)
- **Angle:** What's the psychological entry point for this desire?
- **Medium:** Written (Facebook primary text) or spoken (video script)?
- **Brand/product context:** What product does this hook eventually lead to? (Hooks must be tonally consistent with the body they lead into.)

### Step 2: Select Hook Types

Based on the awareness level mapping, select 3–4 hook types that are effective for this audience. Do NOT generate all hooks from the same type — rotate types across the batch. For solution-aware audiences, always include at least one Contrarian Reveal variant AND at least one non-contrarian type for angle diversity.

### Step 3: Select Loop Types

Decide whether each hook opens a curiosity loop or an identity loop. A batch should include BOTH types. If generating 5+ hooks, aim for roughly 60/40 split in either direction.

### Step 4: Check for Templateable Structures

If the product category has proven hook templates (Misdiagnosis Reveal for condition-based products, Professional Insider for medication-alternative products, Contrarian Reveal metrics hooks for measurable health conditions), generate 2–3 hooks using template adaptation across different axes. Then fill the rest of the batch with non-template hooks.

### Step 5: Engineer Specificity

For each hook, layer in at least TWO of the four specificity layers (concrete detail, scene construction, dialogue as evidence, credential stacking). Check each hook against:
- Does it contain a number, date, or measurement?
- Does it place the reader inside a moment?
- Does it include dialogue or quoted speech?
- Does it stack credentials or identity markers for cumulative weight?

If a hook has none of these, it's not specific enough. Rewrite.

### Step 6: Check the "See More" Cut (Written Only)

Map the hook against the 125-character cut point. If the cut falls after a complete thought, restructure so it falls mid-thought.

### Step 7: Read Aloud Test (Spoken Only)

Read the hook out loud. If any word sounds chosen-by-a-writer instead of said-by-a-person, replace it. If the hook takes more than 5 seconds to speak, it's too long.

### Step 8: The Scroll Test

For each hook, ask: "If this appeared in my organic feed between a friend's vacation photo and a recipe video, would I stop scrolling?" If the answer is "maybe" or "probably," the hook isn't strong enough. Rewrite.

### Step 9: Anti-Mimicry Check

If generating multiple hooks for the same product, verify that no two hooks use the same:
- Hook type
- Loop type
- Opening word/phrase
- Emotional angle
- Specificity layer emphasis
- Template (if using templated hooks, each must adapt along different axes)

If any two hooks feel like variations of the same hook, one of them needs to be rebuilt from a different type entirely.

## Evaluation Criteria

When evaluating existing hooks (your own or competitors'), score against these six dimensions:

1. **Specificity (0–5):** Does the hook contain concrete details, scenes, dialogue, or credential stacks? Or is it generic and interchangeable with any product in the category?

2. **Emotional Accuracy (0–5):** Does the hook mirror the avatar's CURRENT emotional state? Or does it project an emotion the writer wants the avatar to feel?

3. **Awareness Match (0–5):** Is the hook calibrated to the avatar's awareness level? Or does it assume knowledge/experience the avatar doesn't have (or already has)?

4. **Loop Strength (0–5):** Does the hook create a specific open loop that demands resolution? Or could the reader feel satisfied stopping?

5. **Organic Camouflage (0–5):** Does the hook look and feel like something a real person would post on Facebook? Or does it feel written, polished, or ad-like?

6. **Template Potential (0–5):** Could this hook structure be adapted across conditions, medications, demographics, or POVs while maintaining its power? A score of 5 means the skeleton is proven and modular. A score of 0 means the hook depends entirely on its specific details and can't be adapted.

**Score interpretation:**
- 25–30: Exceptional hook. Run it and template it.
- 20–24: Strong foundation. Minor specificity or loop-strength improvements needed.
- 13–19: Mediocre. Hook type or awareness level is likely mismatched. Rebuild.
- Below 13: Fundamental problems. Start over with a different type and angle.

## Common Failures and Fixes

**Hook is too vague.** The avatar reads it and thinks "yeah, that's kind of me" instead of "that's EXACTLY me." Fix: Add one more specificity layer. Replace general descriptions with scenes. Use a credential stack to add weight.

**Hook sounds like an ad.** Any of these signals: ALL CAPS (overuse), emoji, exclamation points, "ATTENTION," benefit language, product mentions, brand tone. Fix: Rewrite as if you're posting to your personal Facebook. Would you actually write it this way?

**Hook matches the wrong awareness level.** A symptom sniper aimed at an unaware audience. A failed-solution graveyard aimed at a problem-aware audience who hasn't tried anything yet. Fix: Re-check the awareness mapping and select a type that matches.

**"See More" cut falls after a complete thought.** The reader reaches a natural stopping point before she taps. Fix: Restructure so the cut falls mid-sentence or after an unresolved contradiction.

**Every hook in the batch sounds similar.** Same type, same loop, same opening rhythm, same template axis. Fix: Force at least 3 different hook types and both loop types into the batch. Change the opening word of every hook. If using templates, adapt along different axes.

**Hook tries to do too much.** It educates, sells, and hooks simultaneously. Fix: The hook does ONE thing — open a loop. Everything else belongs in the body. Exception: a single-sentence mechanism reframe (Type 12) that's sharp enough to function as the loop itself.

**Hook uses marketer language.** Words the avatar would never use: "revolutionary," "breakthrough," "optimize," "transform," "unlock." Fix: Use the words you'd find in the avatar's Reddit post, Facebook comment, or text to her friend.

**Hook wastes the Professional Insider by under-stacking credentials.** "I'm a doctor and statins are bad" is flat. The Professional Insider needs the credential stack — the years, the patients, the prescriptions filled, the career built — so that when the contradiction lands, it has WEIGHT. Fix: Stack 3–5 credentials before the reveal. Make the reader feel the career that's being risked.

**Hook uses a Narrative Trojan Horse that's boring.** The non-health opening must be genuinely interesting as a standalone story. If the reader wouldn't care about the jacket, the pocket, the Goodwill trip without the health angle, the Trojan Horse fails. Fix: Test the opening as a standalone Facebook post. If nobody would engage with it, the narrative isn't strong enough to carry the disguise.

**Effort Betrayal lists effort that isn't specific.** "I eat healthy and exercise" doesn't transfer the injustice — it's a claim, not proof. Fix: Make every behavior verifiable and concrete ("25 miles a week in the dark before sunrise," "tracked every meal," "$800 on supplements"). The specificity is what proves the effort was real, which is what makes its failure feel unjust.

**Exoneration Reframe reassures without relocating the cause.** "It's not your fault" by itself is empty — the reader has heard it and doesn't believe it. Fix: Pair the exoneration with a specific, reversible mechanical cause in the same breath ("not broken — blocked," "the fuel line is clogged"). Remove the blame AND name the fixable thing, or the reframe is just a pat on the head.

**Generational Refusal coasts on a generic decline.** "My dad got sick from his meds" carries no dread. Fix: Make the family member's decline sensory and specific (the cough, the falls, the forgotten names, the yellowed skin) and state the duration as a sentence ("22 years"). Then check the engine — if the live library is already full of dead-parent openers, rotate to a different engine before running another one.

**Headline Stack reads as a paragraph instead of a stack.** If the opener flows as connected prose, it loses the drumbeat. Fix: Break it into closed, clipped, factual sentences with hard periods. Each clause is one fact. The detonation goes last.

### Hook Autopsy: Bad vs. Good

**BAD: "March 14. Turned 53 today. My mother died at 55. Heart attack. Massive. Dead before she hit the kitchen floor."**
Why it fails: "March 14. Turned 53 today." has zero problem signal. The avatar doesn't know what this is about. A date and an age aren't a hook — they're context that belongs AFTER she's already hooked. The mother's death arrives on line 3, but the avatar had to get through two lines of nothing to reach it. The real hook might be the mortality angle, but it takes too long to surface and still doesn't mirror the avatar's specific daily problem.

**BAD: "I need to tell someone this because I can't keep carrying it alone."**
Why it fails: This is pure throat-clearing. It's broad, emotional, and could be about literally anything — divorce, debt, grief, addiction. There's no problem specificity. No avatar relevance. No awareness-level signal. The avatar scrolls because nothing in this line tells her it's about HER problem.

**BAD: "I'm a doctor and I have something important to tell you about statins."**
Why it fails: Weak Professional Insider — no credential stack, no specificity, no stakes. "I'm a doctor" is generic. "Something important" is vague. The reader has seen a thousand "I'm a doctor" ads. Without the years, the patients, the personal cost, the professional risk — the authority is a prop, not a person. Compare to: "I've been a pharmacist for 28 years. A husband for 24. A father for 19." — THAT narrator is a person with a life being threatened.

**GOOD: "I have seventeen bottles in my medicine cabinet."**
Why it works: Solution-aware hook. The avatar who has tried everything sees her own cabinet. "Seventeen" is specific — not "a bunch" or "too many." The number creates instant identity recognition: "that's me." She's hooked because someone finally described her exact situation.

**GOOD: "I was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis at 32. I'm 47 now. Turns out I never had arthritis at all."**
Why it works: Misdiagnosis Reveal. Three sentences. The first establishes shared identity (same diagnosis, similar age). The second creates a time span the reader feels viscerally (fifteen years). The third detonates: "I never had arthritis at all." For a reader who identifies as an arthritis patient, this sentence challenges her entire framework. She MUST continue because the hook just told her the thing she thinks she has might not be what she has. Templateable across conditions. Proven across at least three variants in the swipe file.

**GOOD: "I've been a pharmacist for 28 years. A husband for 24. A father for 19. I've filled over 300,000 prescriptions... I've been on Atorvastatin for nine. It gave me perfect cholesterol. Then it took my memory."**
Why it works: Professional Insider with full credential stack. Every credential adds weight: professional identity, family identity, competence ("300,000 prescriptions"), personal quality ("never forgets a name"). The reader builds a complete picture of this person — his competence, his values, his life. "Then it took my memory" demolishes all of it in six words. The gap between who he was and what the medication did IS the hook. And because he's a pharmacist — someone the reader trusts to give her medication — his turning against that medication is the highest possible trust signal.

**GOOD: "'Your LDL is still elevated.' You said it without looking up."**
Why it works: Contrarian/dismissal hook using dialogue. The avatar has heard these exact words in her own doctor's office. She's back in the exam room instantly. "Without looking up" is the surgical detail — it mirrors the specific indignity of being dismissed by a doctor who doesn't even make eye contact. Problem-aware readers recognize the appointment. Solution-aware readers feel the accumulated frustration of hearing this every year.

**GOOD: "I run four times a week. Haven't eaten red meat in two years. Lost twenty-two pounds. My LDL is 189. My doctor wants the same Simvastatin he gave my brother — who eats fast food four times a week."**
Why it works: Effort Betrayal. The disciplined behaviors are specific and verifiable, so the effort reads as real, not claimed. The comparison to the brother who does everything wrong and gets the same prescription is the surgical cut — it converts private frustration into visible injustice. The disciplined reader sees herself in the effort and must continue, because the only alternative to "something else is going on" is "all my discipline was worthless," which she can't accept.

**GOOD: "It's NOT your fault. And your metabolism isn't 'broken.' It's blocked."**
Why it works: Exoneration Reframe. It removes self-blame and relocates the cause in the same three sentences. "Broken" is hopeless; "blocked" implies something that can be cleared — which is what converts the relief into curiosity ("blocked by what?"). The reader who has been blaming her own willpower gets permission and a puzzle simultaneously. The relief disarms her ad filter; the curiosity carries her into the mechanism.

**GOOD: "Why do Norwegian physical therapists have a 91% success rate with neck pain? It's not because they have better doctors."**
Why it works: Distant Authority / Blue Zone Discovery. The place carries intuitive shorthand (Scandinavia = advanced, methodical care), so the premise is accepted instantly. The statistic makes it feel like data. Pre-empting the obvious explanation ("not because they have better doctors") sharpens the loop to its real question: "then what ARE they doing?" The source sits outside the system the reader has lost faith in, which is exactly why she trusts it.

## The Commandments

1. **The hook opens a loop. Period.** It doesn't sell, pitch, or explain. It creates a gap that the body resolves. (A single-sentence mechanism reframe is the one exception — but only when the reframe IS the loop.)
2. **Specificity is not optional.** Every hook must contain at least TWO of the four specificity layers.
3. **Awareness level determines hook type.** Mismatching is the #1 failure mode.
4. **The hook must pass the organic feed test.** If it looks like an ad, it dies before it's read.
5. **Curiosity loops and identity loops are different tools.** Use both. Know which one you're using and why.
6. **The "See More" cut is an engineering problem, not an afterthought.** Map every written hook against the cut point before finalizing.
7. **Read every spoken hook out loud.** If it sounds written, it is. Rewrite.
8. **Batch hooks must be anti-mimicked.** No two hooks in a batch share a type, loop, and opening pattern.
9. **The scroll test is the only test that matters.** Would this stop YOUR thumb between a vacation photo and a recipe video?
10. **If it smells like AI, it dies.** No hook that sounds generated survives a real feed.
11. **Template what works.** When you find a winning hook structure, adapt it across conditions, medications, demographics, and POVs. That's how you build a hook system, not just a hook.
12. **The credential stack earns the turn.** Professional Insider hooks without a full credential stack are just "I'm a doctor" ads. The stack is what makes the insider turn feel like a crisis of conscience instead of a marketing gimmick.
13. **Some hooks earn the right to delay the problem.** Narrative Trojan Horses and Credential Stacks can delay the problem reveal IF (and only if) every pre-reveal line is specific, concrete, and builds narrative momentum. Vague preamble is still throat-clearing. Vivid storytelling is not.

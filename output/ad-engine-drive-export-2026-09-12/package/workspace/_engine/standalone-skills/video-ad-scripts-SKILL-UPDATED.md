# Video Ad Script Writing

This skill governs how to write direct response video ad scripts that convert through natural conversation, not performance. These scripts are meant to be SPOKEN — by a person on camera, into a phone, as a talking head. Every rule here exists because spoken persuasion works differently than written persuasion.

The core difference: Written copy can re-engage a reader who drifts. Video cannot. Once the viewer's thumb moves, it's over. Every sentence must create enough forward pull that stopping feels like missing something.

**Companion skill:** The `mynuora-direct-response-video-ads` skill contains 29 transcribed production video ads from the myNuora Meta Ad Library. ALWAYS read that skill's transcripts alongside this one before writing any video ad script — those transcripts are real-world executions of every framework, law, and pattern documented here.

Read this entire file before writing. The laws are interdependent.

## The Core Principle: Belief Conversation

A converting video script is a belief conversation disguised as a person telling you something important. The viewer should feel like they're overhearing someone explain something fascinating to a friend — not watching an ad.

This means the script must sound like it was THOUGHT, not WRITTEN. Real people don't speak in topic sentences and supporting evidence. They speak in connective chains — one thought pulling the next thought forward through "so," "and," "but," and "because."

The test: Read the script out loud. If any sentence sounds like it belongs in an article, rewrite it until it sounds like something a person would actually say while explaining this to their sister over coffee.

## The Punch-in-the-Gut Architecture

The laws in this skill are the grammar. Punch in the Gut is the architecture they assemble into for long-form emotional VSLs — the 2-7 minute scripts where the viewer has to be held for minutes, not seconds.

Its governing idea: **the viewer has to earn the solution.** The script is not a pitch with a story attached. It is an emotional descent followed by an emotional ascent, with the product withheld until the viewer has spent enough time inside the narrator's life that the solution feels like a reward rather than an offer.

Five commitments define it:

**We sell the outcome, not the product.** One deep emotional driver runs the entire script. In the Nuora menopause VSL it is *freedom from social shame* — every symptom, every scene, every result is chosen because it touches shame. The weight is never the point. The weight is the mechanism by which shame is delivered.

**We keep the viewer on the edge of the seat.** Curiosity loops are opened faster than they are closed, so there is always at least one unanswered question in the viewer's head. Dopamine, not information, is what keeps the thumb still.

**We hold the viewer's hand.** Beliefs are unwound one at a time, in order, meeting the viewer exactly where she already is. You never ask her to accept two new things at once.

**We twist the knife.** Every belief shift is anchored to a specific, humiliating, sensory moment from the avatar's nightmare state. Abstract pain does not build trust. Precise pain does.

**We filter down to buyers.** Length is a feature. A viewer who sits through six minutes of this has self-selected into the buying pool. The ad is not trying to be watchable by everyone.

**The macro shape:**

1. **Before/after bookend opens** — "here's how I went from this to this" (visual proof up front, mechanism withheld)
2. **Nightmare state, present tense** — the specific humiliations, not the diagnosis
3. **Failed solution stack** — hope, then embarrassment, on a loop
4. **The final straw** — the worst villain, usually the trusted authority
5. **Knife twist** — the emotional line that indicts the villain by what it did NOT feel
6. **Big loop opens** — "in the next thirty seconds I'm going to tell you what I found"
7. **Loop deliberately delayed** — "but before I do, you need to understand how bad it got"
8. **Rock bottom scene** — the single most painful moment in the script
9. **Discovery vehicle** — a non-authority source (forum, practitioner, stranger)
10. **Mechanism via sensory proxy** — the viewer feels the mechanism in her own body
11. **Mechanism chain to the symptom** — ending on the exact symptom from the hook
12. **Failed-research montage** — the villain teardown at solution level
13. **The unexpected discovery** — the ingredient, framed as a surprise to the researchers themselves
14. **The problem with the discovery** — a constraint that creates the product's reason to exist
15. **The missing pairing** — why most companies get it wrong
16. **Product exists, unnamed** — "a small American company"
17. **The catch** — expectation-setting that inoculates against early quitting
18. **Skepticism, then purchase** — the narrator resists on the viewer's behalf
19. **Results as a timeline, honestly slow** — nothing, then small, then the moment
20. **Before/after bookend closes** — loop 1 pays off
21. **The sermon** — zoom out from "me" to "us," name the cost of waiting
22. **Brand named** — this late, and only now
23. **Mechanism-derived scarcity + guarantee + CTA**

Not every script needs all 23 beats. A 90-second script compresses to roughly beats 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, 23. But the ORDER never changes, and beat 22 never moves earlier.

## Format Types

Every video ad script falls into one of three formats. Identify which format you're writing BEFORE you start.

### Format A: The Expert Educator

A credentialed persona (doctor, researcher, practitioner, specialist) educates the viewer about the mechanism. They recommend the product as a professional conclusion, not a personal endorsement.

**Voice:** Authoritative but warm. Teaching, not lecturing. Think "the cool professor," not "the textbook." Uses phrases like "what you need to know is," "in simple terms," "so what do we have to do?"

**Structure spine:**
1. Symptom call-out hook (direct, specific, visual)
2. Credential drop (ONE line, fast, move on)
3. "Here's what's actually happening" transition
4. Mechanism education through a cause-and-effect chain (what causes what, plain language)
5. Villain teardown of existing solutions (stating which cause each misses)
6. Ingredient/solution reveal as scientific discovery
7. "The brand I/we recommend" (professional recommendation framing)
8. CTA

**Key rule:** The expert never SELLS. They RECOMMEND. The entire script is education. The product is the footnote.

**Audience participation framing:** The best expert scripts include moments that make the viewer feel like a student who just "got it." Phrases like "So what do we have to do? We have to break it down" or "So basically: [simple summary of the chain]."

### Format B: The Personal Discovery Journey

A first-person narrator who suffered, failed with conventional solutions, discovered the mechanism through research, and found the product through her own search.

**Voice:** Vulnerable, relatable, slightly frustrated. Uses phrases like "so I started researching," "that's when I found out," "I was honestly furious."

**Structure spine:**
1. Emotional hook (vulnerability, confession, visceral moment)
2. Failed solution stack (what she tried, why it didn't work — brief)
3. Discovery moment (how she encountered the mechanism — Facebook group, Reddit thread, late-night research, a study)
4. Mechanism education (learned from the source, not self-generated)
5. "Alright great plan, but where do I get all this?" transition
6. Product discovery (accidental, peer-recommended, reluctant)
7. Skepticism before trial ("I'd been burned before")
8. Progressive results measured in life moments
9. CTA

**Key rule:** The narrator is NEVER the expert. She learns the mechanism from a study, a forum post, a specialist, a group member. She discovers. She doesn't teach.

**The "where do I get this" bridge:** After the narrator understands the mechanism and knows what ingredients she needs, there must be a moment of practical frustration: "Great, but where do I actually find all this in one place without playing scientist?" This bridges mechanism education to product discovery naturally because it's the exact thought the viewer is having at the same moment.

**Format B is the default carrier for Punch in the Gut.** The architecture above is Format B extended and emotionally deepened.

### Format C: The Third-Party Story

Someone else's story delivers the mechanism education. A mentor, a boyfriend, a friend, a colleague. The narrator WITNESSES and REPORTS another person's knowledge or transformation.

**Voice:** Varies by relationship. Could be admiring (mentor story), protective (partner story), excited (friend story).

**Structure spine:**
1. Relationship hook ("My [person] told me..." / "She kept saying..." / "I almost lost my [relationship] until...")
2. Conflict or problem established through the relationship
3. The other person introduces the mechanism (or the narrator researches to help the other person)
4. Mechanism education delivered through dialogue or reported discovery
5. Product found by one of the two parties
6. Results witnessed by the narrator
7. CTA

**Key rule:** Two layers of social proof are baked into the narrative. The narrator's belief in the product comes from watching someone else's results, which is more credible than self-reported results.

## Spoken Cadence Laws

### Law 1: Connective Tissue as Momentum

Almost every sentence should begin with a connective word: "So," "And," "But," or "Because." Each does a specific job:

- **"So"** = logical consequence. Pulls the listener forward. "So when estrogen drops, the gut lining weakens."
- **"And"** = additive stacking. Builds pressure. "And when the body isn't getting proper nutrients, it goes into panic."
- **"But"** = micro-pivot. Recaptures wandering attention. "But here's where it gets scary."
- **"Because"** = backward justification. Makes the logic feel airtight. "Because the bacteria underneath survives and regrows."

These connectors eliminate natural pause points where the viewer might scroll. There is no "exit ramp" between thoughts.

**Rule:** At least 70% of sentences in the mechanism section should begin with one of these four words. In emotional sections, this drops to around 50%.

### Law 2: Thinking-Out-Loud Cadence

The best converting scripts include moments where the narrator processes IN REAL TIME rather than reporting conclusions. These are trust signals — "this person is remembering, not reciting."

Examples:
- "So I'm like, alright, great plan, but where the heck do I get all this stuff?"
- "And I'm reading this thinking, so THAT'S the missing step nobody's talking about."
- "So my mind started racing. Like, was this why the pooch wouldn't budge?"
- "I may have been born at night, but I wasn't born last night."

**Rule:** Every script needs at least 2-3 thinking-out-loud moments. These are NOT scripted reactions ("I couldn't believe it!"). They are specific internal processing made external.

**Advanced use:** Put the thinking-out-loud beat at the exact moment the VIEWER'S objection arrives. "I was thinking, well that's great for the puffiness, but what about my pooch?" is not a narrator beat. It is the viewer's own doubt, spoken by the narrator half a second before the viewer finishes forming it. Done right, the viewer feels understood instead of sold to. See Law 15.

### Law 3: No Exit Ramps

Every sentence must create a question or tension that the next sentence resolves. The viewer should never reach a point where they feel "okay, I got it" before the CTA.

**How to create forward pull:**
- End explanation chains with "But here's the thing..." or "But it gets worse..."
- Use incomplete cause-and-effect: "So when estrogen drops, the gut lining weakens. And when the gut lining weakens..."
- Stack consequences before resolving: "So cortisol goes up. And immune function drops. And bad bacteria takes hold. And THAT'S when..."

**The scroll test:** After every 3-4 sentences, ask: "Would the viewer feel satisfied stopping here?" If yes, the forward pull is broken.

### Law 4: Sentence Fragment Rhythm

Spoken language uses fragments. Video scripts must use fragments to sound natural.

**Natural:** "Constant dryness. Painful intercourse. Needing lube just to get through it. And even then feeling like razor blades."

**Unnatural:** "I was experiencing constant dryness and painful intercourse. I needed lubricant just to get through intimacy, and even then it felt like razor blades."

Use fragments for symptom stacks, consequence chains, and emotional beats. Use complete sentences for mechanism education where clarity matters.

### Law 5: The "Like" and "Right?" Calibration

- Expert Educator: 0-2 casual markers per script. Warmth comes from simplicity, not verbal tics.
- Personal Discovery: 5-8 casual markers. "Like" before examples, "honestly" before confessions, "literally" before surprising results.
- Third-Party Story: 3-6 casual markers. Calibrate to the relationship.

## Belief-Shift Sequencing

### Law 6: Symptom Specificity Before Mechanism

The hook must name the symptom in the language the viewer uses IN HER OWN HEAD. Not clinical language. Not polite language. Her internal monologue.

**Strong:** "If your stomach is flat in the morning but looks six months along by dinner"
**Weak:** "Tired of digestive problems?"

**Rule:** The hook must contain at least ONE sensory detail or ONE specific behavioral detail.

### Law 7: Villain Before Mechanism

Before teaching the root cause, destroy belief in what the viewer is currently doing. This creates a vacuum that the mechanism fills. The viewer learns the mechanism THROUGH the failure of existing solutions.

**Rule:** By the time the full mechanism is explicitly explained, the viewer should have already absorbed 40-60% of it through the villain teardowns.

### Law 8: Mechanism Through Chain, Not Dump

The mechanism is delivered as a CHAIN — each link pulling the next forward through cause and effect.

"When estrogen drops → gut lining weakens → cortisol goes up → immune function drops → bad bacteria overgrows → bacteria builds biofilm → biofilm blocks nutrients → body panics → cortisol spikes higher → more fat storage → and that's the loop."

**Rule:** Every mechanism sentence must be the CONSEQUENCE of the one before it. If it's not connected by cause-and-effect, you haven't found the right link yet.

**Rule:** The chain must terminate on the exact symptom named in the hook. If the hook was stubborn belly weight, the last link is belly fat. A chain that ends on a biological abstraction has not closed.

### Law 9: The Solution Checklist Beat

Between mechanism education and product discovery, build a mental checklist of what the solution needs. Each item is the logical solution to one link in the mechanism chain. When the product appears, it's evaluated against criteria the VIEWER helped build — she nods instead of resists.

### Law 10: Product As Footnote

The product should occupy no more than 10-15% of total script word count.

**The ratio that converts:**
- Emotional setup: 15-25%
- Villain teardown: 15-20%
- Mechanism education: 25-35%
- Solution checklist: 10-15%
- Product discovery + confirmation + results + CTA: 10-15%

## Engagement Architecture Laws

Laws 1-10 govern how a script sounds and how belief moves. Laws 11-20 govern whether the viewer is still there to hear it.

### Law 11: The Curiosity Loop Stack

A curiosity loop is a question planted in the viewer's head that the script promises to answer later. The engine of a long VSL is that **loops are opened faster than they are closed.** At any moment past the first ten seconds, at least two loops must be open. When the count drops to zero, the viewer leaves — not because she's bored, but because she's finished.

**The three loop sizes:**

**Macro loops** span the whole script. There are one or two, opened in the first fifteen seconds, closed in the last third. "Here's how I went from this to this" opens a loop that only closes at "so that was me three months ago, and this is me now." Everything in between is the viewer waiting for that payoff.

**Mid loops** span thirty to ninety seconds and carry the mechanism. "In the next thirty seconds I'm gonna tell you what I found" is a mid loop. So is "but there was a problem" and "but of course, there was a catch."

**Micro loops** span one to three sentences and are the connective tissue of the mechanism section. "But why does this gut plaque matter?" "And where does this cortisol go?" These are questions the narrator asks on the viewer's behalf and answers immediately. They convert explanation into dialogue.

**The deliberate delay.** The strongest move in the whole framework: open a macro or mid loop, promise to close it, then explicitly refuse. *"But in the next thirty seconds I'm gonna tell you what I found... But before I do, you need to understand just how bad things got."* This does three things at once — it raises tension instead of releasing it, it buys another sixty seconds of runway, and it lets you place the rock-bottom emotional scene at the point of maximum attention. Use it exactly once per script. Twice reads as manipulation.

**Every loop must close.** An unclosed loop is a broken promise, and the viewer feels it as vaguely unsatisfying even if she can't name why. Before finalizing any script, run the **loop ledger**: list every question the script raises, and mark where each one is answered. Anything unmarked either gets closed or gets cut.

### Law 12: The Delayed Brand Reveal

The brand name appears in the final 10-15% of the script, never earlier. The product can be referenced anonymously long before — "a small American company," "the one from the forum," "them" — but it is not named until the viewer has already been converted by the mechanism.

**Why this works:** The moment a brand is named, the viewer reclassifies what she's watching from "a woman telling me something" to "an ad." Every second before that reclassification is a second of real attention. You are spending the viewer's trust, so spend it last.

**The corollary:** the longer the script, the later the name. In a 30-second Expert Educator script the brand can appear at the 70% mark. In a six-minute Punch-in-the-Gut VSL it should not appear before 85%.

**What replaces the brand name in the middle of the script:** the mechanism gets the proper noun instead. "Biofilms." "The Milan method." "Freeze-dried bromelain." The viewer walks away with a concept she can search for, and the concept belongs to you.

### Law 13: Twist the Knife

Every belief you want to shift gets anchored to a specific humiliating moment from the avatar's life. The pattern is always the same three beats:

1. **State the belief plainly** — the thing she currently thinks is true
2. **Attach a nightmare-state scene** — one concrete, sensory, embarrassing moment
3. **Shift the belief** — now that it costs her something, the new belief has somewhere to land

Abstract pain builds no trust. "I was frustrated with my weight" is nothing. "When my daughter touched my belly on our family holiday and asked if she was gonna be a big sister, I broke down in tears" is the entire ad.

**The knife-twist line specifically** is a subtype: you indict a villain by naming what the villain did NOT experience. *"He didn't cry in the mornings at the stupid weight not shifting or feel embarrassed to go out in anything but stretchy joggers and a baggy t-shirt."* The line is nominally about the doctor. It is actually a permission slip — the viewer is being told that crying in the morning about weight is a reasonable thing to have done. That permission is what buys the belief shift.

**Sourcing knife twists:** they come from avatar research, never invention. The right details are the ones the avatar would be embarrassed to have you know: the second charity bag of clothes, the cover-up at the pool, refusing the family photo, ordering a salad and worrying about being judged for it, unbuttoning jeans at dinner. Objects and behaviors, not feelings.

**Rule:** One knife twist per belief shift, minimum three per long VSL. They escalate — the deepest one goes immediately before or immediately after the deliberate delay in Law 11, at the emotional floor of the script.

### Law 14: Sell the Outcome, Not the Product

Before writing a word, name the one deep emotional outcome the script sells. Not the benefit. The emotional state on the other side of the benefit.

Weight loss is a benefit. *Freedom from social shame* is the outcome. Better digestion is a benefit. *Not having to plan your day around a bathroom* is the outcome.

Once named, that outcome is a filter on every line in the script. Every symptom you choose, every scene, every result — it earns its place by touching that outcome. A symptom that doesn't touch it gets cut even if it's true and even if it's relatable.

**How the outcome shows up in results:** never as a number alone. The scale number is permitted, but it is always followed by a life moment that carries the outcome. Six pounds is a number. Picking the jeans off the top of the charity bag and getting them on is freedom from shame. The husband saying "you're very smiley recently" is freedom from shame. One is data, the other is what she's actually buying.

### Law 15: Objections Handled by Story, Never by Argument

The script never argues with the viewer. Every objection gets dissolved inside the narrative, at the exact moment the viewer forms it, by having the narrator have the same objection first.

| Objection in the viewer's head | Handled as narrative |
|---|---|
| "This is another supplement scam" | "I brushed it off as I'd seen enough of this type of thing to know better." |
| "It probably won't work fast" | "And the first week, not a single thing happened." |
| "Why hasn't my doctor told me this?" | "All while my doc never even bothered to look at the new science." |
| "Why is this so expensive / hard to get?" | "The freeze drying process is tediously slow." |
| "Sounds too good to be true" | "But this isn't a magic pill." |
| "What if it doesn't work for me?" | The guarantee, stated once, as a fact, in the last two sentences. |

The rule of thumb: **if you can hear the objection being answered, it's written wrong.** A handled objection should read as a plot event, not a rebuttal. The narrator's skepticism does the work — she resists on the viewer's behalf, and when she gives in, so does the viewer.

**The strongest version** is the pre-emptive doubt (Law 2, advanced use), where the narrator voices the objection a half-second before the viewer completes the thought. This costs one sentence and buys the rest of the script.

### Law 16: The Sensory Proxy

The mechanism needs one moment where the viewer can FEEL it, physically, right now, in her own body. Not understand it. Feel it.

*"If you take your tongue right now and run it across your teeth, unless you've just brushed them, you'll feel a thin slimy plaque layer. Well, the same thing happens in the gut, only there's nothing to brush it away."*

The viewer runs her tongue over her teeth. She feels the film. The mechanism is now not a claim she has to accept — it is a sensation she just verified. That is a completely different category of belief, and it is nearly impossible to argue with.

**What makes a good sensory proxy:**
- It uses a sensation the viewer has already had a thousand times
- It requires no equipment and can be done during the ad
- The analogy is structural, not decorative — the proxy and the mechanism work the same way
- It is homely. Teeth, pineapple, a clogged drain, a wet sponge. Never elegant.

The second proxy in the same VSL does the same job for the ingredient: *"You know the tingly mouth feeling from eating too much pineapple? Well, that's bromelain, eating the bacteria slime off your tongue."* The viewer has felt bromelain work. The ingredient is now pre-validated by her own memory.

**Rule:** One sensory proxy for the problem mechanism, and where possible a second for the solution mechanism. If you can't find one, the mechanism is probably too abstract to sell and should be reframed.

### Law 17: The Recurring Symbolic Object

Pick one physical object that carries the emotional outcome, and bring it back three times across the script — once in the nightmare state, once in the social proof, once in the resolution.

The Nuora VSL uses a charity bag of clothes. She packs a second one in the nightmare state. Other women are "unpacking their charity trash bags" in the social proof. She picks the jeans off the top of the bag in the resolution.

This does what no amount of description can: it gives the viewer a single image to hold the entire transformation in, and it makes the ending feel earned rather than asserted. The bag is the before and after, in one object.

**Rule:** One object per script. It must be ordinary, ownable, and slightly sad. A bag of clothes. A drawer of jeans that don't fit. A cover-up. A photo she isn't in. Not a mirror, not a scale — those are clichés the viewer has been trained to skip past.

### Law 18: The Honest Slow Start

Results are delivered as a timeline, and the timeline begins with nothing happening.

*"And the first week, not a single thing happened. I tried to find her to send an angry reply, but thought, whatever. I'll keep going."*

This beat does more work than any result claim in the script. It inoculates against the viewer quitting in week one, it makes every result that follows more credible because the narrator has demonstrated she's willing to report failure, and it converts the guarantee window from a refund policy into a plot point.

**The escalation ladder:**
- Week 1: nothing (plus a flash of anger — she almost quit, the viewer almost will too)
- Week 2: a small, non-scale, physical change (puffiness, bloat, not blowing up after dinner)
- Week 5: the first number, framed as smaller than expected (*"not a lot, but I hadn't seen that since my thirties"*)
- Bottle 2: the symbolic object returns
- Bottle 3: someone else notices, unprompted

The last rung matters most. A result the narrator observes is a claim. A result someone else observes out loud — *"you're very smiley recently"* — is proof, and it's the one that carries the emotional outcome rather than the benefit.

**Rule:** Never claim a fast result in a category where fast results aren't believable. Slowness, stated plainly, buys more than speed does.

### Law 19: Scarcity That Comes From the Mechanism

Scarcity and urgency are permitted only when they are a consequence of something already established in the mechanism. Invented scarcity ("only 47 left!") breaks the spell the entire script has been building.

*"The only problem is the freeze drying process is tediously slow, so they don't damage the enzymes. So the batches are only available once it's all fully formulated."*

The freeze-drying was introduced two minutes earlier as the thing that makes the ingredient three times more effective. It now returns as the reason supply is limited. The scarcity is not a sales tactic; it is a cost of quality the viewer already agreed was worth paying. Then the narrator models the behavior — *"I make sure to buy a few extra just in case"* — which is a quantity upsell delivered as a personal habit.

**The urgency variant** works the same way, running the mechanism forward in time: *"every month that goes by just means a thicker biofilm, less absorption, and missing that radiant self."* Waiting has a cost, and the cost is stated in the mechanism's own vocabulary.

**Rule:** If your scarcity or urgency line could be pasted into a different product's script without editing, it's invented. Cut it and derive a real one from the mechanism.

**Reconciling this with the CTA rules:** the CTA section says urgency belongs in the overlay, not the spoken script. Mechanism-derived scarcity is the single exception. It is spoken because it functions as information about how the product is made, not as pressure — and it is placed inside the sermon beat and the close, never in the middle of the mechanism.

### Law 20: The Joe Schmo Language Ceiling

The entire script is written in the plainest language available. Not simplified — plain. There is a difference: simplified language talks down, plain language just doesn't show off.

**Hard rules:**
- No word the avatar wouldn't use in conversation, with exactly one exception: the mechanism's proper noun (biofilm, cortisol) which is introduced with a definition and an analogy the first time it appears
- No sentence that requires re-reading
- No clause stacking. If a sentence has two commas, break it
- No metaphor that requires education to land
- Grade 5 reading level for everything except the mechanism paragraph, which may reach grade 7

**The pooch test:** the script says "pooch," "belly," "puffy," "bloat." It does not say "abdominal distension," "visceral adiposity," or "midsection." Use the word she uses when she's alone and annoyed at her reflection.

**Why this is a conversion law and not a style preference:** every unit of cognitive effort spent parsing a sentence is a unit not spent feeling the emotional beat. Complexity doesn't just fail to persuade — it actively drains the emotional charge the knife twists are trying to build.

## The Villain Architecture

### Three Villain Types

**Type 1: The Structurally Incapable Solution** — Reframed as unable to reach the actual problem. "It's not that [solution] doesn't work. It's that it works on the surface while the real problem is underneath." Validates the viewer's past choices.

**Type 2: The Fake Industry** — Products with the right label but wrong ingredients. Creates a "litmus test" the viewer can verify. Positions the viewer as an insider.

**Type 3: The Outdated System** — Doctors behind the research. Standard protocols failing the patient. Aligns with the viewer's lived experience of being dismissed.

**Rule:** Every script should use at least two villain types. All three in longer scripts creates a "closed loop."

### The Failed-Research Montage

A compressed, rhythmic variant of Type 1 that belongs in the mechanism section rather than before it. A credible third party tries every obvious solution and fails, in a two-beat call-and-response:

*"Probiotics? Nope. Antibiotics? Nope. But then they found something that shocked even them."*

This does the villain teardown at solution level in nine words, pre-empts the viewer's "why not just take X" objection, and makes the real answer feel earned by someone else's money and effort. Keep it to three failures maximum, and always end on "but then."

### The Trusted Authority as Final Straw

In a Punch-in-the-Gut script, the last and worst villain is almost always someone the viewer is supposed to trust — usually a doctor. The structure: the authority gave advice, the advice caused real harm, the harm is described physically, and then the knife twist indicts the authority by what he did not feel.

Keep the target narrow: the individual's dismissiveness and the lag between research and practice, never the profession itself. "My doc never even bothered to look at the new science" is a story about one man, which is why it lands — the viewer has met that man. Widen it to doctors in general and the line stops being a memory and starts being an opinion, which is far easier to argue with.

## CTA Construction

**Rules:**
- CTA is 2-4 sentences maximum
- Must reference the mechanism concept, not just the product
- Must include a physical instruction ("tap below," "hit Shop Now," "click the link")
- Must feel casual, not urgent — with the single exception of mechanism-derived scarcity (Law 19)
- The guarantee, when used, is stated once, as a fact, with no exclamation. *"If you're not unpacking those bags of clothes within those ninety days, they give you 100 percent of your money back."* Note that it is phrased as the emotional outcome and the symbolic object, not as "if you're not satisfied"
- "You're welcome" or "I hope this helps" as a closing tag frames the entire script as a favor

### The Sermon Beat

In long VSLs, one beat sits between the results and the CTA: the narrator zooms out from her own story to the viewer's, and speaks to the group rather than the individual.

*"And I'm sharing this because menopause is such an overwhelming time with so little direction, and so many of us are doing everything right and still watching the belly grow."*

Four jobs, and it is the only beat in the script that does all four at once:

**It ties up every belief that was shifted.** The sermon is where the loosened beliefs get restated in their new form, in one place, so the viewer leaves holding a coherent position rather than a pile of moments. It is the summing-up, not new argument.

**It states why she made the video.** "I'm sharing this because" is the answer to a question the viewer has been holding since the first sentence. Answering it — she made this for other women in the same position — converts the script from a testimonial into a mission and buys a final round of trust and relatability at the exact moment the brand is about to appear.

**It returns to the deep emotional driver by name.** *"Feeling like a shadow of their former self is far from normal or something to accept with age."* This is the thesis of the entire ad, and it lands here rather than at the top because the viewer has now spent five minutes agreeing to it emotionally. Stated first, it would be a claim. Stated here, it's a conclusion she reached herself.

**It closes the macro loop.** Whatever the opening promised, this is where the promise is honored in words rather than pictures.

Keep it to four or five sentences, put the urgency line inside it, and place it before the brand name — never after.

### Soft CTA + Risk Reversal

The close is soft on purpose. After a script built entirely on trust, a hard ask breaks the frame it took five minutes to build.

The pattern is three beats and roughly three sentences:

**Soft ask.** *"So I'll drop the link below if you wanna give them a try for yourself."* Permissive, not imperative. "If you wanna" hands the decision back to the viewer, which is the only reason she believes she made it.

**Risk reversal.** The guarantee, stated once, as a fact, with no exclamation and no hype framing. Phrase it as the emotional outcome and the symbolic object rather than as satisfaction: *"If you're not unpacking those bags of clothes within those ninety days, they give you 100 percent of your money back."* Note that the ninety days matches the biology established earlier — the guarantee reads as an honest match to the mechanism rather than a return policy.

**Physical instruction.** *"If that's you, the link's below."* One short line. Conditional framing ("if that's you") keeps it a filter rather than a push, which is consistent with the ad's whole job of filtering down to buyers.

## Anti-Mimicry

When writing multiple scripts for the same product, each must use a different: FORMAT, HOOK TYPE, VILLAIN LEAD, MECHANISM ENTRY POINT, DISCOVERY VEHICLE, and EMOTIONAL ANCHOR. If even two dimensions match a previous script, rewrite.

**Punch-in-the-Gut additions to the anti-mimicry check:** SENSORY PROXY, SYMBOLIC OBJECT, and the DELAYED-LOOP PLACEMENT must also differ. Two scripts that both open a thirty-second loop and both delay it at the same point in the arc will feel like the same ad even if every other element is different — the rhythm is what the viewer recognizes.

## Pre-Writing Checklist

1. Which format? (A, B, or C)
2. Is this a Punch-in-the-Gut script? (2+ minutes, emotional avatar, Format B — if yes, work the 23-beat macro shape)
3. What is the ONE emotional outcome? (Law 14 — not the benefit, the state on the other side of it)
4. What is the hook? (Specific sensory or behavioral detail)
5. Who is the narrator? (Credential or relationship to the problem)
6. What are the villains? (Which types, in what order, which is the final straw)
7. What is the mechanism chain? (Cause → effect → cause → effect, terminating on the hook symptom)
8. What is the sensory proxy? (Law 16 — for the problem, and ideally for the solution)
9. What is the symbolic object? (Law 17 — and where do its three appearances go)
10. What are the three knife twists, and which one is the floor? (Law 13)
11. What are the solution checklist items?
12. How is the product discovered? (Never through an ad)
13. What life moment measures the result? (Not a metric. A moment. Plus one observed by someone else)
14. Where does the scarcity come from in the mechanism? (Law 19 — if it's invented, cut it)
15. Anti-mimicry check across all nine dimensions
16. **Loop ledger:** every question the script opens, and the line where each closes
17. **Read 2-3 matching transcripts from `mynuora-direct-response-video-ads` skill to internalize cadence before writing.**

## Word Count Targets

- **Short (30-60s):** 250-400 words. Expert Educator. Compressed chain. One villain. One macro loop.
- **Medium (90-120s):** 500-750 words. Any format. Full chain. Two villains minimum. One macro + two mid loops.
- **Long (2-4 min):** 800-1,400 words. Personal Discovery or Third-Party preferred. Full architecture. Punch in the Gut viable here.
- **Extended VSL (4-7 min):** 1,400-2,200 words. Punch in the Gut, Personal Discovery, deep emotional anchoring. Two macro loops, four to six mid loops, micro loops throughout the mechanism.

## Common Failures and Fixes

**Script sounds written, not spoken.** Read it out loud. Replace prose with how you'd SAY it. Add connective words. Break into fragments. Add a thinking-out-loud beat.

**Mechanism section drags.** Every sentence must advance the cause-and-effect chain. If it's not a consequence of the previous sentence, cut it. Then check for micro loops — a mechanism section without "but why does this matter?" questions is a lecture.

**Product introduction feels abrupt.** The "where do I get this" bridge is missing.

**Script feels like two scripts glued together.** The villain teardowns aren't seeding the mechanism. The viewer should learn 40-60% of the mechanism through the failures.

**CTA is too long or too salesy.** Cut to 2-4 sentences. Remove re-teaching. Remove urgency language that isn't derived from the mechanism.

**Every script sounds similar.** Run the anti-mimicry checklist across all nine dimensions.

**Viewers drop at the 40% mark.** No loop is open. Run the loop ledger — you probably closed a mid loop without opening the next one. Fix by opening the following loop one sentence BEFORE closing the current one.

**The emotional beats feel generic.** The knife twists are made of feelings instead of objects and behaviors. Go back to avatar research and find the detail she'd be embarrassed you knew.

**The mechanism is understood but not believed.** No sensory proxy. She has accepted a claim instead of verifying a sensation.

**The ending feels asserted rather than earned.** The symbolic object is missing, or appears only once. The transformation needs a physical thing to be measured against.

**It converts clicks but not buyers.** The brand appeared too early and the mechanism never got the proper noun, so the viewer classified it as an ad before the belief work landed.

## Worked Example: Nuora Gut Ritual — Menopause Belly

**Angle:** Stubborn menopause belly weight that won't shift no matter what she does
**Format:** B (Personal Discovery) under Punch-in-the-Gut architecture
**Emotional outcome:** Freedom from social shame
**Symbolic object:** The charity bag of clothes
**Sensory proxies:** Tongue on teeth (problem) / pineapple tingle (solution)
**Villains:** Type 3 (the dismissive doctor, final straw), Type 1 (diets, gym, adaptogens, the shots), Type 2 (companies that skip the biofilm step)

Annotations are in brackets. Read the transcript twice — once clean, once with the notes.

---

> Here's how I went from this to this, by fixing the hidden menopause layer docs were too stubborn to tell me about.

**[MACRO LOOP 1 OPENS.** Before/after bookend. "This to this" promises a payoff the script won't deliver for five minutes. Also plants the Type 3 villain and the mechanism's proper noun ("hidden layer") in the same breath. Brand: absent.]

> So last year's rebound had me packing a second charity bag of clothes, bringing cover ups to the pool, and literally refusing family holiday pics because my doc told me the only way I'd shift the meno weight was with skinny shots until I proved him wrong.

**[Nightmare state in three objects, not three feelings.** SYMBOLIC OBJECT, first appearance. Note "second" bag — the word doing the most work in the sentence. "Until I proved him wrong" is a mid loop opening under the sentence.]

> And for years, I followed all the typical health advice under the sun from strict diets to regular gym sessions and even ashwagandha and mushroom coffees.

**[Villain Type 1, failed solution stack.** Named specifics, not "I tried everything." Ashwagandha and mushroom coffee date her precisely into the avatar's actual world.]

> And I get that spark of hope each time expecting the scale to budge just to get a wave of embarrassment when I felt I'd been fooled.

**[The emotional outcome, stated in its own vocabulary.** Not "it didn't work" — embarrassment. Law 14 filtering the failure stack.]

> But the final straw for me was the horrors I had while on the shots my doc recommended. The nausea got so bad, I'd feel too dizzy to even safely do school pickup just for a few lousy pounds off the scale.

**[Final straw beat.** The harm is physical and specific. "School pickup" enters as a unit of measurement — it comes back later as the proof of results.]

> All while my doc never even bothered to look at the new science because why would he? He didn't cry in the mornings at the stupid weight not shifting or feel embarrassed to go out in anything but stretchy joggers and a baggy t shirt.

**[THE KNIFE TWIST.** Law 13's signature move — indict the villain by what he did NOT feel. Functionally a permission slip: crying about this is reasonable. Note it's aimed at one man's dismissiveness, not the profession.]

> But in the next thirty seconds, I'm gonna tell you what I found that took me from being ashamed to be in my swimsuit among friends to actually buying even cuter ones. And it uses the same science that's helping thousands of other women quietly reclaim themselves again. So let me show you how.

**[MID LOOP 2 OPENS** — an explicit thirty-second promise. Social proof arrives before the product exists. "Quietly reclaim themselves" is the emotional outcome again.]

> But before I do, you need to understand just how bad things got.

**[THE DELIBERATE DELAY.** Law 11's strongest move. The promise is made and immediately withheld. Tension goes up instead of down, and it buys runway for the emotional floor. Used exactly once.]

> Because it wasn't just the pooch for me. I had the moon face and belly bloat that had gotten to the point I'd have second thoughts even ordering a salad out. And when my daughter touched my belly on our family holiday and asked if she was gonna be a big sister, I broke down in tears.

**[EMOTIONAL FLOOR.** The deepest knife twist, placed at maximum attention thanks to the delay. Note "pooch" and "moon face" — Law 20's plain-language ceiling. The daughter line is the whole ad in one sentence.]

> So, of course, I didn't hear this from a doctor, but a five minute reply from a gut practitioner on a menopause health forum.

**[DISCOVERY VEHICLE.** Non-authority source, which is what makes it credible to an avatar who has been failed by authority. "Five minute reply" makes it feel found, not sold. Callback to villain Type 3.]

> She explained it like this. If you take your tongue right now and run it across your teeth, unless you've just brushed them, you'll feel a thin slimy plaque layer. Well, the same thing happens in the gut, only there's nothing to brush it away.

**[SENSORY PROXY #1.** Law 16. The viewer verifies the mechanism with her own tongue during the ad. This is the single highest-leverage paragraph in the script.]

> But why does this gut plaque matter?

**[MICRO LOOP.** The narrator asks the viewer's question for her. Converts lecture into dialogue.]

> Well, because estrogen helps the good bacterial plaque in the gut. But when it declines after menopause, this layer dies off, and the bad bacteria that would usually get brushed out by the good start to build up to form what gut researchers call biofilms.

**[The mechanism gets the proper noun instead of the brand.** Law 12. "Biofilm" is what she'll search for, and it belongs to us.]

> So it thickens over the years and starts to block absorption and inflame the gut. Vitamins can't get through, minerals can't get through, and the good stuff from food starts bouncing off. So before you know it, the body's raising the cortisol alarms in a panic.

**[Chain, not dump.** Law 8. Every sentence is the consequence of the last. Note the triple repetition of "can't get through / bouncing off" — spoken rhythm doing the work of emphasis.]

> And where does this cortisol go? Well, the place with four times four cortisol receptors than anywhere else in the body, the belly.

**[MICRO LOOP, and the chain terminates on the hook symptom.** Law 8's second rule. The abstraction lands on the exact thing she complained about in sentence one.]

> So my mind started racing. Like, was this why the pooch wouldn't budge? Why I felt so exhausted by pickup and puffy even in the mornings? How do I stop it?

**[Thinking-out-loud beat + retroactive symptom capture.** Law 2. The mechanism now explains symptoms she recognizes but hadn't connected. "Pickup" returns as the unit of measurement. "How do I stop it?" opens the next mid loop.]

> She explained that a research team in Milan wanted to answer the same question. So they invested millions, flying in researchers from around the world to test everything they could against these biofilms. Probiotics? Nope. Antibiotics? Nope. But then they found something that shocked even them.

**[FAILED-RESEARCH MONTAGE.** Villain Type 1 at solution level, in nine words. Pre-empts "why not just take probiotics." Ends on "but then" — the loop stays open through the reveal.]

> You know the tingly mouth feeling from eating too much pineapple? Well, that's bromelain, eating the bacteria slime off your tongue, and this same friendly enzyme breaks down biofilm slime in the gut.

**[SENSORY PROXY #2.** Law 16 applied to the solution. She's already felt bromelain work. The ingredient arrives pre-validated by her own memory.]

> But there was a problem. Women would have to eat five pineapples a week to get that kind of effect.

**[MID LOOP.** The constraint that creates the product's reason to exist. Without it, the viewer just buys pineapple.]

> So they went back to the research, testing ways to make it more potent without killing this little guy. Before finding, when they slowly freeze dried the enzymes before packaging, it made them three times as effective at breaking the biofilms. And once they dissolve, the gut starts to happily absorb again and inflammation drops.

**[Freeze-drying is planted here as a quality mechanism.** It returns at the end as the source of scarcity. Law 19 — the scarcity is being set up two minutes early so it doesn't read as a tactic.]

> I was thinking, well, that's great for the puffiness and bloat, but what about my pooch?

**[PRE-EMPTIVE OBJECTION.** Law 15 + Law 2 advanced. The viewer's exact doubt, voiced a half-second before she finishes forming it.]

> But she explained, pairing it with some berberine is the missing key, and it's where most companies go wrong. You see, many women have heard berberine is known as the poor man's skinny shot, and for good reason. It kick starts metabolism and helps regrow good gut bacteria. So most companies skip the biofilm absorption part entirely as it's not easy to freeze dry and costs them more.

**[VILLAIN TYPE 2** — right label, wrong formulation. Gives the viewer a litmus test and makes her an insider. "Poor man's skinny shot" is doing heavy lifting: it borrows an ingredient the avatar already half-believes in, then reframes it as necessary but incomplete, so the pairing feels like the missing piece rather than a new claim.]

> But the women that know this and stick to the science are unpacking their charity trash bags, slipping into summer bikinis, and are the ones asking for those precious moments to be —

**[SYMBOLIC OBJECT, second appearance.** Law 17. Social proof rendered as the object rather than as a testimonial. The sentence is cut off mid-thought, which is itself a micro loop.]

> She sent a link to a small American company.

**[Product exists. Still unnamed.** Law 12 holding at roughly the 60% mark.]

> But, of course, there was a catch. She said they combined the Milan research method with the highest quality sun dried berberine. But this isn't a magic pill. Biofilms thicken over years, and the problems show themselves way later than that. So women who often start seeing the bloat and puffiness drop before it's fully swept out and the gut's rebalanced, it's the women sticking to at least ninety days that see the lasting results.

**[Expectation-setting as objection handling.** Law 15. "This isn't a magic pill" is a credibility purchase. The ninety-day frame is set up here so the guarantee at the end reads as a match to the biology rather than a return policy.]

> I brushed it off as I'd seen enough of this type of thing to know better. But the science made sense, so I bought them.

**[Narrator skepticism.** Law 15 — she resists on the viewer's behalf, then gives in for a stated reason. Two sentences, no argument.]

> And the first week, not a single thing happened. I tried to find her to send an angry reply, but thought, whatever. I'll keep going.

**[THE HONEST SLOW START.** Law 18. The most valuable eight seconds in the script — it inoculates against week-one quitting and proves the narrator will report failure.]

> Then week two, my hands weren't as puffy in the morning, and I didn't blow up from my husband's pasta bake. And the real test was the weight, but I knew to be patient. Around the five week mark, I almost fell off the scale when I saw it had dropped six pounds. Not a lot, but I hadn't seen that since my thirties, so I kept going.

**[Escalation ladder, rungs 2 and 3.** Law 18. The number is deliberately underplayed — "not a lot" is more persuasive than a bigger figure would be.]

> By my second bottle, I picked off the jeans at the top of the trash bag. They slipped on tight, but my god. I hadn't worn them in years.

**[SYMBOLIC OBJECT, third appearance.** Law 17 closing. The bag she packed in beat two is now being unpacked. "Tight" keeps it honest.]

> It was my third bottle that my husband said, you're very smiley recently, and I was. But it was like I'd found myself again. It was always me, but just slipping into the old clothes I loved and enjoying meals again, it felt so freeing.

**[Top rung: the result someone ELSE observes, unprompted.** Law 18. And it's an emotional-outcome observation, not a physical one — which is exactly what she's buying.]

> So that was me three months ago, and this is me now. The pooch that made me look pregnant is gone. The bloating that had me unbuttoning jeans by dinner has calmed down. And I don't know if it's because my gut is less inflamed now or the cortisol that finally came down, but I feel clearer, like I actually have energy past school pickup.

**[MACRO LOOP 1 CLOSES.** The bookend from sentence one pays off. "Energy past school pickup" closes the callback planted in the final-straw beat. "I don't know if it's because" is a credibility move — certainty here would sound scripted.]

> And I'm sharing this because menopause is such an overwhelming time with so little direction, and so many of us are doing everything right and still watching the belly grow and the unchanged scales staring back month after month.

**[THE SERMON BEAT.** Zoom from "me" to "us." She states that she made this for other women in the same position, which builds a final round of trust and relatability right before the brand appears — and answers the question the viewer has been holding since sentence one.]

> Because every month that goes by just means a thicker biofilm, less absorption, and missing that radiant self.

**[MECHANISM-DERIVED URGENCY.** Law 19. The cost of waiting stated in the mechanism's own vocabulary. Could not be pasted into another product's script.]

> And I know so many women out there like me who know deep down that feeling like shadow of their former self is far from normal or something to accept with age. But knowing what's actually happening, like what's really going on with the gut and the impact it has on both our hormones and body, made the world of difference for me.

**[Every shifted belief tied up in one place, and the deep emotional driver named directly.** "Feeling like a shadow of their former self is far from normal or something to accept with age" is the thesis of the whole ad, and it arrives at 88% — after the viewer has already agreed to it emotionally. Stated at the top it would have been a claim she could argue with. Here it's a conclusion she reached herself. This is also where the macro loop finishes closing in words.]

> So the company from the forum, the one I used, is called Nuora Gut Ritual. It's two capsules a day on an empty stomach in the morning.

**[BRAND NAMED.** Law 12 — roughly 90% through. By withholding it this long the whole script has felt less salesy, and the viewer has been allowed to arrive at the point of sale herself rather than being delivered to it. Dosage immediately after, because a viewer who has already decided wants logistics, not more selling.]

> The only problem is the freeze drying process is tediously slow, so they don't damage the enzymes. So the batches are only available once it's all fully formulated. I've been lucky so far, but I make sure to buy a few extra just in case I don't run out whilst waiting for the next batch.

**[MECHANISM-DERIVED SCARCITY** paying off the freeze-drying plant from two minutes earlier. Then the quantity upsell delivered as a personal habit rather than an instruction.]

> So I'll drop the link below if you wanna give them a try for yourself. Because if you're not unpacking those bags of clothes within those ninety days, they give you 100 percent of your money back. If that's you, the link's below.

**[SOFT CTA + RISK REVERSAL, three sentences.** "If you wanna" is permissive, not imperative — it hands the decision back, which is the only reason she believes she made it. The guarantee is stated once, as fact, phrased as the emotional outcome and the symbolic object — "unpacking those bags of clothes" — rather than "if you're not satisfied." Ninety days matches the biology set up earlier, so the risk reversal reads as honest rather than promotional. "If that's you" keeps the close a filter rather than a push.]

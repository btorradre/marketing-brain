## AD COPY

**Headline:** She spent thirty-four hundred dollars on her face before a blood test showed what was actually wrong

I've been in dermatology for nineteen years. I've treated rosacea, cystic acne, melanoma, psoriasis — the full diagnostic range. I've seen thousands of patients. And in the last eighteen months, I've started seeing something I was never trained for.

Women walk into my office with skin that looks like it aged a decade in under a year. Not from sun damage. Not from a disease process. Not from anything in my textbooks. They've all lost significant weight. They're all on the same class of medication. And they've all already spent hundreds, sometimes thousands of dollars on products that did nothing.

I'm going to tell you about Claire because her case is the one that made me stop treating the skin and start looking underneath it.

Claire is fifty-five. She came to me as a referral from a friend. She'd been on Wegovy for nine months and had lost forty-seven pounds, which her primary care doctor was thrilled about. Her A1C was down. Her blood pressure was down. By every standard measure, the medication was a success.

But Claire wasn't in my office about her blood pressure. She was in my office because of a birthday photo.

March fourteenth. Her fifty-fifth birthday. Her daughter took a photo of her blowing out candles at the Italian place they go every year. Same restaurant. Same table by the window. Same daughter holding the phone at the same angle she's used every birthday since phones had cameras.

Claire looked at the photo and put her phone face-down on the table. She didn't post it. She didn't save it. She finished her dinner without bringing it up.

That night, alone in her bathroom, she held that photo next to last year's birthday photo. Same lighting. Same angle. Same smile. The woman in this year's photo looked ten years older.

Not from the weight loss itself. She expected some hollowing. She'd Googled it. She knew. This was different. Her skin had a quality she described to me as given up. Like her skin had stopped trying. The texture was papery. When she pinched the back of her hand, it stayed tented for a full second before flattening. Her pores looked larger. Her under-eye skin was translucent enough to show veins that looked bruised. Fine lines around her mouth that she'd had since her forties had deepened into creases that caught her foundation.

Before she came to me, Claire did what most of my patients do. She went to a department store counter. Told the woman her skin was aging rapidly. Walked out with a hyaluronic acid serum, a peptide cream, a vitamin C brightening treatment, and a night mask. Three hundred and twelve dollars.

She used them religiously for six weeks. No change. If anything, the serum sat on top of her skin instead of sinking in.

She tried collagen peptides in her coffee for two months. Nothing. Retinol — her skin reacted, red and peeling, and she stopped after a week. She booked a microneedling session at a hundred and seventy-five dollars. Her face was raw for three days. The texture improved slightly for about ten days, then returned to exactly where it was. She tried it again. Same result. She bought an LED mask online. Got a brightening facial at a med spa.

By the time she sat in my exam chair, she was at thirty-four hundred dollars spent. And her skin still looked like it had quit.

I examined her under a magnified lamp. Then I asked her about her diet. Not her skincare routine. Her diet.

She told me honestly. Eight hundred to nine hundred calories a day. Sometimes less. The Wegovy suppressed her appetite so effectively that she often forgot to eat until two or three in the afternoon, at which point she'd have a small salad or a protein shake and call it done.

I ordered a micronutrient panel. Not the standard CBC her primary care doctor runs. A panel that measures the specific vitamins and minerals that don't show up on routine bloodwork. Zinc. Selenium. Vitamin A. Vitamin C. Vitamin K. Folate. The ones nobody tests unless someone specifically asks.

Results came back in five days. I turned the monitor so Claire could see it.

Her vitamin A was below normal range. Her vitamin C was at the very bottom of normal. Zinc was low. Folate was low. Vitamin K was below reference range. B6 was borderline.

And that's when I explained what was happening. Not to her skin. To her body.

Your skin is an organ. The largest one you have. It regenerates constantly. Every twenty-seven days, you grow an entirely new layer of skin. That process requires raw materials.

Vitamin A drives cell turnover. Without enough of it, your skin cannot shed old cells and produce new ones at a normal rate. That's the dullness. Claire's skin was covered in cells that should have been replaced weeks ago.

Vitamin C is required for collagen synthesis. Not the collagen you drink in a supplement. The collagen your body builds from scratch in the dermis every day. Without adequate vitamin C, that synthesis slows. The skin gets thinner. It loses structural integrity. That's why the pinch test stays tented. That's why the fine lines deepened. The scaffolding underneath was collapsing.

Vitamin K regulates blood clotting at the capillary level. When it's low, you get that bruised, translucent look under the eyes. That's not fatigue. That's micro-hemorrhaging in tiny vessels under skin that's become too thin to hide it.

Claire asked why. Why are all of these low at the same time.

I told her: your body is in triage mode.

When you eat eight hundred calories a day, your body does not have enough incoming nutrition to support every system at full capacity. So it prioritizes. Heart. Brain. Liver. Kidneys. Lungs. These get funded first. Then, if there's anything left over, it sends resources to the systems that aren't immediately life-sustaining.

Your skin is at the bottom of that priority list. Not because it's unimportant. Because you can survive without healthy skin far longer than you can survive without a functioning heart. When your body is rationing, skin is the first department that gets its budget cut.

And the problem compounds. The medication slows gastric motility. The stomach is moving food through more slowly. Which means even the eight hundred calories Claire is eating aren't being fully processed. Nutrients that require stomach acid and mechanical breakdown to absorb are getting shortchanged. So it's a double hit. Less coming in. Less being extracted from what does come in.

No topical product can fix this. And I told Claire exactly why, one by one.

The hyaluronic acid serum she bought hydrates the surface. But her skin can't turn over the dead cells sitting on top of it because it doesn't have the vitamin A to drive that process. Hydrating dead cells is not the same as growing new ones.

The peptide cream signals the body to produce collagen. But signaling doesn't help when the body doesn't have the vitamin C to actually build collagen. It's like sending a work order to a construction site that has no materials.

The collagen peptides she mixed into her coffee get broken down during digestion into amino acids. They don't arrive at the skin as collagen. And more importantly, they don't address why her body stopped building its own.

The retinol forces cell turnover. But it's burning through vitamin A reserves that are already depleted. That's why her skin reacted so violently. The retinol was demanding turnover her body couldn't sustain.

The microneedling creates micro-wounds to trigger a healing response. Healing requires nutrients. Her body doesn't have them. The ten-day improvement she saw was the body burning through its last reserves to repair the deliberate damage, then returning to baseline because the reserves were gone.

Thirty-four hundred dollars spent on products that were all, in their own way, asking her skin to do something it didn't have the raw materials to do.

I told Claire she needed to address the absorption problem. Not the skin. Not the surface. The stomach.

I told her what any intervention would need to do — and why most of what's available couldn't do it.

First, it would need to restore gastric motility. Not force it, the way a stimulant laxative forces the colon. Gently encourage the stomach muscles to contract again so food actually gets processed and nutrients get extracted. A natural prokinetic. I'd been reading about apigenin — a compound concentrated in celery — and its effect on smooth muscle contraction in the stomach wall. The research was promising. That was the motility piece.

Second, it would need to address the sulfur gas directly. Claire had been having sulfur burps for months and was mortified by them. Charcoal capsules absorb gas broadly in the lower GI tract, but the sulfur is being produced in the stomach from fermentation. By the time charcoal gets there, it's already too late. Chlorophyll neutralizes sulfur compounds at the source. Different mechanism, different location. That was the gas piece.

Third, it would need to deliver the specific depleted vitamins — A, C, K, B6, and folate — in a form that doesn't require robust stomach acid to break down. A standard multivitamin tablet lands in a sluggish GLP-1 stomach and sits there half-absorbed. That's why Claire's levels were still low even though she'd been taking a daily multi for months. The delivery format matters as much as the ingredients. Something that starts dissolving immediately — a gummy, a sublingual, something that doesn't depend on the stomach doing work it's not doing.

I'd been thinking about all three of these as separate requirements. Motility. Sulfur neutralization. Targeted micronutrients in an absorbable form. Three things that would need to work together. And the more patients I saw with Claire's pattern, the more I realized nothing on the market addressed all three from the same source.

A standard multivitamin handles the vitamins but misses the motility entirely. A probiotic handles the gut bacteria but doesn't wake up the stomach muscles or neutralize sulfur. A fiber supplement adds bulk to a system that can't move — making it worse, not better. A chlorophyll supplement addresses the gas but does nothing for the absorption problem underneath.

I kept looking for something that combined all three. A colleague of mine who works specifically with GLP-1 patients mentioned something she'd been recommending for about six months. A gummy. Not a multivitamin. Not a fiber supplement. Something designed around the actual mechanism of what these medications do to the stomach.

It had the celery juice extract with the apigenin — the prokinetic piece. It had the chlorophyll — the sulfur neutralization piece. It had a micro-dose of prebiotic fiber that feeds beneficial bacteria without overwhelming a slow system. And it had the exact vitamins Claire's panel showed were depleted. Pectin-based, so it dissolves the moment you chew it. Doesn't sit in a sluggish stomach waiting to be broken down.

It was called Motilli. I wrote the name on a prescription pad, which Claire thought was funny. I write it down because when I just say the name, patients forget it by the time they reach the parking lot.

Claire ordered it that night.

The first thing she noticed was not her skin. It was the sulfur burps. Gone within a week. The low-grade nausea she'd been living with, which she'd accepted as a permanent Wegovy side effect, lifted. She could eat a meal without that heavy, sitting-in-concrete feeling. Things were moving.

At three weeks, she told me the skin under her fingers felt different when she washed her face. Not visibly different yet. But the texture was changing. Less papery. More give. She did the pinch test on the back of her hand. It snapped back faster.

At six weeks, she took a photo in the same bathroom light where she'd stood on her birthday staring at those two pictures. She put it next to the birthday photo. The dullness was lifting. Her skin had color. The translucent under-eye quality was fading.

At three months, her daughter took a photo of her at a family barbecue and Claire posted it. First photo she'd posted in almost a year. Someone in her book club commented you're glowing and Claire told me she almost laughed because six months ago that was the last word anyone would have used.

I ran the panel again. Vitamin A back in normal range. Vitamin C mid-range. Zinc up. Folate normal. Vitamin K still lagging but trending up.

Here's what I tell my patients now, every one of them who comes in on a GLP-1 with skin that looks like it aged overnight. I tell them what I told Claire.

The skin was never the problem. The skin was the messenger. It was telling you something was wrong at a deeper level. And everything you threw at it topically was trying to shut the messenger up instead of reading the message.

If your mirror has been telling you something is wrong since you started your medication, listen to it. Your skin is not failing you. It's talking to you. It's telling you it's hungry.

And no serum, no cream, no facial, no LED mask is going to feed it from the outside.

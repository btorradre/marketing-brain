## AD COPY

**Headline:** She accepted digestive agony as the price of losing weight until December

I almost cancelled my Wegovy prescription from my bathroom floor at midnight.

Not almost like I thought about it. Almost like I had the app open. I had my thumb over the button. Cancel next shipment. Right there on the screen, bright enough to light up the tile I was sitting on.

I was on that floor for thirty-five minutes. Eleven at night on a Thursday. Cold tile. Pajama pants. The kind of pain where you can feel your heartbeat in your abdomen because everything is so swollen and pressurized that your pulse has nowhere to go but up against the walls of your own stomach.

I was crying. Not the kind where you're sad. The kind where your body just does it because it doesn't know what else to do with the pain.

And I stared at that cancel button for two full minutes.

Then I closed the app.

Because the hunger was worse.

I need to say that again because I need you to understand what I chose. I was sitting on a bathroom floor at midnight, tears on my face, stomach so distended I looked six months pregnant, in enough pain that I couldn't stand up. And I chose to stay on the medication. I chose this. Not because the pain wasn't real. Not because I'm brave or tough or disciplined. Because I remember what it was like before the shot.

I remember standing in front of the refrigerator at nine o'clock at night unable to think about anything except what I was going to eat next. I remember the noise. That's what I call it. The food noise. This constant broadcast running in my head from the moment I woke up. What's for breakfast. When's lunch. Is there cheese in the fridge. Should I eat now or wait. If I wait will I eat more later. If I eat now will I hate myself. The noise was louder than everything else in my life. Louder than my daughter's voice. Louder than my own thoughts. I planned my days around food and then I punished myself after every single meal for forty years.

The shot turned it off. Like someone found the switch and flipped it. Quiet. For the first time in my adult life, quiet.

I lost fourteen pounds the first month. I cried in a dressing room. I put on jeans I hadn't worn in four years and they buttoned and I stood there with my hand over my mouth because I didn't recognize the woman in the mirror. Not because she was thin. Because she was calm. She wasn't thinking about food. She was just standing there. Being a person.

I would do almost anything to keep that quiet.

So when the side effects started around week six, I made a deal. I didn't call it a deal at the time. I just accepted it. The way you accept weather. This is what it costs.

Seven days without a bowel movement. Then eight. Then I stopped counting because counting was making me want to scream.

Sulfur burps so bad my daughter walked into the living room one evening and said from across the room, not even close to me, she said "Mom, is something rotting in here?" She wasn't being mean. She was being honest. And I wanted to disappear into the couch cushions.

Nausea that showed up on its own schedule. Sometimes at breakfast. Sometimes right after dinner. Sometimes at three in the morning for no reason at all, just my stomach deciding that right now, in the dark, while I'm trying to sleep, is a good time to make me wonder if I'm going to throw up.

Bloating that made me look pregnant. I'm fifty-seven years old. I haven't been pregnant in decades. But there I was, turning sideways in the bathroom mirror, watching my stomach press against shirts that fit everywhere else because I was losing weight everywhere except the place where everything was trapped.

And I accepted all of it. As the price.

That's the part nobody talks about. Not the side effects. Everybody talks about those. Every GLP-1 forum, every Facebook group, every TikTok comment section is women listing their symptoms like casualties. What nobody talks about is the deal. The quiet deal you make with yourself at eleven at night when you're on the bathroom floor and you close the cancel button because the alternative is going back to the noise. You accept that you've traded one prison for another. And this one is slightly more bearable because at least the scale is going down.

I made that deal in October.

By November I had a system. Miralax every night. Mag07 before bed. Dulcolax when it got really bad. Ginger chews for the nausea. Gas-X for bloating that the Gas-X did nothing for. I had a little basket on my bathroom counter. Everything lined up. My husband looked at it one morning and called it the pharmacy. I didn't laugh because it wasn't funny. It was evidence of what I'd agreed to.

None of it worked the way it was supposed to. The Miralax would eventually produce something but the cramping made me curl up on the bed. Mag07 was a roulette wheel. Some nights nothing. Some nights I didn't make it to the bathroom in time. Dulcolax felt like my insides were being wrung out like a washcloth. And the bloating never budged. Not once. Not from any of it.

I spent somewhere around two hundred dollars on that basket between October and December.

The worst part was Christmas week. Four days of family. My daughter home from out of state. Meals I couldn't enjoy because everything I ate made the pressure worse. I sat at the Christmas dinner table and ate four bites of turkey and my mother-in-law asked if I was feeling okay and I said I was fine and went to the bathroom and sat on the edge of the tub with my hands on my stomach waiting for the wave to pass.

I went back to the table and smiled. Because that's what you do when you're paying the price and nobody else can know what the price is.

The day after Christmas I was so angry I couldn't sleep.

Not sad. Angry. Furious at the basket on my counter that represented three months of throwing money at a wall. Furious that my doctor told me the side effects were normal and to give it time and it had been four months and nothing was better. Furious that I was losing weight and supposed to feel grateful and instead I was sitting up in bed at one in the morning with heartburn and bloating and this low-grade nausea that never fully went away.

I grabbed my laptop off the nightstand and started searching. Not for products. I was done with products. I was searching for someone to explain to me why nothing was working. Why every single thing in that basket, every single thing my doctor suggested, every single thing women in the forums said helped them, why none of it helped me.

I ended up in a private Facebook group for women over fifty on GLP-1 medications. And there was a post from a woman who said she was a retired pharmacist. Long post. Detailed. The kind of post where you can tell the person actually knows what they're talking about because they're not selling anything, they're just explaining.

She said something that stopped me cold.

She said: you're all treating the last link in the chain. The problem is the first link.

She explained it simply. The medication slows your stomach. That's the mechanism. That's how it kills your appetite. It slows gastric emptying so food sits in your stomach longer and you feel full. That's the part that makes the shot work for weight loss.

But when your stomach slows down that much, the muscles that push food forward start to weaken. Food sits. It ferments. That's where the sulfur burps come from. Bacteria feeding on stagnant food, producing hydrogen sulfide gas. And everything downstream backs up because nothing upstream is moving. The small intestine backs up. The colon backs up. The whole system is a chain and the first link stopped.

Then she said the thing that made me put my laptop down and stare at the ceiling.

She said every product women are taking for this, Miralax, Dulcolax, Mag07, fiber, all of it, is designed to work on the colon. The last link. Downstream. But the problem isn't downstream. The problem is in the stomach. Upstream. She said imagine a highway where a bridge collapsed at mile marker one. Every mile after that is gridlocked. And everyone is down at mile marker thirty trying to clear traffic. You can send a hundred tow trucks to mile marker thirty. Nothing moves until you fix the bridge.

I stared at my bathroom basket the next morning and realized I had a counter full of tow trucks.

That's why the Miralax cramped but didn't fix anything. It was pulling water into my colon. My colon wasn't the problem. That's why the fiber made me more bloated. It was adding bulk to a system that couldn't move what was already there. That's why the Gas-X was useless. The gas wasn't being produced in my intestines. It was being produced in my stomach where food was sitting and rotting. Every single product was aimed at the wrong part of my body.

The pharmacist said what was needed was something that wakes up the stomach muscles themselves. She used the word prokinetic. Something that stimulates gastric motility. Not colonic motility. Not downstream. Upstream. The bridge.

She talked about a compound called apigenin. Found in celery. Research showing it promotes the stomach muscles to start contracting again. Not violently like a laxative. Gently. Like reminding the system it's supposed to be moving.

She also mentioned chlorophyll for the sulfur burps. Different mechanism entirely. Chlorophyll binds to the hydrogen sulfide compounds and neutralizes them. Doesn't mask the smell. Eliminates the chemistry producing it.

And she mentioned a small amount of prebiotic fiber. Not the kind of fiber dose you get from Metamucil that adds bulk to a stalled system. A micro-dose. Just enough to support good bacteria without adding to the backup.

Three things. Upstream motility. Sulfur neutralization. Gentle bacterial support.

I read that post three times. Then I went looking for those three things together because the pharmacist said that's what mattered. Not one of them alone. All three. Working on the stomach. Not the colon.

And I couldn't find them together. I looked at celery supplements. No chlorophyll. I looked at chlorophyll drops. No prokinetic compounds. I looked at prebiotic blends. Giant fiber doses that would make everything worse. Nothing combined all three and nothing was designed for women on GLP-1 medications specifically.

I was still scrolling at three in the morning. I'd moved from the pharmacist's post to searching for apigenin dosing studies because at that point I was considering buying the ingredients separately and figuring it out myself. And somewhere in the middle of those search results a small company's product page loaded. Celery juice fiber gummies. I almost scrolled past it because I wasn't looking for a product. I was looking for dosing information.

But the ingredient panel stopped me. Celery juice extract with apigenin. Chlorophyll. Prebiotic fiber in a small dose. All three. In one gummy. Made specifically for GLP-1 digestive issues.

I did not believe it would work. I want to be clear about that. I was not a hopeful woman in late December. I was an angry woman with a basket full of failures on her bathroom counter and four months of broken promises behind her. I had Miralax promising gentle relief. Mag07 promising overnight results. Dulcolax promising predictable movements. None of them delivered because none of them were built for what was actually wrong with me.

But the pharmacist's explanation was too precise to ignore. The upstream logic. The bridge at mile marker one. It explained every failure. Every morning sitting on the toilet willing something to happen. Every night on the bathroom floor.

The company was called Motilli. Clean label certified. Third-party tested. Made in the US. Pectin-based, not gelatin, which mattered because gelatin gummies were making my nausea worse and I hadn't even connected those until I read it on their page.

I ordered it because I had nothing left to lose except another twenty-four dollars.

The pharmacist said give it six weeks. I gave it skepticism from the first gummy.

Week one. The sulfur burps dropped. Not gone. But the frequency cut in half. I noticed this before anything else because the burps were the thing that humiliated me most. By day nine my daughter was in the room with me for two hours and didn't mention a smell. I didn't say anything. I just noticed.

Week two. Something shifted. I don't know how to describe it except to say I felt movement in my abdomen. Something happening that hadn't been happening. Like a machine that's been off for months humming back to life. And then I went to the bathroom and it was the most unremarkable, normal experience I'd had since September. I almost called my daughter to tell her and then realized that's insane, you don't call your daughter to tell her you went to the bathroom. But that's how far gone I was. Normal felt like a miracle.

Week four. I was going almost every day. Not strained. Not forced. Not after a laxative or a prayer. Just happening. The way it used to happen before the shot. The bloating was softening. Not gone but softening. I could button my work pants without the pressure making me wince.

I looked at the basket on my bathroom counter one morning in February. The Miralax was half full. The Dulcolax was expired. I hadn't opened the Gas-X in three weeks. I picked up the Mag07 bottle and it was dusty.

I threw the basket away. Not the products. The basket. I put the leftover bottles in a bag under the sink and I threw the basket in the trash because I didn't want to look at the evidence of what I'd accepted as normal.

I'm writing this in March. I'm still on Wegovy. I'm still losing weight. My stomach is not perfect every single day but it is so far from the floor at midnight that I can barely connect the woman I am now to the woman who sat on that tile with her thumb on the cancel button.

That deal I made in October. The one where I accepted that digestive misery was the price of silence from the food noise. The one where I agreed to trade one prison for another because at least this prison came with weight loss.

I cancelled that deal. Not by cancelling my prescription. By fixing the part of my system the medication was actually disrupting. Upstream. The bridge.

I need the woman on the bathroom floor right now to hear this. You don't have to make that deal. You don't have to choose between your body and your sanity. Those are not the only two options. I thought they were. For four months I was sure they were. I sat on cold tile and chose pain because I believed the alternative was worse.

The alternative was never the only alternative.

I sat on that floor five months ago choosing between two kinds of suffering. Convinced that was all there was.

I don't sit on the floor anymore.

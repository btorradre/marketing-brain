## DIMENSION 1: BELIEF-SHIFT ARCHITECTURE (19/25)

### Belief Shift 1 -- "My suffering is real": 4/5

**Evidence:**
- Mirror moment 1: "she was sitting in her living room on a Tuesday evening. Television on. Her husband next to her. A show they'd watched a hundred times. Nothing stressful. Nothing triggering. And her heart started pounding."
- Mirror moment 2: "she woke up at three in the morning in absolute terror. No nightmare. No noise. Just a bolt of adrenaline that ripped her out of sleep like someone had grabbed her by the chest. She was soaked. Her heart hammering. Shaking so hard she couldn't hold a glass of water"
- Mirror moment 3: "She sat in her car in the pharmacy parking lot and cried for twenty minutes."
- Identity line: "She is, by her own description, the person other people call when things fall apart. She does not have a history of anxiety. She has a history of holding everyone else together."

**Assessment:** Three strong mirror moments with genuine sensory specificity -- the Tuesday evening, the 3 AM bolt, the parking lot. The identity line is particularly strong because it pre-installs who Denise IS before showing her crumble, which amplifies the wrongness of what's happening. Docked from 5 because all three scenes, while specific in detail, follow the same emotional register. There is no oscillation. Each scene is terror-despair-helplessness. A 5 would show at least one moment of anger, dark humor, or numb resignation to break the pattern. The emotional tone is consistent to the point of being slightly predictable after the second scene.

### Belief Shift 2 -- "My approach is wrong about something": 4/5

**Evidence:**
- ER doctor scene 1: "The ER doctor said she'd had a panic attack. Prescribed Ativan. Sent her home at two in the morning."
- ER doctor scene 2: "A different ER doctor asked if she'd been under unusual stress. She said no. Asked if she had a history of anxiety. She said no. Asked about recent medication changes. She mentioned stopping Mounjaro. He nodded but didn't connect it to anything. Prescribed Xanax."
- Psychiatrist: "The psychiatrist diagnosed generalized anxiety disorder. Likely triggered by hormonal and metabolic shifts from significant weight loss. Started her on an SSRI."
- Narrator reframe (through professional authority): "Every doctor you saw looked at the organ they were trained to look at. The ER looked at your heart. The psychiatrist looked at your brain. Nobody looked at your gut."

**Assessment:** The doctor scenes work through witnessing -- the reader watches each doctor fail. The ER doctor nodding but not connecting the medication change is a strong detail. The psychiatrist diagnosis of "hormonal and metabolic shifts from significant weight loss" rings true as the kind of plausible-sounding wrong answer a real psychiatrist would give. Docked from 5 because of the narrator's reframe line "Every doctor you saw looked at the organ they were trained to look at" -- this is editorializing. A 5 would let Denise reach that conclusion herself, or let the reader reach it silently. The line is effective but it TELLS the reader what to think about the doctors instead of letting the accumulated scenes do all the work.

### Belief Shift 3 -- "The problem is in my gut, not my brain": 4/5

**Evidence:**
- Cause chain delivered through Dr. Ramos explaining to Denise in her office: "Your gut produces roughly ninety-five percent of your body's serotonin. Not your brain. Your gut."
- Scaffolding metaphor: "It's like pulling the supports out of a bridge before the concrete sets."

- Villain teardown (SSRI): "SSRIs try to increase serotonin activity in the brain. But your brain isn't the problem... The SSRI is trying to boost a signal in the wrong building."

**Assessment:** The cause-and-effect chain is clean and retellable. No named frameworks. The villain teardowns are tight -- "noise-canceling headphones" for Ativan and "wrong building" for SSRIs are both one-concept dismantlings that reference the cause directly. However, the mechanism section carries SOME emotional weight when it shouldn't. The line "This isn't in your head. This is in your gut" is delivered as a dramatic reveal moment. The line "She thought I was being metaphorical. I wasn't." adds dramatic weight to what should be the calm-explanation zone. The contrast between visceral storytelling and calm mechanism education is good but imperfect -- the mechanism section occasionally reaches for dramatic effect instead of trusting its own clarity. Additionally, the mechanism runs slightly long. The paragraph beginning "GLP-1 medications change the gut environment dramatically" through "the structure doesn't hold" re-explains what the subsequent paragraph also covers (bacterial populations crashing). There is a small redundancy.

### Belief Shift 4 -- "The solution needs A+B+C": 4/5

**Evidence:**
- Component 1 discovered: "You have to wake the stomach up first. You have to restore the mechanical action..."
- Component 2 discovered: "Something needed to neutralize that sulfur at the source."
- Component 3 discovered: "A gentle prebiotic -- something that feeds the specific bacteria you want to grow back, at a dose a compromised gut can handle."

**Assessment:** Four components are discovered individually with narrative momentum between each. The reader builds a checklist. Skepticism is present through Ramos checking the formulation. The "colleague in Phoenix" entry point is more natural than "then I found." Docked from 5 because the existing-products-fail step is weak. Standard probiotics are mentioned conceptually ("even if she ate every fermented food on the shelf and took every probiotic strain in the store") but no specific competing product is shown failing the checklist. The copy says "Every other gut product I'd evaluated either targeted the colon, which is the wrong location, or threw probiotics at a stomach that couldn't support them" -- but this is a summary dismissal, not specific products being tested and failing. A 5 would name a specific probiotic brand or fermented food protocol, show Ramos evaluating it, and show it failing on the motility criterion specifically.

### Belief Shift 5 -- "This actually works": 3/5

**Evidence:**
- Timeline present: "Within four or five days, the pressure in her stomach after eating started to ease" -> "Around day ten, she slept through the night" -> "The anxiety didn't vanish overnight... it receded... week by week" -> "Denise hasn't had a panic attack in four months"
- Pain callback: "No three AM bolt. She woke up at six-thirty and lay there for five minutes waiting for it. It didn't come."
- Emotional circuit close: "She sleeps with the lights off."
- Social proof: "I've now seen this pattern resolve in nineteen of my twenty-three patients."

**Assessment:** The timeline is present and progressive. The pain callback to the 3 AM bolt is strong -- "lay there for five minutes waiting for it" is a genuine detail that feels observed, not constructed. "She sleeps with the lights off" resolves the earlier "She started sleeping with the lights on." However, skepticism at each stage is largely absent. Denise doesn't doubt after day 5. She doesn't worry it's placebo at day 10. The results section reads as a smooth upward trajectory without resistance. A real person taking a gummy after months of terror would have doubt at every checkpoint. Additionally, the close section runs long. The copy continues for four paragraphs after "She sleeps with the lights off" -- the binary choice, the sister line, the screaming metaphor, and the guarantee. The strongest close point is "She sleeps with the lights off" and everything after it dilutes the impact. The "screaming stops when your gut remembers how to say you're safe" is a statement, not an image. The guarantee delivery is functional but the copy would be stronger if it ended 100 words sooner.

## TOP 3 WEAKNESSES (mandatory)

1. **The close runs too long and dilutes the strongest closing image.** "She sleeps with the lights off" is the emotional circuit closing -- it resolves "she started sleeping with the lights on" from the pain section. But the copy continues for four more paragraphs: Denise's anger, the binary choice, the "screaming" metaphor, and the guarantee. Each of these re-argues a case the story already made. The anger paragraph adds a new note (and is the best of the four), but the binary choice paragraphs step outside the story to argue directly at the reader, which breaks the dual track. **Fix:** Cut from after "She sleeps with the lights off" + Denise's anger paragraph directly to the guarantee line. Remove the "screaming stops" and "binary choice" paragraphs entirely. Trust the story to carry the close.

2. **Results section lacks skepticism at each stage.** Denise's improvement is a smooth upward trajectory: bloating eases (day 5), sleeps through the night (day 10), anxiety fades (weeks), no panic attacks (4 months). At no point does Denise doubt, worry it's placebo, or have a bad day that tests her commitment. Real recovery from months of terror includes setbacks, doubt, and the specific fear that improvement is temporary. The absence of skepticism makes the results feel constructed rather than observed. **Fix:** Insert one moment of doubt after the day-10 sleep breakthrough -- Denise waking on day 12 with a mild flutter and bracing for the bolt that turns out to be nothing. Or Denise telling Ramos she doesn't trust it yet. One sentence of resistance would make the entire trajectory more believable.

3. **Mirror moments share the same emotional register (terror-helplessness) without oscillation.** The living room, 3 AM, and parking lot scenes all hit fear and despair. Real trauma experience oscillates -- moments of numb detachment, flashes of irrational anger at the medication or the doctor, dark humor as coping mechanism, the specific exhaustion of being afraid of being afraid. The consistent terror register is effective but slightly predictable by the third scene. AI-generated emotional content tends to sustain a single emotional tone rather than oscillating the way real human experience does. **Fix:** Revise the parking lot scene to include a flash of anger rather than pure despair. Denise crying and then getting angry at the Xanax bottle in her hand, or at herself for sitting in a parking lot at 54 years old. Anger breaks the pattern and makes her feel more dimensional.

## REVISION PRIORITY

If this copy were to be revised, the single highest-leverage change would be: **Cut the close to end on "She sleeps with the lights off" plus the anger paragraph and the guarantee line, and use the freed-up reader attention to add one moment of doubt/setback in the results timeline.** The close currently does work the story already did (re-arguing the binary, re-metaphoring the mechanism), and the results currently lack the skepticism that makes progressive timelines feel observed rather than constructed. Moving weight from the close to the results section would fix the two biggest weaknesses simultaneously without adding word count.

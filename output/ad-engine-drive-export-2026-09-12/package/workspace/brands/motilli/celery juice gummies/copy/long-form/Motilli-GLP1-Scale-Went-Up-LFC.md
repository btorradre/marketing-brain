I'm on Wegovy and I gained 3.8 pounds in six days.

I hadn't cheated once. I know that for a fact, because I weigh my food on a gram scale and log it like a court reporter. The gain was real. It just wasn't fat.

Nobody tells you where it actually was. So I will.

I'm 51. I do books for a living — I reconcile numbers until they behave — and I brought that same energy to Wegovy. Daily weigh-ins, same time, same conditions. A graph app. Averages, trendlines.

For the first four months the line went down like a ski slope. It was, I'll be honest, the most satisfying data of my adult life. Twenty-seven pounds. After two decades of diets where the numbers eventually betrayed me, the numbers were finally cooperating.

Then the line started doing things that made no sense.

Flat weeks where my logged intake said impossible. Then a stall. Then — the morning that broke something in me — up 3.8 pounds in six days, during a week I could document like a tax filing.

I did what a numbers person does with a discrepancy. I audited myself. Re-weighed the food scale with calibration weights. Checked sodium. Checked my cycle math. Nothing explained it.

Here's what I wasn't putting in any log: I hadn't been to the bathroom in six days.

It didn't occur to me to connect them. Constipation was just the annoying background hum of the medication — the bloating that arrived every afternoon like a scheduled meeting, the sulfur burps I'd learned to swallow in front of clients, the counting.

I treated it as a comfort problem. The scale was a progress problem. Separate ledgers.

So I kept attacking the wrong number. I cut another 200 calories. The line didn't move — but the afternoon bloat got worse, which I now understand and will explain. I added a fasting window. Same. I drank water until I sloshed.

And then I did the thing I'm least proud of. I started splitting my dose. Half now, half "later," without telling my doctor. My private theory: if the medication was doing something weird to my metabolism, less of it might free the line.

What I got for that experiment was the worst of both worlds. The food noise crept back in around day ten — that low radio static of thinking about snacks — while the constipation didn't improve at all. Hungrier, still backed up, line still flat.

I had negotiated with the wrong variable and paid twice.

The audit finally got fixed by my sister, who has been an OR nurse for twenty years and has zero patience for my graphs. I was complaining on speakerphone — six days, 3.8 pounds, the injustice of it — and she cut me off mid-sentence.

"Claire. You know that's transit weight, right? You're not gaining. You're weighing your backlog."

Weighing my backlog.

I stood at my kitchen island with the phone in my hand doing arithmetic I'd never once thought to do. Six days of food, sitting in the system, undelivered. Of course the scale counts it. A scale measures weight, not fat. Food that hasn't moved yet still weighs something.

She said one more thing before she had to go: "The medication slows your stomach. Everything you're taking for it works on your colon. Look it up."

I looked it up. For three evenings, the way I do everything.

Here's what's actually happening, and it's simpler than anything on my spreadsheets. Wegovy slows the stomach down. On purpose. There's a clinical name for it — delayed gastric emptying — and it's part of the design, not a glitch. Food goes in and sits there. Hours. Sometimes days. While it sits, it ferments. That's the sulfur gas I was swallowing in client meetings. It dries out and compacts. That's the brick feeling.

I learned the colon — the place every product in my cabinet is built to treat — is mostly empty. The pileup is at the top of the system. Not the bottom.

The scale measures weight, not fat. A stomach full of food that hasn't moved yet has weight too. Six days of undelivered food was standing on that scale with me every morning, and I was cutting calories to punish weight that was never fat.

Line up my remedy drawer against that and it stops being a mystery.

Miralax pulls water into the colon. Nothing was down there to soften.

Fiber powder adds bulk. Bulk behind a stomach that isn't moving just packs the line tighter — which is exactly why my afternoons got more distended the more of it I took.

Stimulant laxatives squeeze the colon. You can squeeze an empty pipe all day. Nothing comes.

And cutting calories harder? Less food doesn't fix a stomach that's already stalled. It just means less signal for the stomach to move at all. That's why starving myself made the bloat worse. Every lever I pulled was connected to nothing.

Chlorophyllin — concentrated chlorophyll. Binds hydrogen sulfide, the sulfur gas itself, at the molecule. The burps I was swallowing at conference tables — that's chemistry. This is what actually stops it.

I built a comparison table, obviously. Three columns — the three things the research kept naming — and a row for every product I could find. What the audit turned up is that the market is full of the right ingredients in the wrong form, priced like they're the right one.

Celery led the ledger, because apigenin comes from celery and the celery-juice trend has an entire aisle now. The celery-seed capsules that did print a number printed one or two percent.

Right line item, wrong amount.

The motility blends were ginger — a different account entirely, and no entry at all in the gas column.

The chlorophyll products didn't reconcile. The liquid drops trending everywhere are unstable, dosed for skin and "detox," and they degrade before you finish the bottle. And the fiber failed on sight, every one of them. The aisle is bulk — psyllium, wheat dextrin — which is a debit to a system already in the red; it packs the jam tighter. Every product cleared one column and bounced on the other two — and often the one it "cleared" was the wrong form anyway. Trace apigenin. Degrading chlorophyll. Bulk fiber. Nothing balanced all three.

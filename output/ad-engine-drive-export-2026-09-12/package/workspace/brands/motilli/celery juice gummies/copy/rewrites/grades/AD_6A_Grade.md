## DIMENSION 1: BELIEF-SHIFT ARCHITECTURE (22/25)

### Belief Shift 1 — "My suffering is real": 5/5

**Evidence:**
- Bathroom floor scene: "Cold tile. Pajama pants. The kind of pain where you can feel your heartbeat in your abdomen because everything is so swollen and pressurized that your pulse has nowhere to go but up against the walls of your own stomach."
- Food noise: "This constant broadcast running in my head from the moment I woke up. What's for breakfast. When's lunch. Is there cheese in the fridge."
- Daughter's comment: "Mom, is something rotting in here?"
- 3 AM nausea: "just my stomach deciding that right now, in the dark, while I'm trying to sleep, is a good time to make me wonder if I'm going to throw up."
- Looking pregnant at 57: "watching my stomach press against shirts that fit everywhere else because I was losing weight everywhere except the place where everything was trapped."
- Christmas dinner: "ate four bites of turkey and my mother-in-law asked if I was feeling okay and I said I was fine and went to the bathroom and sat on the edge of the tub with my hands on my stomach waiting for the wave to pass."

**Assessment:** Six mirror moments, all surgically specific, all showing life consequences — not just symptoms but identity erosion, relationship shame, daily routine destruction. The food noise passage is the kind of internal monologue a reader would screenshot. The bathroom floor opening is visceral enough to produce a physical reaction. The Christmas dinner scene captures the exact performance of normalcy that GLP-1 users live daily. This earns full marks.

### Belief Shift 2 — "My approach is wrong about something": 4/5

**Evidence:**
- Doctor scene: "Furious that my doctor told me the side effects were normal and to give it time and it had been four months and nothing was better."
- The pharmacy basket: "My husband looked at it one morning and called it the pharmacy. I didn't laugh because it wasn't funny. It was evidence of what I'd agreed to."
- Failed product cascade: "None of it worked the way it was supposed to. The Miralax would eventually produce something but the cramping made me curl up on the bed. Mag07 was a roulette wheel."

**Assessment:** The trust vacuum is built through accumulation rather than a single witnessing scene. The doctor moment is a single line of narrative summary — "my doctor told me the side effects were normal and to give it time" — rather than a SHOWN scene where the reader watches the doctor be dismissive. The reader is told what the doctor said, not shown the appointment. This is a minor editorializing shortcut. The pharmacy basket as the physical embodiment of failed approaches is strong — the reader sees the futility made concrete. But the doctor beat is narrated, not witnessed. Loses one point for the doctor being summarized rather than dramatized in scene.

### Belief Shift 3 — "The problem is in the stomach, not the colon": 5/5

**Evidence:**
- Cause-and-effect chain delivered through pharmacist's post: "The medication slows your stomach. That's the mechanism... But when your stomach slows down that much, the muscles that push food forward start to weaken. Food sits. It ferments. That's where the sulfur burps come from."
- Highway analogy: "imagine a highway where a bridge collapsed at mile marker one."
- Villain teardowns: Miralax ("pulling water into my colon. My colon wasn't the problem"), fiber ("adding bulk to a system that couldn't move what was already there"), Gas-X ("The gas wasn't being produced in my intestines. It was being produced in my stomach").

**Assessment:** Clean cause-and-effect chain in plain language. No numbered system. No named framework. The highway analogy is used exactly once and is not branded or repeated as a label. Every villain is dismantled in 1-2 sentences by referencing which cause it misses. The narrator's realization — "I stared at my bathroom basket the next morning and realized I had a counter full of tow trucks" — is the single most efficient line in the mechanism section, collapsing the analogy and the failed treatment cascade into one image. Mechanism section carries zero emotional weight; the contrast with the visceral storytelling before it IS the credibility signal.

### Belief Shift 4 — "The solution needs A+B+C": 4/5

**Evidence:**
- Three components discovered individually: apigenin/prokinetic for gastric motility, chlorophyll for sulfur compounds, prebiotic micro-dose for bacterial support.
- Existing products fail the checklist: "I looked at celery supplements. No chlorophyll. I looked at chlorophyll drops. No prokinetic compounds. I looked at prebiotic blends. Giant fiber doses that would make everything worse."
- Product confirmation: "Celery juice extract with apigenin. Chlorophyll. Prebiotic fiber in a small dose. All three. In one gummy."

**Assessment:** Components are discovered individually through the pharmacist's post. Existing products explicitly fail the checklist. The product enters while she's searching for dosing studies — "I almost scrolled past it because I wasn't looking for a product." Skepticism is explicit: "I did not believe it would work." The confirmation beat maps the product to the three criteria. However, the product confirmation runs slightly long. From "The company was called Motilli" through the ordering, there are approximately 7-8 sentences of product-adjacent content (clean label, third-party tested, US-made, pectin vs. gelatin). This pushes past the 3-5 sentence confirmation window and edges into feature territory, even though the voice stays consistent. The pectin vs. gelatin detail is a genuine dual-function moment (nausea connection), but the accumulated trust markers read as a product page translated into narrative. Deducting one point.

### Belief Shift 5 — "This works and I keep what I value": 4/5

**Evidence:**
- Progressive timeline with skepticism: Week 1 sulfur burps cut in half ("I gave it skepticism from the first gummy"), Week 2 first normal bathroom visit, Week 4 daily regularity and bloating softening.
- Life moments, not metrics: "my daughter was in the room with me for two hours and didn't mention a smell," "the most unremarkable, normal experience I'd had since September," "I could button my work pants without the pressure making me wince."
- Pain-to-payoff callback: Basket thrown away resolves the pharmacy basket scene. "I don't sit on the floor anymore" resolves the bathroom floor opening.
- Close: "I cancelled that deal... I don't sit on the floor anymore."

**Assessment:** Timeline is progressive with skepticism at each stage. Results are measured in restored life moments, not metrics. The daughter-not-mentioning-the-smell callback resolves the daughter's "is something rotting in here" scene. The basket disposal resolves the pharmacy basket scene. The close resolves the philosophical binary (freedom vs. imprisonment) through emotional weight, not re-teaching. However, the close runs slightly long — the three paragraphs starting with "I need the woman on the bathroom floor right now to hear this" shift into second-person direct address. "You don't have to make that deal. You don't have to choose between your body and your sanity." This is the narrator stepping toward the reader and making the argument explicit rather than letting the story carry it. The final three lines recover ("I sat on that floor five months ago... I don't sit on the floor anymore"), but the second-person pivot is a brief break in the show-don't-tell principle. One point deducted.

## TOP 3 WEAKNESSES (mandatory)

1. **The second-person direct address in the close breaks the show-don't-tell principle.** "I need the woman on the bathroom floor right now to hear this. You don't have to make that deal. You don't have to choose between your body and your sanity." This is the narrator stepping out of the story to argue.

**Headline:** A marriage therapist saw the same pattern in three GLP-1 couples and then recognized her own sister

When he stopped reaching for me I knew it wasn't about attraction

My name is Carolyn. I'm a licensed marriage and family therapist. Twenty-two years in practice. And I need to tell you about something I started seeing in my office that I was not trained to recognize.

Three couples. Same presenting issue. All within two months of each other, starting around March of last year. In each case, the wife had pulled back from physical intimacy. In each case, the husband was reading it as rejection. Two of the three husbands were already using the word separation before they walked through my door. All three wives were on a GLP-1 medication.

I do couples work. I've seen every version of withdrawal. Affairs, depression, resentment, hormonal shifts, grief. I have a framework for all of it. But this was different. These women weren't withdrawing from their husbands. When I got each of them alone, the story was the same. Word for word.

The sulfur burps that come without warning. The bloating that makes her feel six months pregnant by evening. The constipation that cycles between nothing for days and then a morning she can't leave the bathroom. And the thing none of them could say to their husbands: I am terrified of what my body will do if I lie down next to you after dinner.

One woman told me she times her meals now. She eats at five. Alone. She tells Mark she isn't hungry later. That way, by the time he wants to sit on the couch together, her stomach has had three hours to settle. She said it like she was describing a military operation. Entry time. Buffer window. Contingency plan for if the bloating starts anyway. She had an excuse ready for every scenario. Headache. Cramps. Tired.

She'd rather her husband believe she doesn't want him than risk what might happen if she lets him close.

Another woman told me she changed sides of the bed. She used to sleep on the left, facing her husband. Now she sleeps on the right, facing the wall. She said she made the switch because the sulfur burps are worse when she lies on her left side. But the real reason, the one she told me with her voice barely above a whisper, is that facing the wall means he can't see her face when the pressure builds and she has to swallow it back down.

The third woman stopped going to restaurants. She and her husband used to have a Friday night. Nineteen years of Friday nights. She told him she was watching their budget. The truth is she cannot sit in a booth across from him and control what her body does for ninety minutes.

Three women. Three marriages built over decades. All three shrinking their entire lives around a stomach they can't predict.

And their husbands, who don't know any of this, have each written a story. The story men write when they feel rejected. She lost the weight and doesn't need me anymore. She's gained confidence and is pulling away. Maybe she met someone. The stories husbands tell themselves when physical closeness disappears are almost always about their own inadequacy. And they almost never match what's actually happening.

The reality is a woman who misses her husband and is terrified of her own stomach.

I sat with that pattern professionally for about a week before it turned personal. My sister Diane had been on Wegovy since January. She'd made four comments over the previous two months that I'd filed away as general stress. Now I replayed them.

She'd said she and Mark were in a weird spot. She'd said she didn't feel like herself. She'd said her body was doing things she couldn't control. And she'd said the one that hit me when I heard it again in my own memory: I just wish I could be normal for one evening.

I'm a therapist. I listen to language for a living. And I missed it in my own sister because I wasn't looking for it. Because nobody told me to look for it.

I didn't call her right away. I did research first because that's how my brain works when something scares me. But I wasn't looking at it the way a gastroenterologist would. I was looking at it as someone who needed to understand what this was doing to a marriage. What it was doing to four marriages, counting Diane's.

The mechanism is straightforward once you see it. A GLP-1 medication slows the stomach. That is the design. Food sits longer, appetite drops, weight comes off. Every one of these women loves the medication for that. They are not complaining about the drug. They are grateful for it.

But when the stomach slows that much, food ferments. Bacteria break it down and produce sulfur gas. That gas rises as burps. Bloating pushes outward because the system is backed up. And the whole digestive tract stalls because nothing upstream is moving the way it should.

Now here is the piece no one is connecting to the marriages in my office. The withdrawal from intimacy is not about the burps or the bloating as isolated symptoms. It is about unpredictability. These women cannot predict what their bodies will do in any given moment. And intimacy requires surrender. You cannot be close to another person while simultaneously clenching against the possibility that your body is about to betray you. So they pull back. Not from their husbands. From vulnerability itself.

Everything these women had tried — the Gas-X, the charcoal capsules, the probiotics, the peppermint tea — all of it works downstream. In the colon. At the end of the process. But the problem starts upstream. In the stomach. The muscles that are barely contracting because the medication is designed to quiet them. Gas-X breaks up gas that's already formed in the intestines. It can't reach gas that's being produced in a stalled stomach. Charcoal absorbs general gas in the lower tract, but by the time it gets there, the sulfur has already been belched up. Probiotics feed gut bacteria but do nothing about the muscles that aren't moving food through. Peppermint soothes, but soothing a stomach that isn't contracting is like rubbing lotion on a leg that can't walk.

None of it could work. Not because the products are bad. Because the products are in the wrong place.

While I was reading about why the stomach stalls, I kept coming across references to compounds that support gastric motility. Not laxatives, which push from below. Compounds that actually encourage the stomach muscles to contract again. One compound kept appearing in the research: apigenin, concentrated naturally in celery. Study after study describing its effect on the smooth muscle contractions in the stomach wall. Gentle. Not aggressive. More like reminding the muscles they're supposed to be working.

That was one piece. But the sulfur was a separate problem. The gas itself needed to be neutralized, not just moved through. A different line of reading led me to chlorophyll — the green pigment in plants — which binds to hydrogen sulfide and changes its chemistry. Different mechanism, different target, same woman.

And then the nutritional piece. GLP-1 patients who eat less absorb less. The vitamins that drop fastest — A, C, K, the B vitamins — are the ones the gut lining needs to maintain itself. A woman whose stomach isn't processing food properly is depleted in the exact nutrients her digestive system needs to recover.

Three separate requirements. Motility support for the stalled stomach. Sulfur neutralization for the gas. Nutritional restoration for what she's not absorbing. I sat with that and realized I was looking for something that did all three from one source.

I was reading a thread in a GLP-1 support community late one night, still in research mode, when someone mentioned a celery juice fiber gummy. I almost scrolled past it. A gummy. For the thing that was fracturing four marriages. But I clicked through because the ingredient list matched. Celery juice extract with apigenin at a concentration that would actually act as a prokinetic. Chlorophyll for the sulfur. A micro-dose of prebiotic fiber — not the heavy bulk of a Metamucil that would add freight to a stalled system, but a small amount to feed beneficial bacteria without backing things up further. And vitamins A, C, K, B6, folate. Pectin-based, not gelatin, which matters because gelatin can trigger nausea in a stomach that's already slow.

The company was called Motilli. I'd never heard of it. Small. No celebrity endorsements. No advertising I'd ever seen. I checked for third-party testing, GMP certification, read the low-star reviews first. Then I called Diane.

I didn't lead with the clinical stuff. I asked her how she was feeling. Really feeling. And she told me about the night Mark asked if she was still attracted to him and she couldn't answer because the truth was too humiliating and a lie would have destroyed something she couldn't rebuild.

I heard my own clients' words coming out of my sister's mouth.

I told her what I'd learned. About the stomach slowing, the fermentation, the sulfur, why everything she'd tried works in the wrong part of her body. She was quiet for a long time. Then she said: That's why nothing works.

She ordered the gummies that night. I want to be honest about the timeline because as a therapist I believe false hope is a form of cruelty.

The first few days she noticed almost nothing and texted me another dud. I told her to wait. Prokinetic compounds are not antacids. They are not masking a symptom. They are trying to restore a muscular process that's been suppressed for months.

Day five she mentioned the burps had changed. Not gone. Less sulfuric. She'd stopped cupping her hand over her mouth at her desk, which I hadn't even known she was doing.

By the end of week two the evening bloating had dropped enough that she ate dinner at the table with Mark. At the normal time. Sitting across from him instead of eating alone at five and pretending she wasn't hungry later.

Week four she called me laughing. She said, I watched a movie with Mark last night. On the couch. Lying down. And nothing happened.

She meant nothing bad happened. No sulfur. No pressure. No emergency calculation about whether she could make it to the bathroom in time. She leaned against him and watched the movie and it was so ordinary that she cried afterward.

That is the part that breaks me as a therapist. Normal should not make you cry. But when you have been avoiding normal for months because your body turned closeness into a threat, getting it back feels like something enormous even though it should just be a Tuesday.

The constipation took about five weeks to regulate. She said the gaps shortened first — from four or five days to two or three — and then it became daily without her noticing. She stopped tracking it. That is how she knew.

She told me last week that Mark hasn't asked the attraction question again. Not because they had a conversation about it. Because she stopped turning away. He reached for her and she didn't flinch. And the story he'd been writing — the one where she didn't want him anymore — quietly dissolved because the evidence stopped supporting it.

I have since told my clients what I learned. I don't prescribe anything. I'm a therapist. But I think the women in my office deserve to understand why the things they've been trying cannot work. And I think their husbands deserve to know that the withdrawal was never about them.

If you are on a GLP-1 medication and you have been pulling away from someone you love — not because you want to, but because your body has made closeness feel dangerous — please hear me. The problem is likely in your stomach, not your colon. What you have been taking works in the wrong place. And the person next to you in bed is writing a story about what your distance means.

The story is wrong. And every night it continues, it becomes more real for them.

Your marriage might not be broken. Your stomach might just be slow. And there is a difference between a marriage that needs repair and a body that needs to start moving again.

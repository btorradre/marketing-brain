There's a calendar in my phone that nobody has ever seen.

No birthdays. No meetings. Just a red dot on the days I went to the bathroom.

Some months have four dots.

I'm 54. I manage an office of nineteen people. I've raised two children, buried a parent, negotiated a commercial lease. And for most of the past year, the most closely guarded secret in my life was a calendar app tracking my own bowel movements like they were rare bird sightings.

If you're on one of these medications, I don't have to explain the counting. You do it at dinner parties. Somebody passes the bread and part of your brain goes: today's Thursday, last dot was Friday. Six days.

You smile. You take the bread.

I started Zepbound last fall. Before anything else — it worked. Twenty years of diets that ended in regain, and this was the first thing that ever made my brain go quiet around food. Thirty-eight pounds in seven months.

I'm not here to talk anyone out of the medication. I'd fight you over that medication.

But nobody prepared me for what it did to my digestion.

Three days. Then four. Then the bloating that turned my waistband into a countdown timer — fine at breakfast, cutting into me by three o'clock. Then the burps. Sulfur. The kind you taste first and pray nobody else can smell.

I started keeping mints in every coat pocket. Every bag. The car console. Like a woman running a small pharmacy.

And the calendar grew teeth. I planned around it. Moved a dentist appointment once because I was on day seven and couldn't stand the thought of the chair tipping me backward. I own a pair of pants — soft waistband, dark pattern — that I think of, without any humor at all, as the day-six pants.

My medicine cabinet looked like a pharmacy end-cap. Miralax every morning in my coffee. Colace. Fiber gummies. Senna tea that cramped me awake at 4 AM and produced nothing. Magnesium at night.

I wasn't lazy about this. I attacked it the way I attack everything. Thoroughly. On a schedule. With a tracking system.

The tracking system is what broke me.

In April, my husband borrowed my phone in the car to pull up directions. A reminder banner slid down.

"Day 8."

He said, "What's day 8?"

And I said — without missing a beat, at 60 miles an hour — "Work thing. Inventory deadline."

I lied to my husband of 29 years about my own body. In a moving car. Instantly. Like I'd rehearsed it.

He went back to the directions. I stared out the window and thought: what exactly have I turned into?

That night I sat on the edge of the bathtub at 12:15 AM with the calendar open, doing what I can only describe as grief math. Day 8. Fourteen products. One year of dots.

I have a standing coffee date, first Saturday of the month, with my friend Gina. We've known each other since our kids were in diapers. She's also — and I'd somehow never once used this — a gastroenterologist. Twenty-two years of practice.

A year of red dots, and I had never told her a thing.

Apparently you'll confess to a search bar before you'll confess to a friend with a medical degree.

That Saturday I almost canceled. Day 10 by then. I was pulling on the day-six pants — operating well outside their jurisdiction — when I decided that if I could lie to my husband in a moving car, the least I could do was tell one person the truth.

We got our coffees. She asked how I was.

Instead of saying "fine," I put my phone on the table, opened the calendar, and slid it across.

Gina looked at it for a long moment. The red dots. The gaps between them.

Then she said, "How long have you been on the Zepbound?"

Not "have you tried Miralax." Not "are you drinking enough water." I told her — since last fall — and waited for the part where a weight-loss question turned into a bathroom answer.

"Because everyone treats this like a colon problem," she said. "It isn't one."

I must have looked as lost as I felt, because she kept going.

"Your stomach slows down. On purpose. There's a clinical name for it — delayed gastric emptying — and it's part of the design, not a glitch. Food goes in and sits. Hours. Sometimes days. While it sits, it ferments. That's your sulfur burps. It dries out and compacts. That's the brick feeling."

She turned her cup slowly.

"And here's what nobody's told you: your colon — the place everyone treats — is mostly empty. The pileup is at the top of the system. Not the bottom."

My entire cabinet had been aimed at the bottom.

"Miralax pulls water into the colon. Nothing down there to soften."

"The fiber gummies add bulk. Bulk behind a stomach that isn't moving just packs the line tighter."

"Senna squeezes the colon. You can squeeze an empty pipe all day. Nothing comes."

Eight products. Every one working downstream of the actual problem.

"So what do I do?" I asked. "What would you give me if I were your patient?"

And here's the part I respect her for. She was quiet a moment. Then:

"Honestly? My toolbox is Linzess and lowering your dose, and I don't love either one for you. The interesting research right now is upstream — supporting the stomach's motility directly. It's a few years ahead of what we prescribe. If you were my sister, I'd tell you to go read it."

So I read it. Two nights, the way I do everything. She knew exactly what she was doing, assigning me homework.

Three things kept surfacing.

Apigenin — a compound concentrated in celery — kept appearing in motility studies. It supports the vagus nerve, the signal line that tells the stomach when to contract and push. The medication turns that dial down. Apigenin helps turn it back up. Not fighting the medication. Restoring the schedule it muted.

Chlorophyllin — concentrated chlorophyll — binds hydrogen sulfide, the sulfur gas, and neutralizes the actual molecule. Not a mint. Not a mask. Chemistry.

And a low-bulk soluble prebiotic fiber. Gel-forming, moisture-drawing. Softens what's already stacked up without adding anything new to the jam. The exact opposite of the fiber everyone had been handing me.

Wake the stomach. Neutralize the sulfur. Soften the backlog. All three at once, or nothing moves.

So I went shopping with a checklist instead of a hope. And learned that the right ingredient in the wrong form is its own dead end.

Celery first, because Gina had said the word apigenin and the internet is drowning in celery. But the celery-juice ritual everyone swears by delivers only a trace of it. You'd drink a garden a day and still fall short of what the studies used. The celery-seed capsules admitted, when they admitted anything, to one or two percent.

Not a dose. A rumor of one.

The motility blends were all ginger. A gentle nudge on a different pathway. Nothing for the sulfur, which was half my problem, untouched.

The chlorophyll aisle was its own trap. Those liquid-chlorophyll drops all over my feed are unstable, sold for "detox" and skin, dosed for a glow. They degrade on the shelf. The form that actually binds the sulfur molecule is chlorophyllin — stabilized, specific — and I could barely find it.

And the fiber. Every product in the aisle was the bulky kind. Psyllium. The wheat powders. Precisely what had been burying me. The inulin "prebiotics" were worse — they ferment and make more gas. What I needed was the low-bulk, gel-forming soluble kind, and it wasn't sold alone anywhere I looked.

Every jar did one job, and usually a counterfeit version of it. Trace apigenin. Chlorophyll that dies on a shelf. Bulk fiber. Nothing combined all three in the forms Gina's studies actually used.

I laughed at my kitchen island. A gummy. Candy, for the thing that had me lying to my husband in the car.

Somewhere in there I realized I'd stopped opening the calendar. I checked — eleven days since I'd logged anything. I used to open that thing the way other people check the weather.

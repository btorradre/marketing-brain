In March, my wife quit her shot.

She took the $1,300 box of Mounjaro out of the refrigerator and dropped it in the kitchen trash. She was 43 pounds down. The medication was working better than anything she'd tried in twenty years.

She stood there in her robe with her hand still on the trash lid and said, "I don't care anymore. I can't live like this."

I want to walk you through how a smart, disciplined woman ends up throwing away the thing that was saving her. Because I've since learned we were not remotely alone. And because the ending surprised both of us.

Dana is 49. Before Mounjaro there was a decade of earnest, exhausting effort — macro apps, a nutritionist, 5 AM boot camps in a church parking lot. Every one worked for a season and then the season ended.

When her doctor started her on Mounjaro, the change was so fast it was almost eerie. She described it as a radio in her head going silent. Eight months, 43 pounds. She kept the tags on new clothes for a week before wearing them, like she didn't trust them yet.

That was the miracle. Here's the bill.

Around month three, her digestion started shutting down. Three days between bathroom trips, then five, then a week. The bloat that made her undo her jeans in the car outside restaurants. The sulfur burps she was so ashamed of she started answering my questions from the next room. A hard knot under her ribs she'd press on while we watched TV, absently, like she was checking a bruise.

She fought it the way Dana fights everything. Thoroughly. Miralax daily. Colace. Fiber powder. Senna tea. Magnesium. Prune juice. A drawer that looked like a pharmacy sample case. Nothing worked for more than a day, and half of it made the bloat worse.

Her doctor's answer, at two separate appointments: drink more water, and "we can always lower the dose."

Lower the dose. The thing keeping the radio silent.

So in March she made her own call, and the box went in the trash. If the price of the miracle was living like that, she'd rather keep her misery familiar.

Eleven days later, at dinner, she went quiet in a specific way I recognized. She put her fork down and said, "It's back."

The radio. The food noise. Plugged back in at full volume like it had been waiting in the wall.

Over the next nine weeks she regained 14 of the 43 pounds. I need you to understand what that did to her, because it wasn't about the number. It was the trapdoor feeling. She'd escaped, and now the floor was opening again, and the only rope out was the one she'd thrown away because holding it hurt too much.

I found her one night sitting on the step stool in the pantry, lights off, crying as quietly as she could.

"Both directions are bad," she said. "Tell me which bad one to pick."

I'm an engineer. I've spent thirty years fixing systems where both options are bad, and in thirty years the answer has never been either of them. It's always a third thing nobody looked at.

But I'm not a doctor, and this wasn't my system. So I called Mark.

Mark and I have been friends since college — thirty-one years of fishing trips and bad golf. He's also a gastroenterologist, which until that week had never come up for anything except him refusing to let any of us skip a colonoscopy.

I told him I needed an hour and it wasn't about me. Saturday afternoon, his deck, two beers.

I laid the whole thing out. The misery. The drawer full of products. The doctor offering a lower dose. The box in the trash. The fourteen pounds. The pantry.

Mark listened the way he listens — leaned back, let me get all the way to the end. Then:

"First thing you need to hear is that nothing in that drawer ever had a chance. Not because Dana did anything wrong. Because all of it was pointed at the wrong organ."

I asked him what that meant.

"The medication slows her stomach down. On purpose. There's a clinical name for it — delayed gastric emptying — and it's part of the design, not a glitch. Everything she eats is sitting at the top of the system for hours. Sometimes days. While it sits, it ferments. That's the sulfur burps. It dries out and packs down. That's the knot under her ribs."

He took a pull on his beer.

"Meanwhile her colon, which is where every product in that drawer goes to work, is mostly empty. The pileup is at the top. Not the bottom."

I sat with that a second, because I have built an entire career on that exact failure — a whole crew working hard on the wrong end of the line.

"So the Miralax—"

"Pulls water into the colon. Nothing down there to soften. The fiber's worse — bulk behind a stomach that isn't moving just packs the line tighter. The senna squeezes the colon. You can squeeze an empty pipe all day. Nothing comes." He shrugged. "None of it touches the stomach."

"And lowering her dose?"

"Dials the whole medication down to protect the one thing it breaks. And quitting—" he tipped his bottle at me. "You two already ran that experiment."

Fourteen pounds. Yeah. We'd run it.

So I asked the only question left: what would he actually do?

He gave me the honest answer, the one I've thought about a lot since. "On my prescription pad? Linzess — which, if we're being straight with each other, also works on the colon. The research I actually find interesting is upstream. Supporting the stomach's motility directly. It hasn't made it to the pad yet. If it were my wife, that's what I'd be reading about tonight."

So that's what I read about that night. And the next two nights, because engineer habits die hard. I kept a list.

The first thing that kept coming up was apigenin, a compound concentrated in celery. The stomach contracts on a signal from the vagus nerve — the exact signal the medication turns down. In study after study, apigenin showed up supporting that signal. Not fighting the medication. More like reaching over and turning a dial back up that the medication had lowered.

That covered movement. It didn't cover the gas. Weeks of slow digestion means bacteria producing hydrogen sulfide faster than the body can clear it — the burps Dana was so ashamed of she'd answer me from another room. The answer there was chlorophyllin, a concentrated form of chlorophyll that binds the sulfur molecule itself and shuts it down.

And the third piece: everything already backed up in there has to soften and clear, and the worst possible way to do that is bulk fiber — more material into the jam. What the research pointed to instead was a low-bulk, gel-forming soluble prebiotic fiber that draws moisture into what's stuck without adding anything new to the pile.

Get the stomach moving. Kill the sulfur. Soften the backlog. All three at once, or you're just reorganizing the jam.

I put it in a spreadsheet. Dana laughed at me, which I counted as a win — it was the first laugh in a couple of weeks. Three requirements across the top. Products down the side.

Fourteen products over two evenings, and the sheet turned into a graveyard. The failures fell into the same pattern every time: the right part, spec'd wrong.

The celery products — and there were a lot, riding the juice fad — carried apigenin the way tap water carries gold. Trace. The juice you're supposed to drink by the quart isn't concentrated or standardized. The celery-seed capsules listed one or two percent.

Right compound. Useless dose.

The motility blends were ginger — a different mechanism, mild, and blind to the gas.

The chlorophyll column was a mess. The liquid drops everybody's posting about are unstable chlorophyll, dosed for skin and "detox," degrading on the shelf. The form that actually binds hydrogen sulfide is chlorophyllin — stabilized — and it barely showed up.

And the fiber was the whole bulk aisle — psyllium, wheat dextrin — which is the exact wrong spec for a jam. You don't clear a bottleneck by shoving more in behind it. The inulin "prebiotics" ferment and generate gas. The low-bulk, gel-forming soluble kind I actually needed wasn't sold by itself.

Fourteen products. Every one had a single ingredient, and usually the wrong grade of it. Trace apigenin. Dead chlorophyll. Bulk fiber. Not one had all three.

Movement, gas, backlog. All three, in the right spec.

Prevention, not rescue.

# AD 11A GRADE: "The Woman Who Stopped Cooking"

---

## SUMMARY

**Copy assessed:** "She cooked Thanksgiving for 31 years and then one November she couldn't"
**Word count:** ~2,650
**Narrator:** 61-year-old grandmother, identity = family cook, first person retrospective
**Awareness level targeted:** Problem Aware
**Cause-and-effect chain:** GLP-1 slows stomach muscles -> food sits and ferments -> hydrogen sulfide gas builds -> nausea constant -> can't cook -> identity stripped. Everything tried targets colon (downstream). Problem is stomach (upstream).

## DIMENSION 1: BELIEF-SHIFT ARCHITECTURE (21/25)

### Belief Shift 1 -- "My suffering is real": 5/5

**Evidence:** Mirror moments are surgically specific and numerous:
- "I got the berries washed and into the dish and the butter mixture done and when I opened the oven door, the heat and the smell hit me together and I had to walk outside and sit on the porch with my head between my knees."
- "I did it in shifts. Mix. Leave the kitchen. Sit down. Wait for the nausea to pass. Go back. Roll. Leave again. Wait again."
- "I ran the faucet so nobody would hear me."
- "I started carrying mints everywhere. I chewed gum until my jaw ached. I turned my face when I talked to people."
- "I put food on my plate because people were watching. A small piece of turkey. A spoonful of potatoes. A roll I didn't make. I pushed it around."

**Assessment:** At least 7 distinct mirror moments, each at the level of life-consequence erosion rather than surface symptom description. The cinnamon rolls in shifts, the faucet running to mask sounds, carrying mints -- these are the unspoken private behaviors a GLP-1 user would recognize instantly. The scenes progress from activity erosion to identity erosion. This earns the full 5.

### Belief Shift 2 -- "My approach is wrong about something": 3/5

**Evidence:** The trust vacuum is built through failed remedies, not through a doctor scene: "I mixed Miralax into water every night. Nothing. I tried Dulcolax and the cramps doubled me over but nothing moved. My doctor prescribed Colace. Same result." The doctor appears only as a prescriber of Colace and the original Wegovy script. No doctor scene -- no appointment, no dismissal, no moment of the doctor being well-meaning but limited.

**Assessment:** The failed treatment cascade does strong work building frustration. But the framework asks whether the reader arrives at "my current approach is wrong" through WITNESSING a doctor being limited, and that scene is absent. The doctor is mentioned twice in passing -- prescribing Wegovy, prescribing Colace -- but never shown being wrong in a way the reader witnesses. The Facebook group women recommending solutions partially fills this gap (community-level trust vacuum), but it is not the same as watching a doctor miss the root cause in real time. The belief shift happens through accumulated failure rather than through witnessing a specific authority figure get it wrong. This lands at 3 -- the reader recognizes the failed attempts, but the trust vacuum is built through telling ("None of it worked. And I didn't understand why.") rather than through a scene where authority is shown being specifically limited.

### Belief Shift 3 -- "The problem is in the stomach, not the colon": 5/5

**Evidence:** The mechanism arrives through the husband's bedside explanation: "He sat on the edge of the bed and he explained it to me in the simplest way he could." The cause-and-effect chain is clean: medication slows stomach -> food sits -> bacteria ferment it -> sulfur gas -> nausea. Then: "everything I had tried -- the Miralax, the Dulcolax, the Colace, the Mag07, the fiber, the enemas -- all of it works on the colon. The large intestine. Downstream. But the problem wasn't downstream. The problem was in my stomach. Upstream." Villain teardowns are compressed into one passage, each dismissed as "wrong address."

**Assessment:** Plain cause-and-effect, zero named frameworks. The "upstream/downstream" language is directional description, not a branded system. Villain teardowns reference the cause directly. The mechanism section carries zero emotional weight -- it is calm, explanatory, a man talking to his wife at the edge of a bed. The contrast between the visceral Thanksgiving scene and this calm explanation IS the credibility signal. Clean enough to retell to a friend in three sentences. Earns the full 5.

### Belief Shift 4 -- "The solution needs prokinetic + chlorophyll + vitamins": 4/5

**Evidence:** Components are discovered individually through the husband's research: (A) "something that gently stimulates the stomach wall to contract and move food through... a natural prokinetic... apigenin," (B) "chlorophyll neutralizes the sulfur compounds," (C) "my body wasn't getting the vitamins it needed. A, C, K, B6, folate." Product enters through a forum thread: "A woman had mentioned a celery juice gummy that had all of it." Skepticism present: "I told him I didn't believe in gummies."

**Assessment:** Three components discovered sequentially with narrative momentum between each. The product enters sideways through a forum thread -- not pitched. Skepticism is present. However, the existing-products-fail-the-checklist step is weak. The failed treatments (Miralax, etc.) were already dismissed in the mechanism section as targeting the wrong location. They are not re-tested against the new three-part criteria. The reader does not have a moment of thinking "Miralax doesn't have apigenin OR chlorophyll OR vitamins -- that's why." The checklist is implied by the upstream/downstream reframe rather than explicitly tested against the new criteria. Additionally, the product confirmation beat runs slightly long -- the "pectin-based, third-party tested, Clean Label Certified" line reads as a quick feature list squeezed into the narrator's voice. Docking one point for the weak checklist-fail step.

### Belief Shift 5 -- "This works and I keep my medication": 4/5

**Evidence:** Progressive timeline with skepticism: "By Thursday the sulfur burps were fewer. Not gone. But instead of ten a day, maybe four." "I did not let myself believe it yet." Week three: "I ate a full small lunch and my body did not fight me." Week six: the lasagna scene -- a direct callback to the opening pain. "My granddaughter ate two pieces... 'Grandma, you made the good dinner.'" Close: "I'm still on Wegovy. I want to say that clearly."

**Assessment:** The timeline is progressive with skepticism at every stage. Results measured in life moments (bowel movement without laxative, full small lunch, standing at the stove, the lasagna). Two pain scenes explicitly resolved: the can't-make-lasagna opening and the granddaughter's question. The close preserves the medication -- "I'm still on Wegovy" -- resolving the philosophical binary. The close ends on image: "that recipe did not survive three generations to die with me." However, the social proof is thin -- there is no external validation beyond the narrator's own experience. No friend who tried it, no husband confirming the change, no reference to others. The results section also lacks mess -- the progression from Thursday to week three to week six is smooth and tidy, when real supplement experiences have setbacks, bad days, moments of doubt beyond "I did not let myself believe it yet." Docking one point.

**Dimension 1 Total: 21/25**

## TOP 3 WEAKNESSES (mandatory)

1. **The trust vacuum (Belief Shift 2) is built through accumulated failure, not through witnessing authority being wrong.** There is no doctor scene. The doctor prescribes Wegovy and Colace in passing, but the reader never watches a doctor be well-meaning but limited in real time. Adding a brief scene -- even 3-4 sentences -- of the doctor suggesting "try more fiber" or "give it time, side effects usually pass" would give the reader someone to get angry at on the narrator's behalf and would deepen the trust vacuum that makes the husband's mechanism discovery feel necessary. **Fix:** Insert a brief doctor-visit scene between the failed treatment cascade and the "Meanwhile my kitchen was going quiet" transition. Show the doctor being kind but wrong -- suggesting a colon-focused remedy when the problem is in the stomach.

2. **The product confirmation beat carries marketing language that breaks the narrator's voice.** "Pectin-based, which meant it was gentle. Third-party tested. Clean Label Certified." is product-copy cadence, not grandmother-at-the-kitchen-table cadence. This is the single most detectable seam in the piece and it sits at the most sensitive moment -- the product introduction, where any whiff of ad-copy can retroactively damage the preceding story. **Fix:** Rewrite in the narrator's register: "He said it was made from pectin -- gentle on the stomach. And some lab had tested it, which mattered to him." Let the husband's voice carry the confirmation rather than switching to certification language.

3. **The emotional circuit resolves too cleanly.** The lasagna is perfect. The granddaughter says the perfect thing. The husband's eyes are wet. There is no mess in the resolution -- no moment where the lasagna was slightly different than she remembered, no moment where she had to sit down halfway through and wasn't sure she could finish, no awkward silence before the granddaughter spoke. Real recoveries are messy and partial. The surgical symmetry of pain-scene-to-resolution (can't make lasagna -> makes lasagna, granddaughter asks why she's not eating -> granddaughter praises the dinner) is beautiful structurally but reads as slightly constructed. **Fix:** Add one beat of imperfection to the resolution. She burns the garlic bread. Or she has to sit for ten minutes before she can serve it. Or the granddaughter doesn't say anything at first and the narrator has to live in the uncertainty for a moment. One crack in the resolution makes it real.

## Ad Copy

**Headline:** The sleep disorder three ER doctors missed because they never checked her stomach

I've been practicing sleep medicine for sixteen years. I've seen every version of insomnia there is. Circadian disruption. Sleep apnea. Restless legs. Chronic pain keeping people up. Anxiety-driven hyperarousal. Medication-induced wakefulness. I've treated thousands of patients and I thought I'd seen the full catalog.

Then about fourteen months ago, a woman named Patricia sat in my office and described something I'd never heard before.

She said every time she falls asleep, she jolts awake convinced she's about to die. Not a nightmare. Not a bad dream she can't remember. A full-body electrical shock of terror, like someone injected adrenaline directly into her chest. Heart pounding so hard she can feel it in her teeth. Hands numb. Vision narrowing. And a certainty — not a worry, not a fear, a certainty — that she is dying right now, in this bed, tonight.

She'd been to three emergency rooms in six weeks. EKG normal every time. Troponin normal. CT scan clean. One ER doctor said panic disorder. Another said nocturnal anxiety. The third told her to see a psychiatrist. She was prescribed Ativan, then Xanax, then an SSRI that made her so flat she said she felt like she was watching her own life through a window.

Patricia is fifty-six. No psychiatric history. Raised four children. Ran a catering business for twenty years. She described herself as the person other people lean on. She had never had a panic attack in her life until three weeks after she stopped taking Mounjaro.

That detail stopped me.

I asked her to repeat it. She said she'd been on Mounjaro for nine months. Lost fifty-one pounds. Her doctor tapered her off when her insurance changed. Seventeen days after her last injection, the death anxiety started.

I wrote it down. Seventeen days. I didn't know what to do with it yet. But I circled it.

I treated Patricia with standard sleep medicine protocol. Sleep hygiene optimization. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia. We adjusted the SSRI her psychiatrist had started. I gave her a short course of trazodone for the acute wakefulness. The textbook approach.

It didn't work. Not partially. Not slowly. It didn't touch it. She came back three weeks later looking like she hadn't slept in a month because she essentially hadn't. The trazodone knocked her out for two hours and then the bolt hit anyway and she was up until dawn.

Then I saw the second one.

A woman named Karen, fifty-two. Same presentation. Jolt awake. Death certainty. No psychiatric history. Eleven months on Ozempic, stopped five weeks prior. ER workup clean. Prescribed benzodiazepines that dulled the edges but didn't stop the bolt.

Then a third. Janet, sixty-one. Wegovy for seven months. Stopped when she reached her target weight. Three weeks later, couldn't sleep. Not standard insomnia. The death-terror bolt. She told me she'd started sleeping sitting up in a recliner because lying flat made it worse and she didn't know why.

Three women in two months with the same presentation that doesn't exist in any sleep medicine textbook I was trained on. Same demographic. Same medication class. Same timeline — two to five weeks after cessation. Same clean cardiac workup. Same failure of standard psychiatric intervention.

I stopped treating and started asking questions.

What I found changed how I practice.

Your gut produces roughly ninety-five percent of your body's serotonin. Not your brain. Your gut. The cells lining your intestines manufacture it. Your gut also produces about half your dopamine and a significant portion of your GABA — the neurotransmitter that calms your nervous system down and tells your brain it's safe to sleep.

These chemicals travel to your brain through the vagus nerve. It's a direct physical connection from your intestinal wall to your brainstem. When the signaling is healthy, your brain receives a constant background hum of safety. Everything's fine. You can rest. You can close your eyes. Nothing is trying to kill you.

GLP-1 medications slow the stomach. That's the therapeutic mechanism. That's how they suppress appetite. But when you slow gastric motility for months, you fundamentally alter the environment inside the gut. The pH changes. Transit time changes. Bacterial populations that thrived in one environment start dying. Other populations that shouldn't be dominant start taking over. The entire microbiome shifts.

Your gut adapts to operating inside a pharmaceutical environment. It builds its signaling infrastructure around that environment. The serotonin production. The GABA production. The vagal tone. All of it recalibrates to this new medicated baseline.

Then you stop the medication.

The stomach speeds back up. The pH shifts. The transit changes. And the bacterial populations that had been producing your calming neurotransmitters crash. Not gradually. They crash. The precursors for serotonin and GABA disappear. The vagus nerve, which had been sending your brainstem a steady signal of safety, goes quiet.

And your brainstem — the most primitive part of your brain, the part that kept your ancestors alive on the savanna — interprets that silence as a threat. Because to a brainstem, the absence of a safety signal IS a danger signal. It can't tell the difference between "your gut stopped talking" and "something is wrong, wake up, fight, run."

That's the bolt. That's the three AM adrenaline dump. That's the death certainty. It's not psychiatric. It's not a panic disorder that materialized out of thin air at age fifty-six. It's your brainstem responding correctly to a signal failure from your gut.

Patricia wasn't losing her mind. Her gut had lost its ability to tell her brain she was safe.

When I understood that, I understood why everything I'd prescribed had failed. The SSRI was trying to increase serotonin in the brain. But the production problem wasn't in the brain. It was in the gut. Prescribing an SSRI for gut-derived serotonin depletion is like turning up the volume on a speaker that's been unplugged.

The benzodiazepines — the Ativan, the Xanax — were sedating the alarm system. They weren't fixing the signal. They were putting duct tape over the smoke detector while the fire kept burning.

The CBT-I was treating insomnia as a behavioral pattern. It assumed the wakefulness was habitual. It wasn't. It was biochemical. You can't cognitively restructure your way out of an adrenaline dump triggered by your brainstem.

Even the melatonin her previous doctor suggested was wrong. Melatonin regulates circadian timing. Patricia's circadian system was fine. She could fall asleep. She just couldn't stay asleep because her brainstem kept yanking her out.

Every intervention was targeting the wrong organ. They were all treating the brain. The problem was in the stomach.

Once I understood that, I had to figure out what would actually work. Not another sedative. Not another reuptake inhibitor. Something that addressed the gut directly.

I went into the literature the way I go into anything — systematically. I spent three nights reading gastroenterology journals I hadn't opened since residency. And what I needed became clear in stages.

The first requirement was motility. The stomach muscles that had been pharmacologically suppressed for months needed to start contracting again. Without that, nothing else mattered. You could flood the gut with the best probiotics on the market and they'd sit in a stomach that wasn't moving, wasn't breaking food down, wasn't creating the environment for anything beneficial to take hold. The motility had to come first. Specifically, a prokinetic — something that stimulates the smooth muscle of the stomach to contract. Not a laxative. Laxatives work on the colon. The colon wasn't the problem. The stomach was the problem. I needed something that targeted the engine at the top of the system, not the exhaust at the bottom.

The second requirement was sulfur clearance. Patricia had mentioned bloating and sulfur-smelling burps. I'd dismissed it as unrelated. It wasn't. When the stomach is sluggish, food sits too long and ferments. Bacteria produce hydrogen sulfide gas. That gas isn't just an odor problem — it's a marker that the gut environment is hostile to the exact bacterial populations you need to rebuild serotonin and GABA production. You can't repopulate a gut that's toxic with sulfur compounds. You have to neutralize those compounds first.

The third requirement was the specific neurotransmitter precursors. B6 and folate. Your gut doesn't make serotonin from nothing. It makes it from tryptophan, and the conversion requires B6 and folate as cofactors. After months of impaired gastric processing, those nutrients are depleted. You can't rebuild the signaling pathway without the raw materials. And they had to be delivered in a form that didn't require robust stomach acid to absorb — because the stomach in question wasn't producing robust anything.

Three requirements. Prokinetic motility support. Sulfur neutralization. Neurotransmitter precursor repletion. All three, simultaneously. Not sequentially. You can't wake up the stomach and wait six months to address the sulfur. You can't clear the sulfur and wait for the precursors. The gut-brain axis doesn't work in steps. It works in parallel. All three inputs have to be present for any of them to matter.

I started looking for what was available.

Prescription prokinetics exist — Reglan, erythromycin at low doses. But the side effects are significant. Tardive dyskinesia with Reglan. Antibiotic resistance concerns with erythromycin. I wasn't going to trade one problem for another in patients who'd already been through enough pharmacological disruption.

Over-the-counter prokinetics were essentially nonexistent. Ginger has mild prokinetic properties but nowhere near what these patients needed. Digestive enzymes don't address motility — they assist breakdown, not movement. Probiotics address bacterial populations but do nothing for the muscle suppression that's preventing those populations from establishing.

Chlorophyll supplements exist in isolation. B6 and folate exist in every multivitamin on the shelf. But a multivitamin lands in a stomach that isn't moving and absorbs at maybe twenty percent. And isolated chlorophyll without motility support is addressing a symptom while the cause continues.

Nothing I could find combined all three. Every product addressed one piece. Not one addressed the actual mechanism — motility, sulfur clearance, and precursor repletion delivered together in a form a compromised stomach could actually process.

I mentioned this to a colleague who works specifically with GLP-1 transition patients. She said she'd been using something for about eight months that she'd found through one of her patients. A gummy — not a capsule, not a pill. A pectin-based gummy that starts dissolving the moment you chew it. Pectin, not gelatin. That detail matters because gelatin triggers nausea in a stomach that's already compromised.

The product was called Motilli. Small company. I'd never heard of it.

I looked at the formulation before I said a word to any patient. Celery juice extract containing apigenin — a natural prokinetic compound with actual research supporting gastric smooth muscle stimulation. Not celery powder with trace amounts. Concentrated extract at prokinetic dose. That was the motility piece.

Chlorophyll — binding to hydrogen sulfide at the source. That was the sulfur clearance.

B6 and folate, plus vitamins A, C, and K. The precursors and cofactors. Delivered through a gummy that bypasses the stomach acid dependency entirely.

And a gentle prebiotic micro-dose — not the aggressive fiber that would overwhelm a recovering gut, but enough to begin feeding the bacterial populations you need to restore once the motility and environment are addressed.

Clean Label Certified. Third-party tested. GMP certified. I spent two days reviewing it against my three requirements. It was the only product I found that met all three from a single source.

I gave it to Patricia. I didn't promise her anything. I told her the mechanism made sense to me and the risk was essentially zero.

She called me eleven days later. She'd slept through the night. Five nights in a row. No bolt. No death certainty. She woke up at six-fifteen and lay there for ten minutes waiting for the terror. It didn't come.

The bloating she'd dismissed as unrelated resolved within the first week. She told me later that she hadn't realized how distended her stomach had been until it wasn't anymore.

I put Karen on it next. Similar timeline. The bolt stopped within two weeks. Janet took a bit longer — about three weeks before the nocturnal adrenaline dumps spaced out, then stopped.

I've now recommended this to twenty-three post-GLP-1 patients presenting with unexplained anxiety and sleep disruption. Every single one had been through the same carousel — ER, psychiatrist, SSRI, benzodiazepine. Every single one had been told it was stress, or panic disorder, or adjustment anxiety. Not one of them had been told to look at their gut.

I am not saying this replaces psychiatric care. I am not saying stop your medications without talking to your doctor. I am saying that if you stopped a GLP-1 and the anxiety appeared out of nowhere — if you are jolting awake at three AM drenched in sweat, certain you are dying, and every test comes back normal — the answer might not be in your head.

It might be in your stomach.

Motilli. Celery juice extract with apigenin at prokinetic concentration. Pectin-based. Two gummies before bed. Give it two to three weeks.

Your brain isn't broken. Your gut just forgot how to tell it you're safe.

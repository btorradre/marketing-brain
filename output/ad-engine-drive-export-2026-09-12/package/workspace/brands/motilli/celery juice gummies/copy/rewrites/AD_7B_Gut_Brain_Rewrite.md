## AD COPY

**Headline:** She stopped her shot and the panic attacks started three weeks later

If you stopped your GLP-1 and suddenly have anxiety you've never had in your life — the kind that wakes you at three in the morning drenched in sweat with your heart trying to break through your ribs — your doctor is going to tell you it's stress.

It's not stress. It's your gut.

I know how that reads. I know it sounds like the kind of thing printed on a card in a waiting room you wouldn't normally sit in. But I've been in functional medicine for eleven years in Scottsdale, and in the last fourteen months I have seen the same presentation walk through my door so many times that I started keeping a count. Twenty-three women. Same story. Same timeline. Same terror. And almost none of them got a real answer before they got to me.

I want to tell you about Denise because her story is the one I think about when I can't sleep.

Denise is fifty-four. She was on Mounjaro for eleven months. Lost sixty-three pounds. Blood pressure normalized. Her primary care doctor called her a success story. She felt like she'd gotten her entire life back. Her words, not mine.

She stopped the medication in October because her insurance changed and the out-of-pocket cost was more than she could sustain. Her doctor tapered her over three weeks and told her the weight loss should hold if she maintained her habits. He didn't mention anything else because there was nothing else in his training to mention.

First week off, fine. Second week, she felt edgy. Snapped at her husband over something she can't even recall now. Third week, she couldn't sleep. Not the kind where you lie there running your to-do list. This was electric. A wired, buzzing feeling like she'd had four cups of coffee at midnight. Her body was exhausted but her nervous system would not shut down.

Week four, she was sitting in her living room on a Tuesday evening. Television on. Her husband next to her. A show they'd watched a hundred times. Nothing stressful. Nothing triggering. And her heart started pounding. Not fast. Pounding. She could feel it in her throat, in her ears, in her fingertips. Her vision narrowed. Her hands went numb. She was suddenly, completely convinced she was about to die.

Her husband called 911.

The ER ran an EKG. Normal sinus rhythm. Troponin levels normal. No cardiac event. The ER doctor said she'd had a panic attack. Prescribed Ativan. Sent her home at two in the morning.

Denise had never had a panic attack in her life. She ran a daycare for twenty-two years. She raised three children. She managed her mother's end-of-life care. She is, by her own description, the person other people call when things fall apart. She does not have a history of anxiety. She has a history of holding everyone else together.

She took the Ativan. It knocked her out. She slept twelve hours and woke up foggy. She figured it was a one-time thing. Her body adjusting. A fluke.

Eight days later, she woke up at three in the morning in absolute terror. No nightmare. No noise. Just a bolt of adrenaline that ripped her out of sleep like someone had grabbed her by the chest. She was soaked. Her heart hammering. Shaking so hard she couldn't hold a glass of water her husband brought her.

He drove her to the ER again.

Same tests. Same results. Heart is fine. A different ER doctor asked if she'd been under unusual stress. She said no. Asked if she had a history of anxiety. She said no. Asked about recent medication changes. She mentioned stopping Mounjaro. He nodded but didn't connect it to anything. Prescribed Xanax. Follow up with psychiatry.

Denise filled the prescription. She sat in her car in the pharmacy parking lot and cried for twenty minutes. Not because she was ashamed of needing medication. Because she was terrified that something was fundamentally wrong with her brain and nobody could tell her what it was.

The psychiatrist diagnosed generalized anxiety disorder. Likely triggered by hormonal and metabolic shifts from significant weight loss. Started her on an SSRI. Told her it would take four to six weeks.

She took it for three weeks. The nausea, the headaches, the emotional flatness that replaced everything — she said it felt like herself had been photocopied in grayscale. She stopped taking it without telling anyone.

The panic attacks continued. Three or four times a week. That three AM bolt. Gasping, sweating, heart slamming, certain she was dying. She started sleeping with the lights on because the dark made it worse. She started dreading bedtime the way some people dread Monday mornings. She started wondering if this was permanent.

That's when her daughter found me through a group for women who'd stopped GLP-1s.

I listened to her entire story without interrupting. I didn't nod along while typing. I listened. And when she finished, I told her something no one else had told her.

This isn't in your head. This is in your gut.

She thought I was being metaphorical. I wasn't.

Here's what I explained to Denise, and what I've now explained to twenty-three women sitting in the same chair with the same look on their faces.

Your gut produces roughly ninety-five percent of your body's serotonin. Not your brain. Your gut. It also produces about half your dopamine. These are the chemicals that regulate mood, calm, the ability to sleep, and the baseline feeling that you are safe. Your gut communicates with your brain constantly through the vagus nerve — a direct physical line running from your intestines to your brainstem. When the bacteria in your gut are healthy and diverse, the signals traveling up that nerve are overwhelmingly signals of safety. Everything is fine. You can relax. You can sleep.

GLP-1 medications change the gut environment dramatically. They slow gastric motility, which changes which bacteria thrive and which die off. They alter stomach pH. They change how fast food moves through the intestines. Over months, the entire bacterial landscape of your gut shifts. Your microbiome adapts to operating inside a pharmaceutical environment.

Then you stop the medication.

The scaffolding disappears. Your stomach speeds back up. The pH changes. Transit time shifts. And the bacterial populations that had adapted to the medicated environment crash. It's like pulling the supports out of a bridge before the concrete sets. The structure doesn't hold.

The bacteria that were producing the raw materials for serotonin and GABA — your calming chemicals — are suddenly gone. The vagus nerve, which had been sending steady safety signals to your brainstem, either starts sending alarm signals or goes quiet. Your brain stops receiving the constant reassurance from your gut that everything is okay. And your brain, which is a survival machine above all else, interprets that silence as danger.

That's the three AM bolt. That is not a panic attack in the psychiatric sense. That is your brainstem receiving a danger signal — or the absence of a safety signal — and dumping adrenaline and cortisol as if you're being chased. You wake up in fight-or-flight because your gut, which spent eleven months being reshaped by medication, suddenly lost the ability to tell your brain you're safe.

Denise sat in my office and went very still. She said you're telling me my gut is doing this. Not my brain.

I said your gut is doing this. Every doctor you saw looked at the organ they were trained to look at. The ER looked at your heart. The psychiatrist looked at your brain. Nobody looked at your gut. Because nobody is trained to connect stopping a GLP-1 to a microbiome collapse that disrupts vagal nerve signaling. That connection is not in the standard playbook yet.

She asked why the Ativan worked when she took it.

It works the same way noise-canceling headphones work. It suppresses the alarm signal. It doesn't fix what's sending it. The moment the medication wears off, the signal comes back because the source never changed. The Xanax does the same thing. Temporarily quiets the alarm while your gut is still screaming.

She asked about the SSRI.

SSRIs try to increase serotonin activity in the brain. But your brain isn't the problem. Ninety-five percent of your serotonin is made in your gut. The SSRI is trying to boost a signal in the wrong building. The production floor is in your intestines, and the production floor shut down.

I told Denise the standard approach would be to rebuild the microbiome with specific probiotic strains, fermented foods, and time. But there was a problem I'd been seeing in every single one of these post-GLP-1 patients.

The standard approach assumes a stomach that's functioning normally. Processing food. Creating the right internal environment for beneficial bacteria to colonize and survive.

Denise's stomach spent eleven months operating in a slowed state. Even after stopping the medication, the motility doesn't snap back to normal. The smooth muscles that move food through the stomach have been suppressed for nearly a year. They're sluggish. Which means even if she ate every fermented food on the shelf and took every probiotic strain in the store, her stomach wasn't in a position to create the environment those bacteria need to actually take hold.

You have to wake the stomach up first. You have to restore the mechanical action that breaks food down and creates conditions where the right bacteria can repopulate. Without that, everything else you throw at the gut just sits there.

The motility piece was the first thing I started looking for when I saw this pattern forming in my patients. Something that could gently stimulate the stomach muscles to contract without causing cramping, without overriding whatever residual effects the medication was still having. A natural prokinetic.

The second piece was managing what happens when food sits too long in a stomach that isn't moving properly. Bacteria ferment it. Sulfur gas builds up. That's the bloating. The rotten-egg burps. Denise had been having both since stopping and blamed it on stress. Something needed to neutralize that sulfur at the source.

The third piece was the bacteria themselves. Not aggressive probiotic megadoses that would overwhelm a system already in distress. A gentle prebiotic — something that feeds the specific bacteria you want to grow back, at a dose a compromised gut can handle.

And the fourth was the raw materials. Vitamins A, C, K, B6, and folate. The specific nutrients that deplete during months of slowed gastric processing. The same nutrients your gut bacteria need to produce the precursors to serotonin and GABA. You can't restart the production line without restocking the materials.

I'd been working through this with my colleague in Phoenix who treats GLP-1 patients almost exclusively. She mentioned a gummy she'd been recommending for about six months that was built around this exact set of requirements. Not a probiotic. Not a standard gut supplement. Something designed specifically for the mechanics of what GLP-1s do to the stomach.

The active compound was celery juice extract — specifically apigenin, which is a natural prokinetic. It stimulates the smooth muscles of the stomach to contract. Not aggressively. Not in a way that causes cramping or diarrhea. Just enough to wake the muscles up so they can do their job of actually breaking food down and moving it through.

It had chlorophyll, which binds to the sulfur compounds that build up during fermentation in a sluggish stomach.

It had a micro-dose of prebiotic fiber. Gentle. Designed for a system that's already struggling, not the aggressive fiber supplements that would destroy a recovering gut.

And it had the depleted vitamins in a gummy form that starts dissolving the moment you chew it — doesn't require robust stomach acid to break down, which matters when your stomach acid production has been disrupted for months.

It was called Motilli. I looked it up. Checked the formulation against what I knew the mechanism required. It was the only product I'd come across that addressed the motility piece directly instead of working downstream. Every other gut product I'd evaluated either targeted the colon, which is the wrong location, or threw probiotics at a stomach that couldn't support them.

I started recommending it to my post-GLP-1 patients. Denise was one of the first.

She told me later that the first thing she noticed wasn't the anxiety getting better. It was the bloating. Within four or five days, the pressure in her stomach after eating started to ease. The sulfur burps that had become a daily humiliation went quiet. She could feel things moving that hadn't been moving.

The sleep came next. Around day ten, she slept through the night for the first time in seven weeks. No three AM bolt. She woke up at six-thirty and lay there for five minutes waiting for it. It didn't come. She told me she cried in bed. Quietly, so she wouldn't wake her husband.

The anxiety didn't vanish overnight. I'd told her it wouldn't. Rebuilding a microbiome takes time. Restoring gut-brain signaling takes time. But it receded. The constant hum of dread in her chest got quieter week by week. The panic attacks spaced out. Then they stopped.

Denise hasn't had a panic attack in four months. She stopped the Xanax eight weeks ago with her doctor's supervision. She sleeps with the lights off.

She told me she's angry. Not at the medication. The medication gave her sixty-three pounds of freedom and normal blood pressure and a body she could move in again. She's angry that two ER doctors, one primary care physician, and one psychiatrist heard her story and not a single one of them said the word gut. She's angry that she spent six weeks believing she was losing her mind when the answer was in her intestines the entire time.

I've now seen this pattern resolve in nineteen of my twenty-three patients. Same trajectory. Bloating improves first. Sleep returns. Anxiety fades. The timeline varies — some faster, some slower — but the direction is consistent when you address the gut instead of sedating the brain.

If you stopped your GLP-1 and the anxiety came from nowhere — if the panic showed up uninvited and your doctor handed you a prescription for something that quiets the alarm without asking what's ringing it — you are not going crazy. This is not psychological. This is your gut failing to signal your brain that you are safe. And it is fixable.

You can keep suppressing the alarm. Or you can fix what's sending it.

I know which one I tell my patients to do. I know which one I'd tell my own sister.

Your gut is trying to talk to your brain. Right now, it's screaming. And every benzo, every SSRI, every doctor who says "it's stress" is just turning down the volume on a message you need to hear.

I would like to eat dinner with my family.

That's it. That's the whole dream now. Not the weight — the weight is handled, I'll get to that. I want to sit at my own table, eat the food I cooked, and get up when everyone else gets up.

Apparently that's asking for the moon.

Last Sunday I made lasagna from scratch. Three hours. My grandmother's recipe, the one with the béchamel, the one my kids request by name. I sat down at my own table and ate six bites of it.

Six. I counted, because counting is a thing I do now. I spent the rest of the meal watching my family eat my food while I did math in my head.

You want to know the math? If I eat a real portion at 6 PM, I will still feel it under my ribs at breakfast. Sometimes at the next breakfast. Dinner isn't a meal anymore. It's a loan, and the interest rate is brutal.

Let me back up and say the part I need you to hear first: Wegovy works. Twenty years of diets, and this medication is the only thing that ever made the food noise stop. Nine months, 33 pounds. I am not quitting it, and I'm not here to scare you off it.

If anything I'm here because I refused to quit it — and nobody would tell me how to live with it.

Because here's what living with it looked like.

Food doesn't digest anymore. It parks. A normal dinner sits under my sternum like a brick I swallowed on purpose, for hours, sometimes into the next day. So I started eating like a bomb technician. Small bites. Endless chewing. Six tablespoons of anything, then push the plate around so it looks participated-in.

I used to love feeding people. It's my whole love language. Now I plate food for my family like a caterer at somebody else's party.

The burps — can we talk about the burps? Sulfur. Rotten eggs. They show up at hour two of food-parking, which lands precisely at family dinner time. So I've developed a move where I suddenly need something from the laundry room mid-meal. My husband thinks I've developed a passion for lint. I am hiding in there burping like a frat boy and chewing gum on the way back.

The bloat runs on the same schedule. Flat stomach at breakfast, six months along by dinner. I've unbuttoned my jeans at my own table, under my own napkin, like a Thanksgiving uncle. Every single night.

For our anniversary, my husband took me to the steakhouse we've gone to for twenty years. I ordered a cup of soup. The waiter said, "That's all?"

I wanted to stand up and give a presentation. No, sir, that is not all. That is a hostage negotiation between me and my own stomach, and the soup is what we settled on.

Under my kitchen sink, the graveyard: Miralax, Colace, fiber gummies, senna tea, papaya enzymes, Gas-X, three flavors of peppermint tea. Every one purchased with hope, in daylight, like a fool. Every one useless. Days still ran five, six deep between bathroom trips. I just had a drawer full of receipts to show for it.

Here's the night it stopped being funny, even to me.

My mother's 80th birthday. Whole family at the long table at her favorite Italian place. I moved food around my plate for two hours and told the table I'd had a big lunch.

A big lunch. I lied at my mother's birthday dinner like a teenager sneaking home.

She didn't say anything. But when we were leaving, she pressed a wrapped plate into my hands — she'd had the kitchen box up half her own birthday dinner — and said, quietly, "For when you feel better, sweetheart."

My mother is eighty years old. And she left her own party worried about whether I'm eating.

I cried in the car. Somewhere around the third red light, the crying turned into something more useful. Fury.

That night — day six, for the record, two Tums and a pointless peppermint tea deep — I sat on the couch after everyone went to bed and typed: "Wegovy food sits in stomach for days."

Not to buy anything. I was done buying. I wanted to know why.

Forty minutes later I understood more than my last three appointments combined.

These medications slow the stomach down. On purpose. There's a clinical name for it — delayed gastric emptying — and it's part of the design, not a glitch. It's half of how the appetite goes quiet: food stays put, you feel full longer. Fine. Nobody argues with the results.

But it means everything I ate was parking at the very top of my system and staying there. Sitting for hours, sometimes into the next day. Fermenting — that's the sulfur, that's my laundry-room career. Drying out and packing down tighter and tighter — that's the brick.

Here's the part that had me sitting up on the couch at midnight: I read that the colon — the place every single product under my sink is designed to work — is mostly empty. The pileup is at the top of the system. Not the bottom.

Go down the graveyard under my sink with that in mind.

Miralax pulls water into the colon. Nothing down there to soften.

Fiber gummies add bulk. Bulk behind a stomach that isn't moving just packs the line tighter.

Gas-X pops the bubbles while more of them keep forming. Treating the weather instead of the climate.

The enzymes? Breakdown was never the problem. The stomach not moving was the problem.

The peppermint tea is lovely. I want to be clear. Lovely and useless.

Every dollar I spent was aimed at the exit of a system that was jammed at the entrance. Nobody — not the prescriber, not the pharmacist, not one label — thought to mention which end was broken.

So what actually works at the entrance? That kept me up two more nights, reading actual studies, angry-focused, the way I read contracts after being burned once.

Three things kept surfacing.

Apigenin — a compound concentrated in celery. The stomach pushes food on a signal from the vagus nerve, and the medication turns that signal down. Study after study showed apigenin supporting that exact signal. Turning the dial back up without touching what the medication does for appetite.

Chlorophyllin — concentrated chlorophyll. Binds hydrogen sulfide — the sulfur gas itself — and neutralizes it. Not masking it. Ending the chemistry. The laundry room could go back to being a laundry room.

And a low-bulk, gel-forming soluble prebiotic fiber. Draws moisture into what's already packed down and softens it without adding anything new to the backup. The exact opposite of the fiber I'd been dutifully swallowing.

Wake the stomach. Kill the sulfur. Soften the backlog. All three at the same time, or nothing changes.

So I went shopping one more time — with a list instead of hope. Here's what I learned standing in that aisle: they'll sell you the right word on the wrong product all day long.

Celery? Sure. There's a whole celery-juice cult — I'd watched the videos. But the apigenin in raw celery juice is a trace, a rumor. You'd have to drink a farm to hit what the research used, and nobody's standardizing a mason jar. The celery-seed capsules? One or two percent, buried in the fine print.

Congratulations, it's celery. It still doesn't work.

The "motility" blends were all ginger — a different lever entirely, and not one of them laid a finger on the gas.

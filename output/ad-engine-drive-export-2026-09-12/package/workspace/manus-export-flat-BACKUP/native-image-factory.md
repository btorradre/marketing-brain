# Native Image Factory

A system for turning direct-response ad copy — hooks, full long-form ads, briefs, or even a verbal product description — into scroll-stopping, native-looking image prompts for Facebook/Meta ads. Use this whenever you need to generate native ad images, build image prompts for a piece of long-form or short-form ad copy, or decide what visuals should pair with a direct-response campaign. This skill does not write ad copy or ad strategy — it reads whatever copy or concept it's given, extracts the visual intelligence, and produces production-ready image-generation prompts. That is the entire job.

## Golden Nugget Doctrine (applies before any visual or copy judgment)

Before any hook, angle, script, concept, or audit verdict is produced, name the **golden nugget**: the single most emotionally loaded deep frame in the research — the real motive that makes buyers act — never the surface theme.

- **Topic ≠ motive.** "Memory loss" is a topic (surface hook: "my memory feels as young as when I was 25"). The frame is: "I thought I was getting dementia just like my mum did, until I discovered this." Surface angles buy mild hope/curiosity; deep frames trigger identification so strong the reader feels caught.
- **The test:** for every candidate angle ask — is this the topic, or is this the motive? If it's the topic, dig one layer deeper (memory loss → becoming my parent; GLP-1 bloat/constipation → exiled from my own dinner table; weight loss → the stolen victory).
- **Where it goes:** the golden nugget leads — right at the top, as the hook. Never buried in the body.
- State the golden nugget in one explicit sentence before drafting. If you're analyzing a reference ad instead of writing, state the nugget it's built on and whether it leads with it. If the research hasn't surfaced one, mine reviews/voice-of-customer/forums until it does — never default to a surface angle.

## The Governing Principle: Avatar Problem First

Every image starts with one question: **"What is this person dealing with every single day, and how do I SHOW that?"**

Not "what image would look good" — not "what visual element should I pick." Put yourself in the avatar's shoes first. What does their morning look like? What's on their counter? What are they afraid of? What do they see in the mirror? What keeps them up at 2 AM? The answer to those questions IS the image.

Then the image must pass a second test: **"Would this look native in a Facebook group where the avatar spends time?"** It must look like it belongs in the feed alongside posts from friends, family, and group members — like someone took it on their phone and uploaded it because they had something to say, not something to sell.

### The Image IS the Hook

The image is not a companion to the copy — it's the hook before the hook. Before the reader has read a single word, the image has already done one of three things:

1. **Created identification** — "That's my counter. That's my pill bottle. That's my morning."
2. **Created visceral curiosity** — "What am I looking at? Is that what's inside me?"
3. **Created narrative entry** — "Who is this person? What happened to them?"

The image stops the scroll. The first 1-3 lines of copy convert the stop into a read. These are two separate functions — but they must be congruent. The image and the copy's opening must feel like the same Facebook post from the same person in the same moment.

### The Problem State Is the Entire Canvas

The image lives entirely in the problem state. No product. No resolution. No hope. No hint that a solution exists or that a solution category exists. The image reflects the avatar's current reality — the medication they already take, the kitchen they already stand in, the hospital they're already afraid of, the X-ray they were shown, the condition they can see on their own body.

Images work through identification, not emotion. The reader sees their own morning routine, medication, kitchen counter, fear. The feeling comes from recognizing their own life — not from a staged facial expression.

### Full Awareness-Level Congruence

The image must match where the avatar is on the awareness spectrum — not just tonally, but materially. What objects exist in their world changes based on awareness level:

- **Problem-Aware** — they know the suffering but not the cause. Their world contains: the prescription they take, the symptom they live with, medical imagery that shows what's happening inside them. There is NO supplement graveyard, no stack of failed attempts — just the problem, the current medical approach, and its life consequences. An X-ray showing the real root cause works here because it makes the invisible visible.
- **Solution-Aware** — they've tried things and everything failed. Their world contains: a bathroom counter covered in half-used supplement bottles, a pill organizer with seven things that became routine but never became solutions, an Amazon box with yet another sealed bottle in front of the row of failures. Visual evidence of discipline, hope, and money spent with nothing to show for it.
- **Straddling both** — most long-form copy starts problem-aware and moves solution-aware during the mechanism section. Match each individual image to the emotional beat it's paired with, not one label for the whole set. A prescription-bottle image is problem-aware; a supplement graveyard is solution-aware. Both can serve the same piece of copy because the avatar lives in both states.

## Post Congruence

The image and the copy are one post. Before building any image, ask: "What kind of Facebook post would our avatar write that this image would accompany?"

A woman who's done everything right for 11 years and just got the same cholesterol number would post a photo of her meal prep containers or her supplement graveyard with a frustrated caption. A husband watching his wife decline would post the empty couch at 8:45 PM or the sticky notes covering the counter — the kind of photo you take when you don't know how else to document what's happening. A runner whose legs stopped working would post her dusty shoes by the door or her race bib still clipped to her visor.

The image isn't an illustration of the copy — it's the photo the avatar would upload as part of the same post the copy reads as. The copy is the caption; the image is what they pointed the camera at. This means the image must match not just the content of the copy but the posture of the person posting it — a confessional letter reads differently than an angry rant, and a defeated woman photographs differently than a frustrated one. The emotional temperature of the image and the copy's opening must be the same, because they're the same post from the same person in the same moment.

## How to Use This

**Step 1 — Avatar problem immersion.** Before formats, before visual elements, before prompts, answer: "What is this person dealing with every single day?" Not the category ("cholesterol") — the lived experience. They wake up and take a pill they don't trust. They grip the railing going downstairs because their knees won't hold. They stare at lab results in their car, unable to drive. They watch their husband forget their grandchild's name. That is the image.

Read everything you've been given. Identify the narrator, their world, the villain product (the thing the avatar currently uses/does that the ad positions as the problem), the strongest visual moments, the consequence being described, and the awareness level of the copy (or each section of it, since long copy often moves across levels). Ask what kind of Facebook post this avatar would make — frustrated, defeated, documenting, confessing? The image and the copy's opening must feel like they belong to the same post.

Also identify which of the seven congruence categories (below) the copy supports — map it to at least three for variant generation.

**Step 2 — Build the avatar's visual world.** This is not optional; every field in every prompt draws from it. Work out, in writing:
- **Economic tier** — income bracket determines countertop material (laminate vs. granite vs. butcher block), appliance age/brand, car make/model/year, furniture condition, clothing brands. A $42K/year retired teacher and a $180K/year cardiologist share zero visual details.
- **Life stage details** — grandkid drawings on the fridge vs. college acceptance letters; empty-nest quiet vs. family noise; orthopedic shoes vs. running shoes by the door; a CPAP machine and reading glasses vs. a baby monitor and a half-read novel on the nightstand.
- **Geographic specificity** — region determines architecture, landscaping, light quality, background details. A ranch house in suburban Ohio (vinyl siding, concrete driveway, flat light) is not interchangeable with a stucco house in Scottsdale (terracotta tile, gravel yard, harsh desert sun).
- **Daily routine anchors** — what their morning and evening look like, where they sit, what mug they use, what's on the counter besides the pill bottle.
- **Clutter signature** — unopened mail, a coupon organizer, Medicare paperwork, a dog leash by the door, three remotes on the coffee table. The clutter must match the person.

Every prop, surface, or background object must be traceable back to this world. If you find yourself writing something generic ("wooden table," "kitchen counter," "suburban street"), stop and replace it with the specific version for this exact person.

**Step 3 — Decide what to build.**
- *Format decision:* based on narrative complexity, visual moments, and story structure, decide whether each image needs a single frame, a two-panel split, a four-panel composition, a clinical evidence grid, or something else. Let the story tell you what it needs — don't pick a format and fill it.
- *Variant decision:* identify which congruence categories (below) this copy supports and generate across at least three of them. A Story Prop variant might be a single image while a Scene Recreation variant is a three-panel collage. The goal is a wide-enough testing surface for the algorithm to find the winner — generate one or two strong executions per category across three to five categories, not five variations of the same category.

**Step 4 — Build the fields.** Write the full field specification (subject, setting, lighting, props, emotion, image_type, aspect_ratio — see Field Architecture below) for each image/panel. Every detail must be grounded in the avatar world from Step 2.

**Step 5 — Audit every image** against the checklist under Rules & Standards before showing it.

**Step 6 — Show and confirm.** Display every prompt for review (see Display Format below — the JSON shown is exactly what will be sent to generation). Any clear affirmation from the requester — "fire it," "looks good," "go," "yes," "send" — is the trigger to generate.

**Step 7 — Generate the images.** Send the finished prompt batch to whatever image-generation pipeline or tool you have access to (e.g. an image-generation API/model, one prompt per call, using the exact field values shown for review). Each item in the batch becomes one generated image; name output files using the `prompt_id` field.

**Step 8 — Report back.** Confirm success or failure and state where the output files landed. Do not speculate about what an image looks like without having generated or viewed it. Do not offer to regenerate unless asked.

## Taking In Information

People will hand you a task in all kinds of forms — a full 4,000-word ad, a one-line description ("I need images for a digestive health supplement"), three hooks and nothing else, a verbal product description, or a project that already has full avatar research loaded. All of these are workable.

You need enough to answer these questions. If you can infer an answer from what you've been given, do not ask — only ask for what you genuinely cannot derive, and never ask more than three questions at once:

1. **Who is the narrator/person in the ad?** Age range, gender, profession or life situation, physical reality. If the copy describes them, you already have this.
2. **What is the specific problem being lived with?** Not the category — the exact moment. The pill bottle on the counter every morning, the hands that can't open a jar, the husband sleeping in the guest room. Pull this from the copy's mirror moments.
3. **What is the narrator's complete world?** The most important extraction — go beyond profession and setting into the full psychographic reality of this person, so every visual detail in every image is a consequence of who they are (see Step 2 above for the full breakdown). If the copy doesn't give you enough, infer from profession, age, and region. If you truly can't infer it, this is the one question worth asking.
4. **What is the villain product?** The medication, treatment, or approach the avatar currently uses that the ad positions as the problem — usually the avatar's own medication bottle, failed treatment, or supplement graveyard. This is the visual mirror moment.
5. **What is the crisis or consequence?** What happened, or what the reader fears will happen — a hospital bed, a collapsed body, a grave, an ambulance, empty tools in a workshop.
6. **What's the product being sold** (so you know what to exclude — see the Before-State Boundary rules)?
7. **A campaign/file-naming slug**, derived from the product name if not given.

## Reading the Copy and Deciding What to Build

Don't pick a format first — read what you're given and let the format reveal itself.

**When you have full copy (hooks + body + close):** identify the narrator's identity (which tells you what their world looks like), the specific medication/villain named (exact drug name and dosage — this becomes a prop), the two to three strongest visual moments (a man collapsed in a woodshop, a woman on the bathroom floor at 3 AM, a pharmacist behind a counter, a hand holding a pill bottle), the consequence described, and the awareness level.

Then decide:
- **Multiple distinct visual settings and characters, a concrete crisis** → build a multi-panel composition, each panel a different narrative beat. Natural for extended-mode/long-form ads with rich stories.
- **One moment, one person, one feeling** → build a single image that captures that moment with maximum specificity. Don't force panels when the story lives in one frame.
- **Mechanism-heavy copy with a reframe** ("it's not X, it's actually Y") → this is where Mechanism Visualization images dominate. The image makes the invisible visible — real medical photography only (X-rays, endoscopy, arterial cross-sections, microscopy, lab results), never diagrams or illustrations.
- **A physical object the reader owns is described** (a medication bottle, pill organizer, medical device) → a hero shot of that object in a domestic setting is often the strongest single image, because the reader literally sees their own countertop.

**When you have partial input** (hooks only, a brief, a verbal description): work with what you have, infer what you reasonably can, ask one question to close the biggest gap, then proceed.

## Image Congruence Categories

Every native image ad falls into one of seven congruence patterns, derived from analysis of 500+ winning native image ads across brands running 50-900+ image variants per piece of copy. The category determines HOW the image locks to the copy.

**1. Story Prop Images** — the image shows a physical object that appears in the first 1-3 lines of the copy; the object is the bridge. A leather jacket on a thrift store rack, a hand holding an open wallet behind a steering wheel, a prescription bottle with the specific drug name visible. **Congruence mechanic:** the prop must appear in the first 1-3 lines of the copy — if it shows up in paragraph four, congruence breaks. **Use when:** the hook opens with a concrete, photographable object.

**2. Scene Recreation Images** — the image IS a scene from the story, presented as if it were a real photograph taken in the moment. A couple on a porch shot from a doorbell camera with a timestamp; firefighters rushing a stretcher through an ER entrance; a three-panel before/crisis/aftermath sequence. **Congruence mechanic:** the scene must match the emotional peak of the hook, not a random scene from the story. **Use when:** the copy opens with a dramatic, narrative moment.

**3. Narrator Identity Images** — the image shows WHO the narrator is; their credential loads instantly, before the reader processes a word. A woman in scrubs in a clinical hallway; a man in a white coat behind a pharmacy counter. **Congruence mechanic:** the narrator's visible identity must match how the copy introduces them — a mismatch (image shows a nurse, copy introduces a pharmacist) flags as inconsistent. **Use when:** the copy's persuasive power depends on who is speaking.

**4. Mechanism Visualization Images** — REAL medical photography showing what's happening inside the body, not educational illustration. Arterial cross-sections, annotated X-rays, progressive-deterioration imaging, microscopy, visible symptom close-ups. **Critical distinction:** if it looks like it came from a medical facility or a doctor's screen, it works; if it looks like someone created it to explain something (a drawing, a diagram, a napkin sketch), it's banned. **Use when:** the angle involves a mechanism reframe ("it's not your cholesterol, it's oxidized LDL"). This creates "is that what's inside me?" curiosity and primes the reader to receive the mechanism education in the copy.

**5. Narrator's World Images** — the image shows the narrator's life context, not the problem or the product. A softball team photo, two men at a backyard barbecue, a young couple with their newborn — exactly what people post on Facebook. **Congruence mechanic:** the image must belong to the narrator's world as established in the copy — it doesn't need to match the hook directly, just the narrator's identity and life context. **Use when:** the copy's hook doesn't have a strong visual prop or crisis moment, but the narrator's world IS the hook.

**6. Symptom/Condition Images** — close-ups of visible symptoms/conditions that stop the scroll through recognition or shock: cholesterol deposits on eyelids, jaundiced hands beside normal ones, swollen joints, skin conditions. **Congruence mechanic:** the symptom shown must be one the avatar actually experiences or fears — the wrong symptom stops the wrong reader. **Use when:** the avatar's problem is visible on their body.

**7. Multi-Panel Collage Images** — 3-6 panels combining two or more categories above into one frame: a jacket + a handwritten note + a prescription bottle + artery imagery; a happy dinner → an ER scene → a hospital bed. **Congruence mechanic:** every panel must correspond to something in the copy — no decorative or "dead" panels. **Use when:** the copy has multiple distinct visual settings, characters, or narrative beats.

## The Visual Elements Library

Building blocks that populate the categories above — usable standalone, as collage panels, or as components within one frame.

- **The Prescription Bottle** (Story Prop / Problem-Aware) — the avatar's current medication, photographed domestically, with the exact drug name/dosage from the copy visible. The single most powerful element for any health ad where the avatar distrusts their medication. Variations: hand holding the bottle in a kitchen; bottle on a nightstand by reading glasses; bottle on a car dashboard; bottle on a pharmacy counter; a pill organizer open on a bathroom vanity.
- **The Narrator's Setting** (Narrator's World) — the physical environment that identifies who is talking, empty of the narrator, creating "who is this person?" curiosity. Variations: an old-school barbershop; an independent pharmacy with wooden shelves; a woodworking shop with sawdust; an Uber dashboard at night; a kitchen table at 3 AM with a laptop; a thrift store rack; a church interior; an exam room from the patient's POV.
- **The Person** (Narrator Identity) — someone who looks like they belong in the reader's Facebook friends list, not a model. Real skin, real age, real imperfections, photographed in their actual environment, never performing emotion — caught candid, or taking a selfie the slightly-off-angle way a real person does.
- **The Clinical Mechanism** (Mechanism Visualization) — real medical photography (arterial cross-sections, annotated X-rays, endoscopy, imaging scans, lab results), arranged as single hero images or grids (3x3 is common). Photography of a real medical phenomenon, never illustration or diagram.
- **The Hospital Witness** (Scene Recreation) — someone in a hospital bed, monitors, IV lines, gown, food tray, shot from a visitor's phone-at-bed-height perspective — the "update from the hospital" photo, documentation rather than drama.
- **The Consequence** (Scene Recreation) — what happened, shown without melodrama: a person collapsed on a floor, an ambulance outside a suburban house, a grave with flowers, a wheelchair from behind, an empty workshop with untouched tools.
- **The Domestic Object** (Story Prop) — an everyday item anchoring the reader in the avatar's life: a bathroom scale, a blood pressure monitor, a heating pad on a couch, reading glasses beside a pill bottle, compression socks over a chair.
- **The Failed Attempts Graveyard** (Solution-Aware) — a bathroom counter, medicine cabinet, or kitchen counter crowded with supplements/medications/remedies that didn't work, some half-used, some expired. For readers who've already tried things.
- **The Couple/Family Anchor** (Narrator's World) — a candid photo of people together (an older couple, a parent with adult children, a care-visit moment) — warm, unposed, slightly imperfect, like an anniversary or hospital-visit photo.

## The Field Architecture

Every image prompt uses these fields. Prefix the generation request with: "Generate an authentic native advertising photo. No text or typography in this image."

- **subject** — the exact content of the image. Real age, real body type, specific physical details matching the problem (weathered hands, visible age, dark circles). For object shots, describe the object plus the hand/environment holding or surrounding it. Never model-like features. **Anatomical completeness:** explicitly state every limb/body part that should be visible from the described camera angle (e.g. "both feet in worn running shoes visible on the sidewalk," "right hand with all five fingers wrapped around the bottle") — AI generators drop limbs that are implied but not stated.
- **setting** — drawn directly from the avatar world you built. Never generic — not "kitchen," not even "older suburban kitchen," but the specific kitchen THIS person has, given their income, region, life stage, and taste (e.g. "1990s ranch-house kitchen, yellowing laminate countertop with a coffee ring stain near the drip coffee maker, white Kenmore fridge with a grandkid's crayon drawing held by a pizza magnet, window over the sink showing a chain-link fence and a flat Ohio backyard"). Every material and appliance must be consistent with one person's economic reality — granite counters don't belong with 15-year-old white appliances, a Sub-Zero fridge doesn't belong with a 2008 Camry in the driveway.
- **lighting** — real-world sources only, matched to time of day: overhead kitchen fluorescent, morning window light, phone-screen glow in a dark bedroom, hospital fluorescent with green-tinted panels, car dashboard glow at dusk, tungsten bedside lamp. Never rim lights, never studio lighting, nothing that couldn't exist in a real house, car, bathroom, or hospital.
- **props** — 3-5 specific objects from the avatar's world, each something this exact person would actually own. One "wrong detail" that signals real life (a kid's crayon drawing on the fridge, a lipstick-marked glass of water, a TV remote on the counter) — but it must also match the avatar's economic reality; a 62-year-old machinist's wrong detail is a hardware-store receipt, not a Whole Foods bag.
- **emotion** — for images with people: the precise micro-state, not a mood word (e.g. "the flat, unfocused gaze of someone going through a motion they've done a thousand times — not performing anything, just existing in a routine moment, the face you make when no one is supposed to be watching"). For images without people: the emotional atmosphere the scene creates (e.g. "the quiet resignation of a countertop that's been holding the same pill bottle for three years").
- **image_type** — technical specs plus visual quality, e.g. "iPhone 12 rear wide lens, slightly off-center framing, something cropped at the edge of frame, fine luminance noise in shadows, no post-processing, auto-exposure with slight highlight blow, phone-sensor depth of field with harsh bokeh transitions, looks like it came from someone's camera roll and was uploaded to Facebook without editing." For multi-panel compositions, add: "This is panel [N] of a [X]-panel composition. Maintain visual cohesion with the other panels — similar warmth, similar exposure level, similar grain texture — but each panel should look like a different photo from the same person's phone, not four frames from one photoshoot."
- **aspect_ratio** — 1:1 for single square images (most common for Facebook feed), 4:5 for single vertical images. For multi-panel compositions, generate each panel at 1:1 and assemble afterward.
- **prompt_id** — filename format `[campaign_slug]_[descriptor]`, lowercase with underscores, no spaces (e.g. `motilli_bathroom_scale`, `lunessa_pharmacist_counter`).

## Multi-Panel Compositions

Generate each panel as a separate prompt; panels get assembled after generation. Each panel plays one of four narrative roles (not rigid categories — lenses for deciding what each panel contributes):

- **The Anchor** — what identifies the narrator or their world (their profession's setting, their home, their daily environment)?
- **The Villain** — what is the reader currently using/doing that the copy positions as the problem (the medication bottle, the waiting room, the pill organizer)?
- **The Human** — who is involved (the narrator, their spouse, family, the person affected)?
- **The Evidence** — what happened, or what the reader fears (a hospital bed, clinical images, a consequence shot, a grave)?

Not every composition needs all four. A three-panel image might use Anchor + Villain + Evidence; a two-panel might use Villain + Consequence. The copy determines which roles matter.

Visual cohesion rules across panels: similar warmth/color temperature (all slightly warm domestic, or all cool clinical — never mixed); similar grain/noise level (all look like the same phone); similar level of "imperfection" (don't mix a perfectly composed panel with a drastically off-center one); different content but the same quality feel, like four photos from one person's camera roll.

## Image Variant Generation Framework

The best native-image operators don't build ONE image per piece of copy — they build image categories and test systematically, running anywhere from a handful to 50-100+ image variants against a single piece of copy. The copy stays the same; the images rotate; the algorithm finds the winning pairing.

Why: the image's job is to stop the scroll; the copy's job is to convert. These are separate functions. A good artery-cross-section image can pair with multiple cholesterol-angle copy pieces because it creates the same "medical context" scroll-stop regardless of which specific narrator is telling the story.

For any piece of copy, generate images from at least three of the seven congruence categories. Not every copy piece supports all seven, but every copy piece supports at least three.

| Category | Question it answers | Works best when |
|----------|---------------------|------------------|
| Story Prop | "Is this a real person sharing something?" | Hook opens with a concrete, photographable object |
| Narrator Identity | "Can I trust who's talking?" | Copy depends on authority/credentials |
| Mechanism Visualization | "What's actually happening to me?" | Copy involves a mechanism reframe |
| Narrator's World | "Is this person like me?" | Hook is about life context, not a specific object |
| Scene Recreation | "What happened?" | Copy opens on a dramatic moment or crisis |
| Symptom/Condition | "That's what I see on my own body" | Avatar has visible, recognizable symptoms |
| Multi-Panel Collage | "Tell me the whole story at once" | Copy has multiple settings, characters, or narrative beats |

The winner isn't predictable — generate the variants and let the data decide.

## Rules & Standards

### The Before-State Boundary — absolute, no exceptions

- No product in any field, ever — not implied, not in props, not in the background.
- No text or typography in any image. No labels (except a medication label that IS the villain product). No overlays, no captions.
- No hint of resolution, improvement, or hope — every image lives in the before-state.
- No model features. If the subject could appear on a stock photo site, rewrite it.
- No studio lighting — if the light source doesn't exist in a real house, car, bathroom, or hospital, delete it.
- No clean environments — every setting needs at least one detail that signals "lived in."
- No product-category imagery. Whatever form the product takes (gummy, capsule, powder, cream, device), that form is invisible in every image.
- No branded elements — no logos, no packaging, no company names, except the villain brand (e.g. a prescription label for the medication the avatar currently takes).
- **No illustrated, drawn, or diagrammatic imagery, ever.** No napkin sketches, whiteboard drawings, infographics, flowcharts, or hand-drawn visual metaphors — nobody in a Facebook group posts napkin sketches. Real medical photography is the exception and one of the strongest scroll-stops: X-rays, endoscopy, arterial cross-sections, imaging scans, microscopy, lab-result printouts. Test: does it look like it came from a medical facility or a doctor's screen (allowed), or does it look like someone created it to explain something (banned)?
- **No visible phone or device screens, ever.** No one scrolling, reading, texting, or viewing content on a phone, tablet, smartwatch, or fitness tracker. AI cannot render screen content convincingly, and "person looking at phone" is generic — it doesn't show the specific problem the avatar lives with. Phones/watches may appear face-down or off, but never with a lit, content-showing screen.
- **No blurred, obscured, or pixelated faces.** A face must be naturally visible or naturally out of frame (turned away, cropped out, shot from behind, looking down) — never digitally blurred or smudged, which reads as surveillance footage rather than a real post. If the face shouldn't be the focus, recompose the shot instead of obscuring it.
- **No concepts that require decoding, calculating, or interpreting.** Every image must land within about 200 milliseconds. A pill bottle on a counter works instantly; a calculator showing "55-53" to imply an age gap requires cognitive work and fails. Literal beats clever every time.

### Audit checklist — run before showing any image

- Does it start from the avatar's specific problem, not something merely decorative or atmospheric?
- Does it live entirely in the before-state?
- Does it match the awareness level of the copy beat it's paired with (no supplement graveyard in a pure problem-aware beat; no empty-of-failed-attempts image in a solution-aware beat)?
- Is it congruent with its category (Story Prop features a prop from the first 1-3 lines; Mechanism Visualization is real clinical photography; Narrator Identity shows visible credentials)?
- Would the image and the copy's opening feel like the same Facebook post — same emotional temperature?
- Does the image work as a standalone hook — cover the copy and check whether it still stops a scroll and creates identification, visceral curiosity, or narrative entry on its own?
- Would it look native in a Facebook group where the avatar spends time?
- Does the subject look like someone the reader knows, not someone from a catalog?
- Is the setting hyper-specific to this narrator's life, not generic?
- Is lighting exclusively real-world sourced?
- Does image_type specify phone-camera imperfections?
- Is there zero product, zero brand (other than the villain), zero text, zero resolution?
- Is there zero illustrated/drawn imagery (real medical photography is the only exception)?
- Are all phone/device screens invisible (face-down/off only)?
- Are all faces naturally composed, never digitally obscured?
- Does the image communicate in under 200ms with no decoding required?
- Is every visible body part explicitly stated in the subject field?
- Is every detail in the prompt traceable to the avatar's specific world?
- Does the full set span at least three congruence categories, so the testing surface isn't too narrow?

## Display Format

Show every prompt as the exact JSON that will be sent for generation — no translation layer between what gets reviewed and what fires.

Single image:
```json
{
  "image_label": "A",
  "description": "[what this image captures]",
  "role": "[narrative function]",
  "identification_trigger": "[what the reader recognizes as their own life]",
  "prompt": {
    "prompt_id": "brandslug_descriptor",
    "subject": "...",
    "setting": "...",
    "lighting": "...",
    "props": "...",
    "emotion": "...",
    "image_type": "...",
    "aspect_ratio": "..."
  }
}
```

Multi-panel composition:
```json
{
  "composition": "[name]",
  "panel_count": 4,
  "panel_layout": "[describe arrangement]",
  "panels": [
    {
      "panel_number": 1,
      "role": "anchor",
      "prompt": {
        "prompt_id": "brandslug_panel1_descriptor",
        "subject": "...",
        "setting": "...",
        "lighting": "...",
        "props": "...",
        "emotion": "...",
        "image_type": "...",
        "aspect_ratio": "..."
      }
    }
  ]
}
```

The `prompt` object inside each image/panel is exactly what should be sent to the image-generation tool. What gets shown for review is what gets sent.

## Cross-Tests

When setting up cross-tests to isolate which variable is driving performance:

- **Round 1 — test image impact:** pair Image A with Hook B, Image B with Hook C, Image C with Hook A.
- **Round 2 — test headline impact:** pair Image A + Hook A with Headline C, and so on.

Naming convention: `[slug]_[image]_x_[hook]` — e.g. `brandslug_pharmacist_x_hookB`.

## The Standard

Every image must be capable of making one specific person feel like someone broke into their camera roll and put a photo of their actual life in their feed. Every prop, every lighting choice, every framing imperfection exists to pass the reader's subconscious authenticity filter — the filter that decides in 200 milliseconds whether this is a person sharing their experience or a brand trying to sell something. If any field doesn't serve that filter, rewrite it until it does.
